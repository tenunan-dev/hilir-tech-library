ABSTRACT - Hydrolyzed polyacrylamide (HPAM) polymer flooding is a practical chemical enhanced oil 
recovery method, but polymer performance can deteriorate under high salinity, elevated temperature, and high 
shear rate. This study presents an integrated laboratory screening workflow for five commercial HPAM polymers 
under reservoir-representative conditions. Polymer solutions were prepared at 500–5000 ppm and tested at shear 
rates of 1 –1000 s⁻¹, salinities of 3,000 –200,000 ppm, and temperatures up to 90°C. Rheological screening was 
followed by adsorption and core flooding tests for the most suitable candidates, Polymers C and E. The se two 
polymers were carried forward because they achieved the highest composite scores in the technical screening, 
combining strong viscosity retention with favorable injectivity and salinity/temperature tolerance. All polymers 
showed shear-thinning behavior, while salinity and temperature reduced apparent viscosity. Polymer A provided 
the highest bulk viscosity; however, Polymer C gave the best balance among viscosity retention, salinity tolerance, 
injectivity, adsorption behavior, and recovery performance. Core flooding confirmed that Polymer C at 3000 ppm 
produced the highest incremental recovery, approximately 10.0% in Core 1 and 8.8% in Core 2. The results show 
that HPAM selection should be based on integrated rheology, adsorption, and recovery testing  rather than 
viscosity alone. The main contribution of this work to polymer technology is a transferable, six -stage integrated 
screening protocol that links rheological behavior, salinity and thermal tolerance, adsorption loss, and core -scale 
recovery response within a single laboratory workflow, providing a practical decision basis for selecting HPAM 
polymers for high-salinity EOR projects rather than relying on isolated viscosity or adsorption data. 
Keywords: HPAM, polymer flooding, rheology, high salinity, core flooding.

1. Introduction 
Enhanced oil recovery (EOR) is commonly applied after primary and secondary recovery to mobilize oil that 
remains in mature reservoirs. Polymer flooding improves waterflood performance by increasing the viscosity of 
the injected aqueous phase and reducing the water-oil mobility contrast. Better mobility control can delay water 
breakthrough, improve sweep efficiency, and increase recovery from waterflooded reservoirs (Green & Willhite 
1998; Sorbie 1991; Lake et al., 2014; Gbadamosi et al., 2022; Ambaliya & Bera 2023; Seright & Wang 2023). 
HPAM remains one of the most widely used EOR polymers because it is commercially available, relatively 
inexpensive, and capable of producing high solution viscosity at practical concentrations (Seright & Wang 2023). 
Its performance, however, is sensitive to salinity, temperature, shear rate, polymer -rock interaction, and injected 
concentration. Monovalent and divalent ions can shield charged polymer groups, compress polymer coils, and 
reduce apparent viscosity, whe reas temperature and high shear may accelerate viscosity loss or mechanical 
degradation (Zhang et al., 2022; Navaie et al., 2022; Qi et al., 2023; Perez et al., 2024; Khlaifat et al., 2024). 
Recent laboratory studies also show that polymer selection cannot rely on bulk viscosity alone. Static 
adsorption, retention, in -situ rheology, and injectivity can change the effective polymer concentration reaching 
the displacement front and may control the final recovery response. High-salinity and high-temperature screening 
studies have therefore combined rheology with adsorption and core displacement measurements to identify 
candidates that remain stable in brine while maintaining acceptable porous -media flow behavior (Mousapour et 
al., 2021; Mousapour et al., 2024 ; Alajmi et al., 2025 ; Yarahmadi et al., 2025) . For example, Mousapour et al. 
(2021) reported that polymer ranking based on rheology alone did not match the ranking obtained once static 
adsorption was included, while Mousapour et al. (2024)  showed that in -situ injectivity in sandstone could fall 
well below bulk -viscosity predictions once retention effects were accounted for. Alajmi et al. (2025)  further 
demonstrated that fracture presence altered adsorption and in -situ rheology relative to intact core plugs, and 
Yarahmadi et al. (2025)  found that synthetic HPAM generally outperformed bio -based polymers in adsorption 
resistance despite comparable initial viscosities. Together, these studies indicate that rheology, adsorption, and 
displacement behavior do not always rank polymers consiste ntly, motivating the integrated multi -criteria 
screening adopted in the present work. Modified or hybrid polymer systems have also been examined to 
improve salinity tolerance and recovery efficiency under harsher reservoir conditions (Karimov et al., 2025 ; 
Khamis et al., 2026) . 
Nanoparticle-assisted chemical systems are being developed for the same purpose  Subagio et al. (2026), for 
example, stabilised silica nanofluids with an AOS –DLS surfactant pair in formation brine, maintaining 
dispersion stability for more than three months at 60 °C and obtaining 12.9% OOIP incremental recovery in 
sandstone core flooding, which underlines that thermal and salinity stability of the injected chemical is a 
prerequisite for recovery performance.  Field-scale experience supports the practical relevance of this laboratory 
screening approach. In the Daqing oilfield, the world's largest commercial polymer flood, HPAM injection has 
sustained an average incremental oil recovery of about 11% over waterflo oding across the tested blocks, with 
performance strongly dependent on the polymer grade and injection strategy selected during preliminary screening 
(Wang & He 2006) . Similarly, laboratory-guided polymer selection under high -temperature, high -salinity 
conditions in the Bohai heavy -oil field improved water -cut reduction and increased per -well production after 
the target polymer was identified through property screening prior to fie ld deployment  (Zhang et al., 2022) . 
These field cases illustrate that the choice of HPAM grade, informed by rheological, adsorption, and 
displacement screening rather than viscosity alone, directly affects commercial recovery outcomes, reinforcing 
the motivation for the integrated screening workflow presented  here.  
Although previous studies have reported HPAM viscosity, adsorption, or injectivity behavior separately, fewer 
screening studies connect these indicators with recovery response in one compact experimental workflow. The 
present work therefore ranks five comm ercial HPAM candidates using the same sequence of rheology, salinity 
and temperature tolerance, adsorption, and core-flood recovery tests. This integrated comparison reduces the risk 
of selecting a polymer only because it gives high bulk viscosity under simple laboratory conditions. The objective 
of this work is to evaluate five HPAM polymers through an integrated experimental workflow and identify the

oil recovery. Despite the availability of commercial HPAM products, systematic comparative screening that links 
rheology, adsorption, and displacement performance under high-salinity conditions in a single compact workflow 
remains limited in the published literature. This study addresses this gap by presenting an integrated experimental 
screening protocol for five commercial HPAM polymers under  reservoir-representative conditions. The specific 
objectives are to: (i) characterize the rheological response of each polymer under varying shear rate, salinity, and 
temperature; (ii) evaluate polymer adsorption in porous media; and (iii) quantify incremental oil recovery through 
core flooding. The results provide a ranked comparison of candidate polymers and a transferable six -stage 
screening procedure applicable to high-salinity EOR projects. 
 
2. Methodology 
2.1 Polymer materials and solution preparation 
Five commercial HPAM polymers, denoted A, B, C, D, and E, were evaluated. Their molecular weights were 
10–12, 9–11, 5–7, 4–6, and 3–5 MDa (million g/mol) for Polymers A, B, C, D, and E, respectively, with degrees 
of hydrolysis in the range of 25% to 35%. P olymer E was supplied as a liquid polymer, whereas the remaining 
polymers were supplied in powder form. Polymer solutions were prepared at concentrations of 500, 1000, 2000, 
3000, 4000, and 5000 ppm. Powder polymers were slowly added to brine or deionized water under controlled 
vortex mixing at 300 RPM to minimize fish -eye formation and incomplete hydration. The solutions were then 
mixed under moderate agitation and aged for 24 h at 25 °C before rheological testing. The experimental workflow 
is summarized i n Figure 1  as shown below. The six -stage sequence includes polymer selection, solution 
preparation and aging, rheological screening (shear rate, salinity, temperature), candidate ranking by technical 
screening, adsorption and core flooding tests, and final polymer selection based on combined viscosity retention, 
injectivity, adsorption loss, and recovery response.  Each stage of the workflow served a specific screening 
function and generated the input required for the next stage. In Stage 1 (polymer selection), the five candidates 
were selected to span a practical range of molecular weight and hydrolysis degree avail able commercially. In 
Stage 2 (solution preparation and aging), controlled hydration under vortex mixing minimized fish-eye formation 
and ensured reproduci ble baseline viscosity before any stress condition was applied. In Stage 3 (rheological 
screening), each polymer was tested sequentially across shear rate, salinity, and temperature to isolate the 
sensitivity of apparent viscosity to each variable individually before combined conditions were imposed. In Stage 
4 (technical ranking), the rheological outputs were normalized and scored against four criteria (viscosity retention, 
salinity tolerance, thermal stability, and injectivity; Table 2, Figure 5) to narrow the five candidates to the two 
most promising ones, avoiding the cost and time of running full adsorption and core -flood programs on all five 
polymers. In Stage 5 (adsorption and core flooding), the two shortlisted polymers, C and E, were evaluated under 
reservoir-representative salinity and temperature in duplicate core plugs to obtain adsorption and incremental 
recovery data. In Stage 6 (final selection), the outputs of Stages 3 -5 were combined to identify the polymer 
offering the best practical balance of performance criteria, rather than the single criterion of highest bulk viscosity. 
This staged design allowed early -stage rheological results to guide which polymers advanced to the more 
resource-intensive adsorption and core-flooding stages. 
2.2 Rheological measurements 
Rheological measurements were performed using a Brookfield DV -II+ Pro rotational viscometer equipped 
with an OFITE stainless-steel measurement cup. Apparent viscosity was evaluated over shear rates from 1 to 1000 
s⁻¹. The experimental program focused on po lymer concentration, salinity, and reservoir -relevant temperature 
effects up to 90°C. Salinity levels of 3,000, 33,000, 93,000, and 200,000 ppm total dissolved solids (TDS) were 
used to represent low - to high-salinity brine environments. The 93.000 ppm bri ne, representative of the target 
reservoir formation water, was prepared with NaCl (73,000 ppm), CaCl₂ (12,000 ppm), and MgCl₂ (8,000 ppm). 
All rheological comparisons were made after polymer hydration and using the same concentration, salinity, and 
temperature sequence to support direct ranking among the tested polymers. Apparent -viscosity readings were 
recorded after temperature equilibration and stabilization of the instrument response at each condition.

2.3 Oil characterization and core flooding 
Crude oil was collected from the target field and dewatered before testing. The measured dead -oil viscosity 
was 79 cP and the live-oil viscosity was 8 cP, with an API gravity of approximately 30. Cyclohexane was added 
at approximately 25% v/v to match the target live -oil viscosity of 8 cP. Two sandstone core samples from the 
target field were used for adsorption and recovery experiments. The core flooding procedure included core 
cleaning, drying, brine permeability measurement, tracer injection, polymer injection at a constant injection rate 
of 1 cm³/min, and post-polymer brine injection until pressure stabilization. Additional oil recovery was evaluated 
by comparing  oil production after polymer flooding with the preceding waterflood recovery. Before polymer 
injection, each core was prepared and waterflooded to establish a reference recovery. Polymer flooding was then 
applied after waterflooding so that the reported a dditional recovery reflected incremental performance under 
comparable porous-media conditions.

2.4 Adsorption measurements 
Adsorption was evaluated by comparing polymer breakthrough with tracer breakthrough under the same core 
conditions. The difference between injected and produced polymer concentration was used to estimate the 
adsorbed polymer mass. Adsorption was reported a s milligrams of polymer per gram of rock. The candidate 
polymers, C and E, were tested at 1800 and 3000 ppm under high-salinity reservoir conditions at a temperature of 
60 °C, representing the target reservoir's operating flooding temperature, whereas the bulk rheological screening 
was extended to 90 °C to establish the polymers' thermal -tolerance envelope beyond the actual field condition. 
Each measurement was performed in duplicate and the reported values represent the mean; the deviation between

3. Result and discussion 
3.1 Effect of shear rate and concentration 
All tested polymers showed shear -thinning non -Newtonian behavior. Viscosity increased with polymer 
concentration because higher polymer loading increases molecular entanglement and solution network strength. 
However, viscosity decreased as shear rate incre ased because polymer chains progressively aligned in the flow 
direction. Figure 2 below presents the viscosity–shear rate comparison at 5000 ppm polymer concentration, 30°C, 
and 3000 ppm salinity. Polymers A and C showed the strongest thickening ability at low shear rate, whereas 
Polymers B and D had lower apparent viscosity but better  expected injectivity. Polymer E showed the lowest 
viscosity response, but it maintained stable flow behavior and therefore remained attractive for pressure -
constrained applications. Quantitatively, apparent viscosity at 5000 ppm and 1 s⁻¹ ranged from approximately 210 
cP for Polymer A to 8 cP for Polymer E, a more than 25 -fold spread that highlights the practical significance of 
polymer selection at the screening stage. Across the tested shear -rate range, all polymers displayed the shear -
thinning index characteristic of entangled HPAM solutions, with the steepest viscosity decline occurring between 
1 and 100 s⁻¹ and a comparatively flat response above 100 s⁻¹; this behavior is consistent with progressive chain 
disentanglement and alignment rather than mechanical scission, since viscosity did not show the irreversible step-
change typically associated with chain breakage. From an operational standpoint, the narrower viscosity gap 
between polymers at high shear rate implies that near-wellbore injectivity differences among the candidates should 
be smaller than the bulk-viscosity contrast alone would suggest, reinforcing the need for the injectivity-weighted 
screening applied in Section 3.4. At the lower concentrations used in the downstream adsorption and core -flood 
tests, the bulk apparent viscosity at 10 s⁻¹ was approximately 35 cP for Polymer C and 25 cP for Polymer E at 
3000 ppm, and approximately 5 cP for Polymer C and 2 cP for Polymer E at 1800 ppm, consistent with the shear-
thinning and concentration-dependent behavior characterized in Figure 2.

screening implication: viscosity measurements at low salinity alone would have over -stated the practical differences 
among candidates under the target reservoir's 93,000 ppm formation-water salinity. 
2.6 Effect of temperature and combined reservoir conditions 
Temperature reduced the apparent viscosity of all tested polymer solutions. The reduction became more 
pronounced as temperature increased from ambient conditions to 90°C, where thermal motion weakened polymer -
chain interactions and reduced the viscosity re tained under reservoir -representative conditions. Figure 4 shows the 
viscosity response over the tested temperature range of 30 –90°C. Figure 4 shows that Polymers A and C maintained 
the highest apparent viscosity over the investigated temperature range, whereas Polymer E remained the most 
injectable low-viscosity candidate. The temperature-induced viscosity loss reached up to 55% between 30 and 90°C, 
somewhat smaller than the salinity-induced loss, suggesting that under the tested reservoir conditions ionic shielding 
was the dominant degradation mechanism relative to thermal chain disruption alone. Because the 90°C tests were 
conducted at the 93,000 ppm reservoir salinity, the combined effect of temperature and salinity was cumulative rather 
than independent: polymers that already retained less viscosity under high salinity (Polymers B, D, and E) showed a 
comparatively smaller additional percentag e loss from temperature, since much of their salinity -sensitive viscosity 
had already been lost, whereas Polymers A and C, which retained more viscosity at high salinity, showed a larger 
absolute (though not necessarily larger relative) drop when temperature was subsequently increased. This combined-
condition behavior is the practical basis for testing salinity and temperature together rather than assuming their effects 
are simply additive.

2.10 Practical interpretation 
The experimental results confirm that the best polymer is not necessarily the polymer with the highest bulk 
viscosity. Field applicability requires a balance between viscosity generation, injectivity, salinity tolerance, 
temperature resistance, adsorption loss, and incremental recovery. Polymer C was the best overall candidate because 
it combined strong rheological performance with favorable adsorption behavior and higher incremental recovery. 
Polymer E may still be useful in reservoirs with strict pressure limitations because of i ts higher injectivity and stable 
flow behavior; however, its lower viscosity reduced its recovery performance relative to Polymer C. Notably, Polymer 
C showed higher adsorption (0.42 mg/g rock in Core 1 versus 0.28 mg/g for Polymer E), yet it still achieve d better 
incremental recovery, indicating that its viscosity advantage outweighed its higher adsorption loss under the tested 
conditions. These findings support the use of integrated rheology, adsorption, and core flooding evaluation before 
field-scale polymer selection. 
The present results add an important practical screening implication: the highest bulk viscosity did not produce 
the best overall candidate. Polymer C gave stronger recovery than Polymer E and better overall suitability than 
Polymer A because it combined s ufficient viscosity retention with acceptable injectivity, adsorption behavior, and 
displacement response.

The study should be interpreted as a laboratory screening workflow. Before field implementation, the selected

4. Conclusion 
All tested HPAM polymers exhibited shear -thinning non-Newtonian behavior. Apparent viscosity at 5000 ppm 
ranged from approximately 8 cP (Polymer E) to 210 cP (Polymer A) at 1 s⁻¹, and decreased by up to 70% as salinity 
increased from 3,000 to 200,000 ppm and by up to 55% as temperature increased from 30 to 90 °C. These magnitudes 
confirm the necessity of screening under reservoir-representative conditions rather than relying on ambient-condition 
viscosity data alone. Polymer A showed the highest bulk viscosity, but Polymer C provided the best practical balance 
among viscosity retention, salinity tolerance, injectivity, adsorption behavior, and recovery performance. Polymer E 
showed lower viscosity but stable flow behavior, making it a possible option for pre ssure-constrained applications. 
Core flooding confirmed that Polymer C at 3000 ppm produced the highest additional recovery, approximately 10.0% 
in Core 1 and 8.8% in Core 2. HPAM polymer selection for high -salinity EOR reservoirs should therefore be based  
on integrated rheology, adsorption, and displacement testing rather than bulk viscosity alone. The six-stage screening 
workflow demonstrated here provides a reproducible laboratory basis for ranking polymer candidates prior to field -
scale evaluation or pilot design.

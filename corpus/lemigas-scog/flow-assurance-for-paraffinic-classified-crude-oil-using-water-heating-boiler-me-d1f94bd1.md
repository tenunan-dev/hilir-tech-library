1. Introduction 
Field development and the drilling of new wells in Indonesia continue to be carried out with the aim of  
maintaining and increasing oil and gas production. Transporting crude oil from the well to the storage tank requires 
special handling. The transportation of crude oil through pipelines has the potential for several issues, one of 
which is wax deposition. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of hot water flushing as a method for 
mitigating wax deposition in the pipeline segment. 
Flow Assurance is a term that refers to the process of maintaining the flow of fluids (water, gas, oil) from the 
well to the pipeline delivery point using chemical treatments and engineering approaches. The basis of flow 
assurance lies in fluid chemistry (composition, P, and T) and fluid hydraulics (ratio, production rate, topography). 
Flow assurance is essential for determining the optimum flowline size based on the results of well reservoir 
tests under working pressure and flow rate. 
If the pipe size is increased, increased heat loss due to greater surface area, which could lead to a temperature 
drop. If the pipe size is too small, pressure and temperature will be very high. This will result in a larger required 
pipe wall thickness and is expected to cause significant thermal expansion. 
Based on the background described above, the research problem can be formulated as follows. Oil production 
that carries a higher level of risk, difficulty, and cost is a fitting example when discussing flow assurance. Wax 
deposition is one of the major challenges in oil and gas transportation systems, pa rticularly in pipelines 
transporting waxy crude oil under low -temperature conditions, as it can significantly affect production 
performance and reduce the overall economic viability of the system (Carnahan 1989; Aiyejina et al., 2011). 
Flow Assurance covers the entire source, process, facilities, and conditions that affect the successful flow of 
hydrocarbons from the reservoir to the processing or sales point, where sub-surface and surface aspects together 
determine the overall flow assurance design. 
In general, the objective of  this research is to develop a paraffinic-paraffinic oil analyze an existing one to 
optimize mitigation flowline system by utilizing crude assay analysis report data, fluid properties, reservoir 
characteristics, and well data. This model is expected to be able to estimat e the maximum value of the flowline 
system method from the well to the production facility up to the delivery point. 
The effectiveness of hot water is strongly influenced by operational parameters, including injection 
temperature, flushing duration, and flow characteristics. Therefore, an evaluation based on  actual field data is 
essential to assess the effectiveness of this method in mitigating wax deposition and improving flow performance 
in pipeline systems (Bell et al., 2021). 
Flow assurance research ensures  sustainable and stable production, avoiding downtime caused by flow 
problems. Understanding the risks associated with fluid flow allows companies to take appropriate preventive 
action and better manage operational risk (Kiyingi et al., 2022). The specific objectives of this research include: 
• Identifying the temperature drop that can result in wax deposition (Wax Appearance Temperature). 
• Analyzing the performance of the production piping system to identify, at an early stage, the initial location 
where paraffinic wax deposition occurs. 
• Identifying risks associated with fluid flow to enable the company to take appropriate preventive action and 
manage risk.

Crude oil is a mixture of hydrocarbons (consisting of combinations of hydrogen and carbon atoms) that forms 
and occurs naturally within the earth’s crust. In addition to hydrogen and carbon atoms, crude oil also contains 
impurity compounds such as sulfur, oxygen, and nitrogen bonded with hydrogen and carbon  atoms, as well as a 
small amount of non-hydrocarbon compounds such as H₂S, CO₂, N₂, heavy metals, and water. 
The physical properties of hydrocarbon deposits in a reservoir (whether in liquid, gas, or solid form) are 
determined by the composition of the hydrocarbon compounds and the reservoir’s pressure and temperature. If 
the main components  of the hydrocarbon are light hydrocarbon components or compounds with low molecular 
weight such as methane and ethane, the hydrocarbon will be in gas form under atmospheric conditions. If the 
hydrocarbon is dominated by heavy hydrocarbon components, it wi ll be in liquid, semi -solid, or solid form 
under atmospheric conditions. The common term used for liquid hydrocarbon is petroleum or crude oil, while 
hydrocarbon in gas form is called natural gas. Crude oil can be classified as aromatic or paraffinic depen ding 
on which molecules dominate its composition. Crude oil dominated by paraffin molecules will yield very fine 
lubricating oils in its gas -oil fraction and paraffin wax from its residue. Conversely, if crude oil is dominated 
by aromatic molecules, the he avy fraction of the crude oil is used for pitch, roofing compounds, asphalt, and 
other compounds  (Alwazzan et al., 2019).

2. Methodology 
This research is quantitative in nature, using a process-data-based predictive modeling approach carried out 
through the collection and processing of fluid physical property data and production behavior characteristics. 
This subsection explains the overall research design, from the data collection process and model development 
to the model validation process. This research uses a data-based associative and comparative design. 
The research process began with the collection and validation of commissioning data and relevant operational 
data, including pressure, flow rate, temperature, flushing duration, flushing fluid temperature and  flow rate, as 
well as the pipeline configuration and supporting facilities. These data were subsequently analyzed to identify 
changes in system performance and evaluate their compliance with the intended operational targets. Available 
supporting data were then utilized to strengthen the interpretation of the performance  improvement mechanisms, 
such as indications of reduced flow resistance associated with the removal or reduction of wax deposits, without 
developing a new model or conducting a new simulation. Based on this analysis, the study aims to identify the 
key parameters influencing the effectiveness of hot water flushing and to formulate technical recommendations 
to improve the effectiveness, efficiency, and practical applicability of subsequent flushing operations. 
This modeling approach is subsequently integrated into various multiphase flow simulation platforms, such as 
OLGA, to evaluate the effects of wax deposition on flow characteristics in subsea and submarine  pipelines, as 
well as to analyze the sensitivity of key factors influencing the growth rate of the wax layer                                          
(Donepudi & Kamerkar 2024). 
OLGA simulations were employed to evaluate the hydraulic response of the pipeline under existing conditions 
and selected flushing flow -rate scenarios using the total liquid volume flow (QLT), liquid superficial velocity 
(USLT), pressure (PT), fluid temperature, and flow regime as the primary evaluation parameters. This study does 
not include detailed modeling of wax formation mechanisms, calculatio n of wax deposit thickness, or transient

3.1 Data analysis 
This subsection explains the procedures used to process and analyze the data that has been collected, as well as 
the algorithm used to build the predictive model. 
The analysis was conducted using a quantitative and evaluative approach based on a comparison of operating 
conditions before and after the implementation of hot water flushing, as well as the interpretation  of available 
technical data and previously established simulation/calculation results. The analytical procedures consisted of the 
following steps: 
3.1.1 Pipeline system performance evaluation 
The evaluation was conducted by comparing pipeline operating data before and after hot water flushing  
to assess changes in system performance. The primary parameters analyzed included inlet and outlet pressure, 
pressure drop ( ΔP), fluid flow rate, and liquid velocity, which were used as indicators of reduced flow 
resistance resulting from wax deposition (Chala et al., 2018). 
3.1.2 Interpretation of supporting technical data 
Supporting technical data, including previously developed flow assurance simulation results or internal  
calculations, were re-evaluated to understand the effects of increased flow rate and temperature on pressure 
distribution, fluid residence time, and the potential removal of wax deposits. This interpretation was performed 
without developing a new simulation model. 
3.1.3 Identification of critical flushing parameters 
The analysis was conducted to identify the key parameters affecting  the effectiveness of hot water 
flushing, including flushing-water temperature, flushing flow rate, flushing duration, and the initial operating 
conditions of the pipeline. The assessment focused on the relationship between these parameters and changes 
in pipeline performance. 
3.1.4 Correlation of actual operating conditions with planning/simulation data 
Actual field conditions were qualitatively correlated with the assumptions and targets established during 
the planning or simulation stages to assess the degree of consistency between idealized conditions and actual 
operating conditions. This analysis aimed to identify the root causes when the flushing results did not achieve 
the expected targets.

mitigation strategies have been implemented, including the use of inhibitors, pigging, and  thermal treatment

The limited effectiveness of Pour Point Depressant (PPD) in heavy high -waxy crude oil can be attributed to 
several factors, including the high wax content, high base -oil viscosity, and the compatibility  between the 
chemical structure of the PPD and the wax components present in the crude oil. PPD does not remove wax from 
the crude oil; rather, it primarily modifies the morphology and growth  of wax crystals, thereby reducing the 
tendency of the crystals to form a rigid three-dimensional network that can significantly restrict fluid flow. 
In heavy crude oil with a high wax content, the quantity of wax available for crystallization is relatively large. 
Consequently, even when PPD modifies the morphology of the wax crystals, the remaining wax crystals may still 
interact and form a continuous structure within the oil. This condition can limit the improvement in flowability, 
particularly at temperatures approaching or below the Wax Appearance Temperature (WAT). 
Furthermore, the inherently high viscosity of heavy crude oil can remain a significant source of flow resistance. 
The application of PPD may reduce the strength of the wax crystal network, but it does not substantially  reduce 
the intrinsic viscosity of the base oil. Therefore, a reduction in pour point does not necessarily correspond to a 
proportional improvement in pipeline flowability or hydraulic performance. The effectiveness of PPD is also 
strongly dependent on its compatibility with the molecular characteristics of the crude oil and its wax 
composition. The molecular structure, carbon-number distribution, and concentration of paraffinic components 
can influence the interaction between the PPD molecules and the wax crystals. Consequently, a PPD formulation 
that is effective for one type of crude oil may exhibit limited effectiveness when applied to another crude 
oil with different wax characteristics. 
It is also important to distinguish between pour point reduction and wax deposition control. A decrease in pour 
point indicates that the treated crude oil can retain its apparent flowability at a lower temperature under the 
specified test conditions. However, this does not necessarily indicate that wax crystallization  or deposition has 
been eliminated. If the operating temperature remains below the WAT, wax crystals may still precipitate and 
potentially deposit on the inner wall of the pipeline. 
Therefore, for heavy high -waxy crude oil, the effectiveness of PPD should not be evaluated solely based  on 
the reduction in pour point. Other parameters, such as WAT, viscosity, wax content, pressure drop, liquid velocity, 
and the actual extent of wax deposition, should also be considered. In cases where significant wax deposits have 
already accumulated inside the pipeline, thermal treatment methods such as hot water flushing may provide a more 
direct approach because the increased temperature can soften and  mobilize the existing wax deposits, thereby 
helping to restore the effective flow area and hydraulic performance of the pipeline (Yao et al., 2022).

On the other review studies concerning the transportation of waxy crude oil through offshore pipeline systems 
have emphasized that maintaining flow reliability requires an integrated approach encompassing thermal 
management, hydraulic considerations, and operational design. Such an approach includes maintaining 
appropriate operating temperatures, controlling flow rates, and implementing periodic pipeline cleaning strategies 
to minimize excessive wax accumulation and maintain the hydraulic performance of the system (Bell et al., 2021). 
In field operations, one of the pr actical applications of thermal management is high -temperature fluid 
flushing, which can be implemented during both the pre -commissioning stage and normal operation as a 
corrective measure. This method is intended to remove accumulated deposits that may re strict the effective 
flow area and consequently impair pipeline hydraulic performance. The effectiveness of this approach is 
strongly influenced by the flushing -fluid temperature, flow rate, and duration, which must be appropriately 
controlled according to  the operating conditions and characteristics of the deposited material . The accumulation 
of this deposit has the potential to narrow the pipe’s effective diameter, increase pressure dr op, reduce flow 
capacity, and eventually form a wax plug that can impede or even stop the oil transfer process                                      
(Ratnakar et al., 2025).

The effectiveness of PPD is also strongly dependent on its compatibility with the molecular characteristics of 
the crude oil and its wax composition. The molecular structure, carbon-number distribution, and concentration of 
paraffinic components can influence the interaction between the PPD molecules  and the wax crystals. 
Consequently, a PPD formulation that is effective for one type of crude oil may exhibit limited effectiveness when 
applied to another crude oil with different wax characteristics. 
It is also important to distinguish between pour point reduction and wax deposition control. A decrease in pour 
point indicates that the treated crude oil can retain its apparent flowability at a lower temperature under the 
specified test conditions. However, this does not necessarily indicate that wax crystallization  or deposition has 
been eliminated. If the operating temperature remains below the WAT, wax crystals may still precipitate and 
potentially deposit on the inner wall of the pipeline. 
Therefore, for heavy high -waxy crude oil, the effectiveness of PPD should not be  evaluated solely based on 
the reduction in pour point. Other parameters, such as WAT, viscosity, wax content, pressure drop, liquid velocity, 
and the actual extent of wax deposition, should also be considered. In cases where significant wax deposits have 
already accumulated inside the pipeline, thermal treatment methods such as hot water flushing may provide a more 
direct approach because the increased temperature can soften and  mobilize the existing wax deposits, thereby 
helping to restore the effective flow area and hydraulic performance of the pipeline. 
One method that can be applied is hot water injection, which functions to raise the fluid temperature so that wax 
crystal formation can be prevented. By keeping the fluid temperature above this critical threshold, the risk of wax 
deposit formation can be minimized, so that production continuity, oil transportation system efficiency, and 
pipeline operation reliability can be maintained (Jiang et al., 2020). 
Crude oil with a high paraffin content flows inside the pipe. When the fluid temperature drops below the Wax 
Appearance Temperature (WAT), paraffin begins to crystallize and adhere to the pipe wall. As wax crystals 
continue to accumulate (agglomeration): 
• The pipe’s effective diameter narrows. 
• The flow rate decreases. 
• Pressure drop increases. 
• The risk of plugging increases.

The Wax Disappearance Temperature (WDT) is defined as the temperature at which previously formed wax 
crystals or deposits begin to disappear, dissolve, or become sufficiently mobilized as the crude oil is heated. 
WDT is an important parameter in flow assurance because it provides an indication of t he thermal condition 
required to reduce or eliminate wax that has previously precipitated from the crude oil (Brown & Dobbs 1998). 
WDT is closely related to the Wax Appearance Temperature (WAT), but the two parameters represent 
different thermal conditions. WAT refers to the t emperature at which wax crystals initially begin to precipitate 
from the crude oil during cooling, whereas WDT represents the temperature at which the previously formed wax 
phase begins to disappear during heating. Therefore, WDT is particularly relevant t o thermal remediation and 
pipeline cleaning operations involving wax deposits.

The operational parameter profiles are described as follows: 
 
4.1 Geometry profile (geometry [ft]) 
The black line represents the pipeline elevation profile along its total length. The pipeline geometry exhibits a 
continuous positive slope from the inlet at 0 ft to a maximum elevation at approximately 16,500 ft. Beyond this 
point, the elevation decreases slightly toward the pipeline outlet at 18,000 ft. This profile indicates that the pipeline 
predominantly follows an uphill configuration throughout most of its length. 
4.2 Fluid temperature profile (TM [°F]) 
The brown line illustrates the fluid temperature distribution along the pipeline. The inlet temperature is 
approximately 240°F. A significant and rapid temperature decline occurs within the first 2,000 ft, decreasing from 
approximately 240°F to 110°F. Beyond 4,000 ft, the fluid temperature becomes relatively stable at approximately 
105°F until the pipeline outlet. The sharp temperature reduction in the initial section indicates substantial heat loss 
from the fluid to the surrounding environment. 
4.3 Pressure profile (PT [psia]) 
The red line represents the pressure profile along the pipeline. The inlet pressure of approximately 340 psia 
decreases rapidly within the first 500 ft to about 325 psia. Thereafter, the pressure remains relatively constant until 
approximately 15,000 ft. Between 15,000 ft and 16,500 ft, the pressure increases to a peak value of approximately 
350 psia, corresponding to the maximum elevation observed in the pipeline geometry profile. Subsequently, the 
pressure decreases slightly toward the pipeline outlet. 
4.4 Wax Deposition Thickness (DXWX [ft]) 
The blue line shows the thickness of the wax layer deposited on the inner wall of the pipeline. The maximum 
wax thickness occurs near the inlet and decreases exponentially within the first 2,000 ft. After approximately 4,000 
ft, the wax thickness approaches zero and remains nearly constant until the outlet. These  results indicate that 
significant wax deposition is concentrated primarily in the upstream section of the pipeline. 
4.5 Wax Appearance Temperature (°F) 
The magenta line represents the Wax Appearance Temperature (WAT), which corresponds to the temperature 
at which wax crystals begin to form. The WAT remains relatively constant throughout the pipeline at 
approximately 5°F, indicating that this thermodynamic property is essentially unchanged along the pipeline under 
the simulated operating conditions. 
4.6 Liquid Flow Rate (QLST [STB/d]) 
The red line represents the liquid flow rate along the pipeline. The QLST remains relatively stable 
throughout the system, decreasing only slightly from approximately 6,000 STB/d at the inlet to around 5,800 
STB/d at the outlet located at 18,00 0 ft. This gradual decline in liquid flow rate may indicate liquid 
accumulation, phase redistribution, or minor fluid -property changes occurring along the pipeline under steady -
state operating conditions.  
4.7 Recommendation 
Alternative development scenarios ma y be investigated by evaluating the transfer of crude oil mixed with 
water to assess its influence on flow assurance performance and wax deposition characteristic.  Additional 
development scenarios may be explored by blending the crude oil with pour point d epressants (PPDs) or other 
flow assurance chemicals to evaluate their effectiveness in mitigating wax deposition and improving pipeline 
operability. At the field scale, further studies may investigate blending the produced crude oil with lighter crude 
oil from nearby fields, where operationally feasible, to facilitate lifting operations and improve the flow 
characteristics of the transported crude.

ABSTRACT - The quality of biodiesel pro duced from waste cooking oil (WCO) is confirmed by compliance 
with the ASTM D6751 standard specification to ensure the products meet commercial fuel standards. Compared 
with batch reactors, microreactors offer several benefits, including reduced residence time, lower specific energy 
consumption (SEC), and improved processing of low -quality WCO feedstock with high viscosity and free fatty 
acid (FFA) content. However, a knowledge gap remains regarding the capacity of reactor systems to handle highly 
degraded WCO feedstock while complying with ASTM D6751, especially total glycerin limits (≤ 0.240 wt%) and 
minimum ester content. In addition, comparative data between batch reactors and microchannel reactors for low-
quality oil processing remain limited. This rese arch aims to establish an evaluation framework for assessing 
reactor tolerance to highly degraded WCO feedstock by comparing the performance of batch reactors and 
microchannel reactors under slug flow conditions. Results indicate that the microchannel reac tor, with an 80 -
second residence time, achieved a peak FAME conversion of 98.2% under optimal conditions (1.0 wt% KOH, 
HH oil) and consistently maintained high conversion (91.1%-98.2%) across all tested feedstocks. Conversely, the 
batch reactor, with a one-hour reaction time, achieved a conversion of only 68.2%- 88.4% and could not reach the 
high FAME content required to exceed the total glycerin limit based on ASTM D6751 (> 1.0% berat). The SEC 
of the microchannel reactor was also 98.96% lower than that of the batch reactor. Consequently, the microchannel 
reactor demonstrated superior efficiency compared to the batch reactor in transforming lower -quality WCO into 
biodiesel that meets the ASTM D6751 standard in terms of FAME conversion, energy efficiency, and processing 
time. Future research may explore the design and optimisation of microchannel reactors for industrial -scale 
applications, as well as test a wider range of WCO quality variations to establish a more stable and sustainable 
biodiesel production system.  
Keywords: Biodiesel purification, ASTM D6751, waste cooking oil microchannel reactor, specific energy 
consumption.

1. Introduction 
Growing energy demand and fossil fuel depletion have driven research toward renewable, environmentally 
friendly options, especially biodiesel as an alternative to petroleum diesel (Ma & Hanna 1999) . As a potential 
substitute, biodiesel can replace fossil fuels because it is similar to conventional diesel and offers significant 
ecological benefits (Tiwari et al., 2018). The main benefit of biodiesel is its potential to reduce CO2 emissions, 
particulate matter, and greenhouse gas emissions because of its biogenic, renewable carbon content (Kulkarni & 
Dalai 2006) . Therefore, biodiesel contributes only marginally to the greenhouse effect and has low sulphur 
content, a high flash point, low aromatic content, and good biodegradability (Patil & Deng 2009) . Commercial 
biodiesel is traditionally produced from a variety of vegetable oils depending on regional availability, mainly 
palm, soybean, rapeseed, canola and sunflower oil. Biodiesel is produced by transesterification of triglycerides 
with monohydric alcohols to form long -chain monoalkyl esters, which are effective alternative diesel fuels 
(Freedman et al., 1986; Lang et al., 2001) . However, producing biodiesel from pure vegetable oils faces severe 
commercial challenges because of high feedstock costs. Feedstock costs directly influence market price and can 
account for up to 75% of total operating expenditure (Gui et al., 2008; Nelson et al., 1994; Noordam & Withers 
1996). Biodiesel feedstock can be edible or non -edible vegetable oils, animal fats and waste cooking oil (WCO) 
(Mohadesi et al., 2019) . However, disposal of WCO on a large scale directly into water bodies creates serious 
environmental problems and aquatic pollution (Sharma et al., 2008). Many developed countries have responded 
with penalties for improper disposal. Using WCO as a biodiesel feedstock is a two -pronged approach to reduce 
total production costs and decrease dependence on fossil -based diesel (Kulkarni & Dalai 2006;  Okitsu et al., 
2010). However, repeated frying at high temperatures accelerates hydrolysis, oxidation, and thermal 
polymerisation, significantly increasing free fatty acid (FFA) and moisture content in the oil (Choe & Min 2007).  
Commercial food establishments tend to generate higher levels of degradation in waste cooking oil than 
household oil because of longer frying ti mes and larger quantities of moisture released from food products 
(Alsultan et al., 2021; Choe & Min 2007). The most common methods for converting WCO to biodiesel are direct 
blending (dilution), thermal cracking (pyrolysis), microemulsification, and transesterification. Of these methods, 
transesterification is the most popular in commercial and industrial use (Shahid & Jamal 2011) . However, high 
FFA levels (usually >0.5%) lead to undesirable saponification side reactions in base-catalysed transesterification 
with KOH, producing potassium soaps and water (Canakci & Van Gerpen 2001;  Eze et al., 2018;  Özbay et al., 
2008). These side reactions deactivate the active catalyst, increase micellar viscosity, form stable emulsion 
matrices, and greatly complicate downstream separation of biodiesel and glycerol (Atadashi et al., 2013;                     
Jain et al., 2023). 
In conventional stirred batch reactors, biodiesel production takes long processing times (between 2 and 24 
hours) to achieve high conversions, using either acid, base or enzyme catalysts (Noureddini & Zhu 1997; Sun et 
al., 2008). The limited interfacial contact between the nonpolar oil phase and the polar methanol phase leads to 
significant mas s-transfer restrictions (Stamenković et al., 2008) . These mass -transfer limitations worsen 
saponification during the processing of highly degraded WCO, resulting in unreacted glycerides and biodiesel that 
does not meet in ternational quality standards (Atadashi et al., 2013;  Eze et al., 2018) . Alternative methods 
operating under supercritical conditions (e.g.,> 250 °C and >10 MPa) or using co -solvents like tetrahydrofuran 
speed kinetics. However, they require complex, high-energy input (Sun et al., 2008). These operational challenges 
directly affect the economic feasibility and commercial scale -up of batch systems (Pasha et al., 2021;  Tiwari et 
al., 2018) . To overcome these limitations, microchanne l reactor technology has emerged as a process 
intensification tool for liquid –liquid reactions with improved performance (Hessel & Löwe 2005;  Tiwari et al., 
2018). Microchannels are available with a range of dimensions from submicrometre to submillimetre, with ultra-
high surface area-to-volume ratios (>8000 m2/m3), rapid heat and mass transfer rates and short molecular diffusion 
distances, so that the primary transport mechanism changes from macroscopic convection to microscale diffusion 
(Sun et al., 2008;  Wen et al., 2009) . However, there are still important research gaps in terms of the risks of 
product purification and the strict compliance with t, defined here as the heat energy per unit volume of biodiesel 
produced (kWh/L)he  international biodiesel specifications (ASTM D6751) when using degraded feedstocks 
without acid -catalysed pretreatment (Maddikeri et  al., 2012;  Standard 2009) . Although microfluidic

2. Methodology 
Waste cooking oil (WCO) used as the biodiesel feedstock was obtained from two sources, namely household 
WCO and WCO from a fried chicken restaurant. The two oil samples were first characterised for free fatty acid 
(FFA) content, water content, density, and  viscosity. Because the measured FFA values were below 1 –3%, the 
oils could be used directly in the base -catalysed transesterification process without an acid esterification 
pretreatment step with sulfuric acid, which is commonly applied to high -FFA oils t o prevent soap formation. 
Technical grade methanol for analysis (EMSURE, Merck) was used as the alcohol, and the base catalyst was 
potassium hydroxide (KOH, AR grade, Chemiz) in pellet form, dissolved in methanol before being mixed with 
the WCO. The micror eactor consisted of a FEP tube (IDEX Health & Science, type 1548XL) with an effective 
length of 1 m and an inner diameter of 0.508 mm, assembled as a microchannel reactor and placed in a water bath 
at a controlled temperature of 60 °C. Two independent high -precision syringe pumps injected the reactants into 
the microchannel at a total volumetric flow rate of 100 μL/min (37 μL/min for WCO and 63 μL/min for methanol-
KOH solution), giving a residence time of 80 seconds in the microchannel. Positive -displacement syringe pump 
volumetric dosing is strictly constant and unaffected by dynamic viscosity and density variations between HH -
WCO and FCR-WCO feedstocks. For comparison, we also carried out experiments in a batch reactor using a glass 
beaker heated and stirr ed at 60 °C at 600 rpm for 60 min. For both reactor types, we kept the methanol -to-oil 
molar ratio at 12:1. We varied the KOH concentration at 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 wt% relative to the oil to determine the 
optimum conditions for FAME formation. After the reaction, the product mixture was allowed to settle until two 
phases formed. The upper methyl ester phase was then separated, washed with water if necessary, and dried before 
analysis. The FAME content in the biodiesel produced from each experiment was determin ed using gas 
chromatography with a flame ionisation detector (GC FID) following the FAME standard method (EN 14103), in 
which the biodiesel sample was first diluted with n-hexane at a specified ratio and injected into a capillary column 
designed for biodie sel analysis. The areas of the separated FAME peaks were used to calculate the FAME 
percentage in the sample.  
2.1 Materials

2.3 Reactor evaluation and ASTM D6751 compliance 
Evaluating both reactor systems across varying catalyst loadings highlights the inherent limitations of the 
conventional batch setup when processing degraded feedstocks.  
The microchannel reactor consistently achieved elevated FAME conversion rates (91.1% –98.2%) a nd 
significantly reduced total glycerin levels to well below the ASTM D6751 limit (0.240 wt%). The performance 
of the microchannel reactor is dictated by microfluidic interfacial physics; the low Bond number (Bo<1) facilitates 
the formation of highly stable Taylor slug flow, thereby shortening diffusion pathways to the sub-millimetre scale 
(Santacesaria et al., 2012) . Kinetically, the vigorous internal vortices expedite transesterification within 80 
seconds, effectively surpassing slower, detrimental saponification side reactions (Wen et al., 2009). 
Evaluation of both reactor systems at different catalyst loadings indicates the conventional batch system's 
inherent limitations in processing degrad ed feedstock. The synthesised biodiesel was tested for compliance 
with ASTM D6751. The main criteria were total glycerin content below 0.240% and a high FAME 
conversion rate (≥ 90.0%). The microchannel reactor consistently achieved high FAME conversion (91 .1–
98.2%) and low total glycerin (0.016 –0.023% wt), well below the ASTM D6751 limit. Conversely, all batch 
reactor runs failed the compliance benchmark due to incomplete transesterification, resulting in high residual 
total glycerin levels (> 1.0 wt).  
Furthermore, this process intensification significantly reduces the thermal footprint. The microchannel system 
reduced total energy consumption from 0.6600 kWh in the 1-h batch cycle to 0.0133 kWh (98.0% reduction), and 
reduced SEC to 0.219 kWh/L (Talebian-Kiakalaieh et al., 2013) . By reducing processing time by 97.8% 
(Natarajan et al., 2019), microfluidic reactors are an important engineering solution for achieving sustainable, 
clean bioenergy compliance (Madhawan et al., 2018).

3. Result and discussion 
3.1 Initial characteristics of the feedstock 
The feedstock used in this study was WCO, obtained from two main sources with different usage 
characteristics: household waste cooking oil (household, HH) and waste cooking oil from nan FCR. These two 
sources were selected to compare biodiesel feedstock qu ality across different scales of utilisation. Heating 
frequency, use duration, and the types of foods fried can greatly influence the oil's chemical composition and 
degree of degradation. Used cooking oil from domestic sources was collected from several households around the 
research location that regularly use cooking oil for daily frying.  
Usually, the oil has been used several times to fry different kinds of foods, such as tempeh, tofu, chicken, and 
other starchy foods. In contrast, WCO from the fried chicken restaurant was collected from a restaurant that uses 
large amounts of oil for deep -frying. The oil source is often repeatedly heated to high temperatures for longer 
periods than household oil. It thus has the potential for higher levels of oxidation,  polymerisation, and free fatty 
acids. Samples of WCO were collected from each source and were stored in clean plastic containers which were 
tightly sealed to avoid contamination from the surrounding environment. The first preparation stage was rough 
filtration of the oil through a filter cloth to remove suspended solid particles such as food crumbs, flour residues, 
and carbon particles formed during frying.  
The filtration step is important to remove solid particles that might hinder oil characterisation o r affect 
subsequent reaction processes in biodiesel production. The oil samples were filtered and stored in dark 
containers at room temperature. This storage condition prevented further oxidation from light and oxygen 
exposure. Furthermore, the containers were tightly closed to prevent direct contact with air, which can increase 
oil degradation.  
3.2 Determination of density 
The density of waste cooking oil from household (HH) and fried chicken restaurant (FCR) sources was 
determined using a gas pycnometer (AccuPyc II 1340; Micromeritics) at a controlled temperature of about 25 °C, 
with helium as the analysis gas. Before measurement, we cleaned and dried the measuring cell and sample cup to 
avoid contamination. We then calibrated the cell volume according to the manufacturer's procedure. For the FCR 
oil sample, ~3.1943 g of oil was added to the sample cup (cup mass 2.1325 g).  
The pycnometer cell was then purged with helium gas 10 times to remove residual air and to ensure pressure 
stabilisation inside the chamber . After equilibrium conditions were reached at 24.96 °C, the instrument ran ten 
measurement cycles. In each run, the AccuPyc software automatically determined the sample volume and 
calculated the density. The procedure for the HH oil sample was similar. Th e sample mass was 2.3641 g (cup 
mass: 2.1342 g) at 25.01 °C. The instrument calculates the true sample volume using the ideal gas law by 
measuring the pressure difference of helium gas between the reference cell and the sample cell in each cycle.

3.2.2 Interpretation of density differences 
The slightly higher density of FCR oil compared to HH oil may be attributed to differences in triglyceride 
composition, as well as differences in the extent of thermal degradation and oxidation during repeated heating. 
Oils with higher contents of saturated fatty acids or fractions with higher average molecular weights also show 
higher density values. Thus, the higher density in the FCR sample compared with the HH sample can be 
explained by compositional changes caused by repeated heating and exte nded use of frying oil during 
restaurant operations.

3.4 Water content 
Water content of HH and FCR WCO was determined by volumetric Karl Fischer titration using a KF titrator 
type V30 (Mettler Toledo). The method is based on a stoichiometric reaction between water and the Karl Fischer 
reagent (iodine, sulphur dioxide and a base in an alcohol solvent ) and the amount of reagent consumed during 
titration is proportional to the sample water content. An oil sample of ca. 0.25 g was introduced into the titration 
vessel (blank value corrected) and then titrated automatically until the electrochemically dete rmined end point 
was reached. The instrument reported the final water content directly as mass % of the sample. From the titration 
results, the water content of FCR oil was about 0.068%, while that of HH oil was lower at about 0.038%. The 
higher water content of FCR oil contributed to greater water absorption during repeated frying due to contact with 
water-containing foodstuffs and exposure to water vapour from the environment. However, the water content of 
both oils is still considered low for WCO, so the risk of severe hydrolysis can be minimised. Water in the oil can 
react chemically with the KOH base catalyst, producing carbonate (K 2CO3) and promoting saponification 
reactions with FFA, thus reducing transesterification efficiency and methyl ester yield. However, because the FCR 
and HH WCO have low water content (less than 0.1 wt%) and low FFA content, the transesterification process 
can be carried out in a single step with a base catalyst without intensive pretreatment to remove water. 
3.5 Determination of FFA (free fatty acid) 
FFA content was determined by acid-base titration. An analytical balance was used to weigh 0.3–2.0 mL of a

3.6 Result synthesis and implications for the biodiesel process 
Overall characterisation shows that FCR oil has higher density, viscosity, water content, and FFA than HH, 
indicating a higher level of thermal a nd oxidative degradation. This makes FCR a “higher risk” feedstock, with 
a higher tendency for soap formation, higher catalyst consumption, and mass -transfer limitations in base -
catalysed transesterification.  
In contrast, HH oil has lower FFA, water content, and viscosity and thus requires only minimal pre-treatment, 
and its behaviour is similar to that of lightly used vegetable oil. From a process -design point of view, this 
difference provides the basis for comparing batch and microreactor performance und er identical operating 
conditions. The viscous, degraded FCR oil is expected to benefit more from process intensification in a 
microreactor (due to its high surface area -to-volume ratio and short diffusion distances). For HH oil, these 
differences are more likely to be due to hydrodynamic factors and catalyst utilisation.  
In addition, since both WCO samples were pre-treated to FFA ≤ ~1.0-1.5 wt.% and water content ≤ 0.1 wt.%, 
the one-step transesterification with KOH catalyst can be utilised. This allows direct evaluation of the effect of 
initial WCO quality on maximum FAME conversion in the two reactor types.  
3.7 Operational conditions and experimental design 
3.7.1 Experimental design 
 The study used a simple factorial design to assess the effect of feedstock type, reactor type, and base 
catalyst concentration on FAME formation. The study used two WCO feedstocks, HH and FCR, and tested 
each in two reactor configurations: a batch reactor and a microchannel reactor. To systematically examine 
every combination of factors, we used three levels of KOH catalyst concentration (0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 wt% based 
on oil mass). We carried out all experiments at a constant methanol -to-oil molar ratio of 12:1 and a reaction 
temperature of 60 °C, with a low enough FFA content to allow single -step base-catalysed transesterification 
without acid pretreatment. The aims of this design were (i) to establish the effect of KOH concentration on 
FAME formation for each oil and reactor type and (ii) to compare the performance of the batch reactor, 
operated with a reaction time of 60 min, with that of the microchannel reactor, operated at a residence time of 
80 s under the same stoichiometric and temperature conditions. Thus, variations in reaction efficiency are more 
directly linked to reactor characteristics and operating conditions. 
3.7.2 Calculation of methanol and KOH 
 We calculated the stoichiometric methanol and KOH required for each experimental run based on a fixed 
12:1 methanol-to-oil molar ratio and the mass of oil used in each reactor configuration. Table 5 summarises 
the specific reactant formulations and catalyst mass loadings for all conditions tested.  Before feeding, the 
KOH pellets were completely dissolved in the specified volume of methanol to prepare a homogeneous 
potassium methoxide solution in both reactor systems, guaranteeing uniform catalytic dispersion throughout 
the reaction phase.  
3.7.3 FAME GC–FID analysis 
GC-FID was used to evaluate transesterification performance, and the total peak area of all fatty acid methyl 
ester (FAME) components in each chromatogram was calculated. Under each operating condition (type of oil, 
catalyst concentration and type of reactor), 1 µL of the biodiesel phase was injected into a WAX capillary column, 
and the FAME peaks in the C8–C24 range were integrated using the same calibration method. The total peak area 
of all FAME components (ΣArea FAME) was used as a relative measure of FAME content, assuming the FID 
response is linear with concentration over the measurement range. Therefore, this parameter characterises the 
relative degree of FAME formation under different operating conditions, but not the absolute FAME content.

3.8 Comparison between batch and microreactor 
3.8.1 FAME  
In the first characterisation, the HH feedstock had higher initial quality than FCR, with lower FFA (0.17 
wt%), water (0.038 wt%) content, and dynamic viscosity (31.5 mPa·s). HH is therefore intrinsically more 
suitable for base -catalysed transesterification. However, the GC –FID conversion data show that the 
microchannel reactor provides the greatest relative performance improvement for the lower quality FCR 
feedstock. The microchannel system increased FCR oil conversion from 68.2% to 92.4% at a low catalyst 
concentration of 0.5 wt% KOH (1.35x improvement), overcoming the severe mass -transfer limitations of 
conventional batch mixing. 
The batch reac tor achieved a maximum conversion of 88.4% (FCR at 1.5 wt% KOH) after 60 minutes, 
while the microchannel reactor achieved higher conversions of 91.1 to 98.2% in only 80 seconds. The 
maximum conversion of 98.2% with HH feedstock at 1.0 wt% KOH demonstrates the optimum operating 
window for the microchannel system. This shows that process intensification not only significantly reduces 
reaction time but also maximises chemical yield. The microreactor performs better at 1.0 wt.% KOH 
(MR/Batch ≈ 1.40), and both reactors are performing similarly at 1.5 wt.% (MR/Batch ≈ 0.97). This indicates

3.8.2 Differences between batch and microreactor 
The observed performance differences between the batch reactor and the microreactor can be understood 
in terms of hydrodynamic behaviour and mass -transfer phenomena. In the microreactor, the small ch annel 
dimensions favour segmented (slug) flow, where the methanol phase is dispersed in the oil phase, creating a 
large interfacial area (Sun et al., 2008) . In addition, internal recirculation within the slugs improves mixing 
and reduces diffusion-layer thickness, significantly enhancing mass -transfer and reaction rates. By contrast, 
methanol droplets in the batch reactor are larger and more heterogeneously distributed. The interfacial area is 
reduced, and stagnant zones may be pr esent, so that mass transfer becomes a limiting factor. Thus, longer 
reaction times are necessary to reach similar conversion rates. 
This limitation is more relevant for FCR feedstock, which has higher viscosity, FFA and water content. 
These properties inc rease diffusion resistance and the likelihood of soap formation, reducing batch -process 
efficiency. The microreactor overcomes these limitations by intensifying mass transfer. These limitations are 
less important for HH feedstock, which is relatively clean er; therefore, the performance differences between 
the two reactor types are smaller. 
For both HH and FCR, conversion increases are limited at higher catalyst concentrations (1.5 wt.%). This 
may be due to side effects such as saponification, increased visc osity and difficulties in phase separation, for 
example, further catalyst addition does not significantly improve conversion. 
3.8.3 Relation to biodiesel standards 
The observed trends of ΣArea FAME, although a relative parameter, provide a glimpse of the quality of 
the produced biodiesel. Higher ΣArea FAME values mean more complete conversion of triglycerides and less 
residual mono-, di-, and triglycerides.

These results are consistent with the initial characterisation where HH is of better quality tha n FCR. 
However, the microreactor benefit is more pronounced for FCR, showing its ability to enhance conversion for 
more degraded feedstocks. As the microreactor produces more ΣArea FAME, parameters such as total glycerol 
and free glycerol are more likely to meet biodiesel standards such as ASTM D6751 and EN 14214. 
Nevertheless, feedstock quality is critical. Even in a microreactor, oils with high FFA and water content 
still risk soap formation and lower process efficiency. A one-step base-catalysed transesterification is enough 
for HH to produce high -quality biodiesel. In contrast, for FCR, the microreactor is an important strategy to 
compensate for less favourable physical and chemical properties. 
Future work should be directed toward the conversion of ΣArea FAME into absolute FAME content (% 
mass) using internal standards and validated calibration curves. Additionally, studies of residence time 
distribution (RTD) and flow patterns in microchannels are needed better to correlate hydrodynamic behaviour 
with conversion efficiency and side-product formation. 
 
4. Conclusion 
In this study, we conducted a comprehensive comparative evaluation of a conventional stirred batch reactor 
and a capillary microchannel reactor under the Taylor slug flow regime for continuous biodiesel production from 
degraded waste cooking oils (HH-WCO and FCR-WCO). Initial characterization showed that FCR oil was more 
thermally and oxidatively degraded than HH oil. FCR oil had higher dynamic viscosity (40.3 mPa.s vs 31.5 mPa.s 
at 60 °C) and density (0.9198 g/cm3 vs 0.9142 g/cm3 at 15 °C) than HH oil. However, because the measured FFA 
levels were below the critical limit of 1 –3 wt%, we processed both feedstocks directly using a single -step, base-
catalyzed transesterification without acid esterifi cation pre -treatment. The continuous microchannel reactor 
successfully tackled the limitations of severe mass transfer in batch. The microchannel system consistently 
achieved high FAME conversions (91.1 –98.2%) across all catalyst loadings (0.5 –1.5 wt% KOH) at a residence 
time of only 80 seconds, with optimal conversion of 98.2% at 1.0 wt% KOH. In contrast, the batch reactor showed 
only 68.2%–88.4% conversion after a long reaction time of 60 min. 
Furthermore, the microfluidic system met ASTM D6751 fuel quali ty specifications without secondary 
extraction, with residual total glycerin ranging from 0.016 –0.023 wt% (vs. the maximum limit of 0.240 wt%). 
In contrast, all batch products failed to meet the compliance threshold, with high residual glycerin accumulatio n 
(>1.0 wt%). Crucially, process intensification with microreactor technology delivered great energetic and 
operational improvements. The reaction time dropped by 97.8%, and total thermal energy consumption fell 
from 0.6600 kWh to 0.0133 kWh (98.0% reducti on), achieving a low Specific Energy Consumption (SEC) of 
0.219 kWh/L. These findings show that Taylor slug -flow capillary microchannels are an efficient, compact, 
and sustainable engineering platform for upgrading low -grade waste lipid feedstocks to high -quality 
commercial biodiesel.

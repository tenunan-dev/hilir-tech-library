ABSTRACT - Cryogenic air separation units (ASUs) are the most widely applied technology for producing high-
purity oxygen and nitrogen for industrial applications. Conventional double -column ASUs generally employ 
either turboexpander- or Joule–Thomson (JT) valve-based refrigeration systems, whose refrigeration performance 
strongly influences product distribution and overall energy consumption. Since the amount of refrigeration 
generated by a turboexpander depends on the fraction of compressed air directed to the expans ion process, 
optimizing the expanded-air fraction offers a promising approach to improve the energy performance of cryogenic 
ASUs. This study investigates the effect of expanded -air fraction on the performance of a cryogenic ASU and 
compares turboexpander - and Joule –Thomson (JT) valve -based refrigeration systems using Aspen HYSYS 
simulation. The simulation is performed using a feed air flow rate of 12,000 kmol.h⁻¹ at 30°C and atmospheric 
pressure, while the expanded-air fraction in the turboexpander configuration was varied from 20% to 50% of the 
total feed airflow.  Increasing the expanded -air fraction enhanced refrigeration generation and power recovery, 
resulting in lower column thermal duties and specific power consumption. Gaseous nitrogen production increased 
from 2,418 to 5,055 kmol.h⁻¹, while liquid nitrogen p roduction decreased from 7,000 to 4,375 kmol.h⁻¹. 
Temperature profile analysis of the low-pressure column shows that additional refrigeration lowered the operating 
temperature and improved vapor –liquid equilibrium, promoting oxygen enrichment in the liquid phase and 
nitrogen enrichment in the vapor phase. Consequently, oxygen purity increased from 97.4% to 98.0%, while 
nitrogen and oxygen recoveries remained above 99%. The optimum performance was obtained at 50% expanded 
air, achieving the lowest specific power consumption of 354.5 kWh.ton⁻¹ GOX. Compared with the JT valve 
configuration, the turboexpander reduced the specific power consumption by approximately 6.1% owing to 
simultaneous refrigeration generation and mechanical work recovery. These findings demonstrate that optimizing 
the expanded-air fraction provides an effective operational strategy for improving both product flexibility and 
energy efficiency in double-column cryogenic ASUs.

1.  Introduction 
Cryogenic air separation units (ASUs) are essential industrial facilities for producing high-purity oxygen and 
nitrogen required in petroleum refining, petrochemical manufacturing, steel production, chemical industries, and 
nitrogen injection for enhanced oil recovery (EOR). High-purity nitrogen is widely used for inerting, blanketing, 
pressure maintenance, and enhanced oil recovery through nitrogen flooding, whereas oxygen is utilized in 
oxidation, gasification, and oxy -fuel combustion processes. Because cryogenic air separation is one of the most  
energy-intensive gas separation technologies, the refrigeration system accounts for a substantial proportion of the 
overall electricity consumption. Consequently, improving refrigeration efficiency has become an important 
strategy for reducing operating costs, improving process sustainability, and enhancing the energy performance of 
industrial gas production facilities (Klein et al., 2021; Shingan et al., 2023; Young et al., 2021). 
In conventional turboexpander-based cryogenic ASUs, only a portion of the compressed air is diverted to the 
turboexpander to generate refrigeration before entering the distillation columns, whereas the remaining air 
bypasses the expander and is cooled thro ugh the main heat exchanger by exchanging heat with returning cold 
product streams prior to separation. Consequently, the proportion of air directed to the turboexpander, referred to 
as the expanded-air fraction, governs the amount of refrigeration generated and directly affects the thermal balance 
of the cryogenic process (Huo et al., 2023; Klein et al., 2021) . Despite its practical importance, the influence of 
expanded-air fraction on vapor –liquid equilibrium, nitrogen product distribution, and overall separation 
performance has received limited attention in previous studies (Klein et al., 2021). 
Cryogenic distillation remains one of the most energy -intensive gas separation technologies because 
substantial compression and refrigeration duties are required to maintain cryogenic operating conditions. 
Consequently, improving refrigeration efficiency h as become one of the primary strategies for reducing the 
specific power consumption of industrial oxygen and nitrogen production (Chen & Yu 2021; Young et al., 2021). 
Current research has mainly focused on process optimization, energy integration, and the comparison of 
refrigeration devices, particularly turboexpanders and Joule –Thomson (JT) valves, to improve overall ASU 
performance (Cheng et al., 2022; Klein et al., 2021; Piguave et al., 2022). Turboexpanders generate refrigeration 
through near-isentropic expansion while simultaneously recovering mechanical work, whereas JT valves rely on 
isenthalpic expansion without energy recovery, resulting in lower thermodynamic efficiency (Huo et al., 2023). 
Although these studies have demonstrated the energy advantages of turboexpanders, they have primarily 
emphasized energy, exergy, or economic performance. A systematic investigation of how the expanded -air 
fraction influences vapor –liquid equilibrium, nitro gen product distribution between gaseous and liquid phases, 
column thermal duties, and specific power consumption remains scarce. 
Previous studies have mainly focused on improving the energy, exergy, or economic performance of cryogenic 
air separation units through process optimization and refrigeration system modification. However, limited attention 
has been devoted to understanding how the expanded -air fraction affects the internal vapor –liquid equilibrium, 
nitrogen product distribution between gaseous and liquid phases, column thermal duties, and specific power 
consumption in double-column ASUs. Addressing this gap is important because refrigeration availability governs 
both separation efficiency and product flexibility. Therefore, this work systematically investigates expanded -air 
fractions ranging from 20% to 50% in a turboexpander -based ASU and compares its performance with a 
conventional JT valve refrigeration system. The effects on product purity, recovery, nitrogen product distribution, 
temperature profile, thermal duties, net energy consumption, and specific power consumption are comprehensively 
evaluated to provide guidance for energy-efficient operation of industrial oxygen and nitrogen production facilities.

2. Methodology 
2.1 Process description 
Figure 1  illustrates the simulated cryogenic air separation unit (ASU) developed in Aspen HYSYS V15. 
Atmospheric air was filtered, compressed, purified, cooled to cryogenic temperatures, and subsequently separated 
into oxygen and nitrogen products through a double -column distillation system. The major process units are 
described in Figure 1.  
2.2  Bag Filter 
Ambient air was first passed through a bag filter to remove dust, particulate matter, and other suspended 
contaminants. The removal of solid particles is necessary to protect downstream equipment, particularly 
compressors and heat exchangers, from fouling, erosion, and operational inefficiencies. The filtered air was then 
directed to the compression system (Perry & Green 2019). 
2.3 Air Compressor (MAC) 
The filtered air was compressed using a three -stage main air compressor to achieve the pressure required for 
cryogenic separation. Intercooling was applied between compression stages to reduce air temperature and 
minimize compression work. The compressed a ir was subsequently sent to the pre -purification unit for 
contaminant removal (Smith et al., 2021; Young et al., 2021). 
2.4 Pre-Purification Unit (PPU) 
The pre-purification unit removed moisture, carbon dioxide, and trace hydrocarbons from the compressed air 
stream. These impurities must be eliminated prior to cryogenic cooling because they can freeze at low 
temperatures and cause equipment blockage and r educed heat transfer efficiency. Molecular sieves are widely 
employed for moisture removal from compressed gas streams due to their strong adsorption capability, including 
in high -purity oxygen applications (Baqir et al., 2026) . Industrial ASUs commonly employ molecular sieve 
adsorption beds for this purpose (Erdöl et al., 2024; Young et al., 2021). 
2.5 Main Heat Exchanger (MHE) 
The purified air was cooled in the main heat exchanger through indirect heat exchange with cold process 
streams returning from the distillation columns. This heat integration enables efficient refrigeration recovery and 
reduces external cooling requirements. The feed air temperature was reduced to near its liquefaction temperature 
before entering the cryogenic section (Seader et al., 2024; Shingan et al., 2023).

2.6 Refrigeration System 
Two refrigeration configurations were investigated in this study. In Case A, as illustrated in Figure 2a , 
refrigeration was generated by diverting a portion of the compressed air through a turboexpander. The expanded 
air underwent near -isentropic expansion, producing a significant temperature reduction while simultaneously 
recovering mechanical work. The rem aining compressed air bypassed the turboexpander and was subsequently 
cooled in the main heat exchanger through indirect heat exchange with returning cryogenic product streams and 
the expanded air before entering the distillation columns. Therefore, no ext ernal refrigeration system, such as a 
mechanical refrigeration cycle or refrigerant-based cooling, was employed. The expanded-air fraction was varied 
from 20% to 50% of the total feed airflow to evaluate its influence on refrigeration generation and separa tion 
performance. In Case B, as illustrated in Figure 2b, refrigeration was generated using a Joule–Thomson (JT) valve, 
where cooling was produced through isenthalpic expansion without power recovery. The refrigeration system 
provides the low-temperature conditions required for cryogenic distillation (Cheng et al., 2022; Huo et al., 2023). 
2.7 High-Pressure Column (HPC) 
The cooled air was introduced into the high -pressure column, where preliminary separation of nitrogen and 
oxygen occurred through cryogenic distillation. Due to differences in volatility, nitrogen-rich vapor accumulated 
at the top of the column while oxygen-enriched liquid collected at the bottom. The HPC acts as the first separation 
stage and supplies feed streams to the low-pressure column (Erdöl et al., 2024; Seader et al., 2024). 
2.8 Low-Pressure Column (LPC) 
The oxygen -enriched liquid from the HPC was further separated in the low -pressure column. Through 
repeated vapor –liquid equilibrium stages, nitrogen -rich products were recovered from the top of the column as 
gaseous nitrogen (GAN) and liquid nitrogen (LIN) , while oxygen -rich liquid accumulated at the bottom and 
was subsequently vaporized to produce gaseous oxygen (GOX). The separation performance of the LPC is 
strongly influenced by refrigeration availability and internal vapor –liquid equilibrium conditions  (Seader et al., 
2024; Smith et al., 2021) . 
2.9 Thermodynamic Model 
The Peng–Robinson (PR) equation of state was selected as the thermodynamic package for all simulations 
because it provides satisfactory predictions for vapor –liquid equilibrium and thermodynamic properties in 
cryogenic air separation processes. Similar thermodynamic approaches have been employed in previous cryogenic 
ASU simulation studies (Chen & Yu 2021; Erdöl et al., 2024). Ambient air was assumed to consist primarily of 
nitrogen, oxygen, argon, and carbon dioxide. In addition, a hypothetical dust component representing suspended 
particulate matter in ambient air was introduced with a mole fraction of 0.10% to simulate the presence of airborne 
particles. This component was completely removed in the baghouse filter prior to compression and purification, 
ensuring that only clean air entered the downstream cryogenic air separation unit. The hypothetical dust 
component was include d solely to represent particulate removal and was excluded from subsequent separation 
and product calculations. The feed composition used in the simulation is presented in Table 1.

3. Result and discussion 
3.1 Model validation 
The developed cryogenic ASU model successfully produced high-purity nitrogen and oxygen products under 
all operating conditions, as presented in Figure 3. The simulated low -pressure gaseous nitrogen (GAN) purity 
remained approximately 99%, while the liquid oxygen (LOX) purity ranged between 97% and 98%. These values 
are consistent with the purity range typically reported for industrial cryogenic air separation processes which also 
affected the specific power consumption (Ye et al., 2019).  
To further validate the developed simulation model, the optimum operating condition (Case A4, 
corresponding to a 50% expanded -air fraction) was compared with the cryogenic ASU simulation reported 
by Erdöl et al. (2024) , as summarized in Table 4 . The predicted liquid oxygen (LOX) and liquid nitrogen 
(LIN) purities exhibited relative deviations of only 0.49% and 0.40%, respectively. These low deviations 
demonstrate good agreement between the present Aspen HYSYS simulation and the published cryogen ic 
ASU model, confirming that the developed model is sufficiently reliable for evaluating the influence of 
expanded -air fraction on product distribution and energy performance.

The high product purities indicate that the selected process configuration, operating conditions, and Peng –
Robinson equation of state provide reliable predictions of vapor–liquid equilibrium under cryogenic conditions. 
Accurate representation of phase equilibrium is particularly important because the separation efficiency of 
nitrogen and oxygen strongly depends on the relative volatility between the two components at low temperatures. 
Therefore, the validated model provides a reliable basis for evaluating the influence of expanded-air fraction and 
refrigeration strategy on product recovery, energy consumption, and overall process performance. 
Figure 3 shows that increasing the expanded-air fraction from 20% to 50% increased GOX purity from 97.4% 
to 98.0%, while GAN and LIN purities remained nearly constant at approximately 99.0% and 99.5%, respectively. 
The GOX purity increased progressively with the expanded -air fraction, whereas the nitrogen product purities 
showed negligible variation. The JT valve configuration produced comparable nitrogen purities but a lower GOX 
purity of 97.4%.

The improvement in GOX purity with increasing expanded -air fraction is primarily attributed to the 
additional refrigeration generated by the turboexpander. A higher expanded -air fraction increases the amount 
of air undergoing expansion, resulting in a lowe r cold -end temperature before entering the distillation 
columns. The reduced operating temperature enhances oxygen condensation and improves the vapor –liquid 
equilibrium in the low -pressure column, thereby increasing oxygen enrichment in the gsaseous oxyge n 
product. In contrast, the operating pressures of the distillation columns remained nearly constant throughout 
all cases; therefore, the observed improvement in product purity was predominantly governed by temperature 
rather than pressure variations.  
 
3.2 Effect of expanded-air fraction on nitrogen product recovery 
Nitrogen and oxygen recoveries exceeded 99% for all investigated operating conditions, as depicted in Figure 
4, indicating that the developed simulation adequately represented the separation process. The result shows that 
nitrogen and oxygen recoveries remained above 99% under all investigated operating conditions. Nitrogen 
recovery remained essentially constant at approximately 100% as the expanded-air fraction increased from 20% 
to 50%. In contrast, oxygen recovery showed a slight increasing trend with increasing expanded -air fraction, 
reaching a maximum of 99.48% at 50% expanded air. These results indicate that in creasing the expanded -air 
fraction had only a minor effect on overall product recovery while maintaining high separation efficiency. 
In contrast, oxygen recovery exhibited a slight increase with increasing expanded -air fraction, reaching a 
maximum value of 99.48% at 50% expanded air. The improvement can be attributed to the enhanced 
refrigeration generated by the turboexpander, which promotes more effective oxygen enrichment in the LPC. 
The obtained product specifications demonstrate that the process configuration and operating conditions 
adopted in this work are suitable for evaluating the influence of expanded -air fraction and refrigeration strategy 
on ASU performance.  The slight improvement in product recovery with increasing expanded -air fraction is also 
associated with the enhanced refrigeration effect. Lower operating temperatures promote more effective phase 
separation within the distillation columns, reducing component losses and improving oxygen recovery. Since the 
column pressures were maintained at nearly constant values, the variation in recovery is mainly attributed to the 
improved vapor–liquid equilibrium resulting from the lower operating temperature.

Figure 5 shows a clear shift in nitrogen product distribution as the expanded -air fraction increased from 20% 
to 50%. GAN production increased progressively from 2,418 to 5,055 kmol.h⁻¹, corresponding to an increase of 
approximately 109%, while LIN production decreased from 7,000 to 4,375 kmol.h⁻¹, representing a reduction of 
approximately 37.5%. This trend indicates that a higher expanded -air fraction favors nitrogen recovery in the 
gaseous phase rather than the liquid phase. The shift in nitrogen product distribution is primarily attributed to the 
increased refrigeration generated by the turboexpander as the expanded -air fraction increases. Additional 
refrigeration lowers the operating temperature within the low -pressure column, thereby modifying the vapor –
liquid equilibrium and enhancing nitrogen enrichment  in the  vapor phase. Under these conditions, a larger  
fraction of nitrogen exits the column as gaseous nitrogen (GAN), while the amount condensed as liquid nitrogen 
(LIN) decreases. This behaviour reflects the strong dependence of cryogenic distillation performance on internal 
temperature profiles and phase equilibrium, where relatively small temperature changes can significantly 
influence the distribution of nitrogen between the vapor and liquid phases (Seader et al., 2024; Smith et al., 2021). 
From an industrial perspective, the ability to manipulate the distribution between gaseous and liquid 
nitrogen through operational adjustment provides greater process flexibility. Facilities requiring continuous 
gaseous nitrogen supply, such as natural gas  processing plants, refinery inerting systems, and petrochemical 
complexes, may benefit from higher expanded -air fractions because they maximize GAN production while 
maintaining high nitrogen recovery. Conversely, applications requiring liquid nitrogen sto rage or transportation 
may operate at lower expanded -air fractions to increase LIN production without substantial reductions in 
overall separation efficiency.

The portion of compressed air that bypasses the turboexpander is not cooled by an external refrigeration 
system. Instead, it is progressively cooled in the main heat exchanger through indirect heat exchange with the 
returning cryogenic product streams and the expanded air stream before entering the distillation columns. 
Therefore, even at the lowest expanded -air fraction (20%), the available refrigeration remains sufficient to cool 
the entire feed to cryogenic conditions, although the refrigeration duty and  temperature profile are less favorable 
than those obtained at higher expanded-air fractions. 
The lower operating temperature shifted the vapor –liquid equilibrium toward increased condensation of 
oxygen-rich liquid and enhanced enrichment of nitrogen in the vapor phase. Consequently, higher expanded -
air fractions resulted in improved oxygen purity and increased gaseous nitrogen production. This observation 
is consistent with the fundamental principles of multicomponent distillation, in which temperature directly 
affects phase equilibrium and component partitioning between vapor and liquid phases (Seader et al., 2024 ; 
Smith et al., 2021) . 
A nearly constant temperature region was observed between Stages 15 and 40, indicating that separation in 
this section was primarily governed by vapor–liquid equilibrium and latent heat transfer rather than sensible heat 
effects. Such behavior is characteristic of distillation columns operating near equilibrium conditions, where mass 
transfer and phase equilibrium dominate the separation process (Seader et al., 2024). 
In contrast, the JT valve configuration exhibited the highest temperature profile throughout the rectification 
section. The lower refrigeration effectiveness of the JT valve reduced the thermodynamic driving force for 
separation, resulting in lower oxygen enrichment compared with the turboexpander -based cases. This finding 
further demonstrates the advantage of turboexpander-generated refrigeration in maintaining favourable cryogenic 
separation conditions (Cheng et al., 2022; Huo et al., 2023).

controlling refrigeration generation but also a key variable influencing the thermodynamic behaviour of the 
cryogenic distillation process. Through its effect on column temperature profiles and vapor –liquid 
equilibrium, the expanded -air fraction simultaneously governs product distribution, separation efficiency, 
and energy consumption. These findings highlight the importance of refrigeration optimization in improving 
the performance of industrial cryogenic air separation units.  
Although the turboexpander demonstrated superior thermodynamic performance by reducing the specific 
power consumption, the overall industrial feasibility should also consider capital and operating costs 
associated with the turboexpander system. Since the p resent study focuses on thermodynamic performance 
evaluation, a comprehensive techno -economic assessment was beyond its scope. Future work should 
therefore integrate energy and economic analyses to determine the optimum operating configuration for 
industri al implementation.  
 
 
4. Conclusion 
The present study demonstrated that increasing the expanded -air fraction improved both separation 
performance and energy efficiency in a double-column cryogenic ASU. Increasing the expanded-air fraction from 
20% to 50% increased the GOX purity from 97.4% t o 98.0%, while maintaining GAN and LIN purities at 
approximately 99.0% and 99.5%, respectively. The optimum operating condition was achieved at an expanded -
air fraction of 50%, which provided the lowest specific power consumption (SPC) of 354.5  kWh.ton⁻¹ GOX and 
the lowest net energy consumption among the investigated cases. Compared with the JT valve configuration, the 
turboexpander-based refrigeration system exhibited superior product quality and energy performance due to 
enhanced refrigeration generation and mechanical power recovery.  The results demonstrated that increasing the 
expanded-air fraction improved refrigeration generation and power recovery, resulting in lower column thermal 
duties and reduced specific power consumption while maintaining high product purity and recovery. 
Analysis of the LPC temperature profiles showed that higher expanded -air fractions reduced the column 
operating temperature, thereby enhancing vapor –liquid equilibrium conditions. The improved separation 
environment promoted oxygen enrichment in the liquid  phase and nitrogen enrichment in the vapor phase, 
leading to increased gaseous nitrogen production and reduced liquid nitrogen production without significantly 
affecting overall recovery. These findings provide a thermodynamic explanation for the observed  
improvements in process performance.

Among the investigated operating conditions, the 50% expanded -air fraction exhibited the best overall 
performance, achieving the lowest specific power consumption and the highest oxygen purity. Compared with the

simultaneous refrigeration generation and mechanical work recovery. The results highlight the potential of 
expanded-air fraction as an effective operational variable for optimizing product distribution  and energy 
consumption in cryogenic air separation processes. 
Future work should incorporate comprehensive techno-economic analyses, including detailed assessments of

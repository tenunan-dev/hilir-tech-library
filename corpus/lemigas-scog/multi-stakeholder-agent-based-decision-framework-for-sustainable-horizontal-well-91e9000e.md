ABSTRACT - The oil industry is under mounting pressure to reconcile economic viability with environmental 
sustainability while managing complex stakeholder dynamics. Traditional modelling approaches struggle to 
capture emergent behaviours from multi -agent decision-making in sustainable oil recovery (Bonabeau, 2002; 
Macal & North, 2010). Three field wells are examined through an economic review within an agent -based 
framework that integrates Ultra-Deep Azimuthal Resistivity (UDAR) technology with 3D far -field petrophysics 
to optimize sustainable oil recovery by improving stakeholder collaboration (Manuaba et al., 2025a). We 
developed a multi -agent system comprising nine stakeholder types of petrophysicists, drilling engineers, 
geologists, reservoir engineers, oil operators, service companies, government regulators, oil market analysts, and 
geopolitical forces that incorporate UDAR’s 3D far-field characterization capabilities. The model examines four 
critical points: economic feasibility, environmental impact, technical risk, and political stability. An agent-based 
framework illustrates how UDAR technology serves as a central information hub, facilitating informed decision-
making across technical, business, and regulatory domains (Manuaba et al., 2024; Pewekar e t al., 2025). The 
model captures complex feedback mechanisms, including market signals, regulatory alignment, technology 
integration loops and human interactions. Sustainability metrics, such as well lifespan, energy efficiency, social 
impact, economic via bility, and resource conservation, are embedded in agents' decision -making processes 
(Erhueh et al., 2023). Results show that integrating UDAR technology with agent -based provides a robust 
framework for optimizing sustainable oil recovery operations. This approach enables coordinated stakeholder 
action while balancing competing objectives and adapting to dynamic market and regulatory conditions, offering 
significant potential for improving both economic and environmental outcomes. 
Keywords: sustainable oil recovery, energy policy, agent -based, drilling risk, UDAR technology, 3D far -field 
petrophysics.

1. Introduction 
The global oil sector now operates in an environment shaped by diverse stakeholders, competing objectives, 
and evolving regulatory frameworks (Höök & Tang 2013). Traditional approaches often miss the interconnected 
technical, economic, and policy dimensions that influence project outcomes. Recent advances in subsurface 
characterization, particularly Ultra-Deep Azimuthal Resistivity (UDAR) logging, create opportunities to enhance 
reservoir understanding and decision-making (Manuaba et al., 2025a). UDAR systems provide deep-investigation 
resistivity measurements that reveal geological features extending far beyond the borehole, supporting improved 
geo-steering, pay-zone identification, and enhanced oil recovery (EOR) decisions (Wu et al., 2023) . Alongside 
these technological developments, the agent -based framework has become a powerful tool for analyzing 
complex socio-technical systems. It can simulate autonomous actors, technical teams, regulatory bodies, market 
forces, and geopolitical players w hose interactions produce system -level behaviours that deterministic models 
cannot capture. For oil recovery, agent -based models were particularly valuable for examining technology 
adoption, stakeholder coordination, and regulatory compliance within an int egrated framework (Ringler et al., 
2016). Integrating UDAR technology with Agent -Based therefore presents a promising approach for designing 
sustainable oil recovery strategies that account for both subsurface complexity and stakeholder heterogeneity. 
1.1 Research motivation 
Today’s oil industry must strike a balance between economic performance and environmental sustainability. 
Increasingly stringent climate policies, stakeholder expectations, and regulatory mandates necessitate the adoption 
of more comprehensive decision -making frameworks (Höök & Tang 2013) . Existing reservoir and economic 
models, while sophisticated, often fail to integrate environmental considerations and stakeholder constraints into 
operational planning. UDAR technology improves reservoir characterization and reduces operational uncertainty, 
but successful integration requires coordinated decision-making among multiple stakeholder groups with distinct 
priorities, informational inputs, and risk perceptions.  Understanding these interactions is essential for designing 
sustainable oil recovery strategies that are both technically sound and socially acceptable (Clarkson 1995). 
1.2 Research objectives 
This study develops an integrated Agent-Based framework designed to: 
• Represent interactions and decision processes of key oil-sector stakeholders 
• Incorporate UDAR technology as a central data source affecting multi-agent decision-making 
• Embed sustainability metrics, such as environmental, economic and social, into agent behaviours  
• Evaluate trade-offs between competing operational, regulatory, and market objectives  
• Explore scenario-based outcomes under varying technological, regulatory, and geopolitical conditions.

1.4 Literature review 
Agent-based modelling has gained traction as a framework for analysing complex energy systems 
characterised by heterogeneous actors, decentralised decision-making, and dynamic feedback loops. Early Agent-
Based Framework implementations focused on electrici ty markets and supply chain behaviour, showing the 
method's ability to capture emergent system properties from local-level interactions. These studies have revealed 
that traditional top-down approaches often overlook the adaptive behaviours and bounded rat ionality inherent in 
real-world energy systems (Bonabeau 2002). 
As agent-based models matured, researchers applied them to broader energy contexts, including long -term 
planning, policy simulations, and operational efficiency. Applications in oil and gas expanded to refinery 
operations, maintenance scheduling, and produ ction planning, where multi -agent coordination reduced 
inefficiencies and improved operational resilience (Basu 2017) . Prior work demonstrates that agent -based 
frameworks are suitable for modelling socio-technical systems in which multiple stakeholders influence outcomes 
through iterative, interdependent decision -making, making them particularly appropriate for sustainab le oil 
recovery analysis.  
Ultra-Deep Azimuthal Resistivity (UDAR) technology represents a significant advancement in formation 
evaluation, enabling the detection of resistivity contrasts far beyond the wellbore (Manuaba et al., 2025a). Unlike 
conventional resistivity tools that rely on near -borehole measurements, UDAR systems deliver far -field 
petrophysical information, enabling operators to map geological structures, fluid contacts, and stratigraphic 
variations at substantially greater depth (Figure 1). Conventional resistivity, also known as propagation resistivity, 
can reach up to 4 ft, whereas UDAR resistivity can reach up to 100 ft.

This enhanced detection is particularly valuable for real -time geo -steering, where continuous directional 
resistivity measurements support more informed well placement decisions. Examples show UDAR technology 
enhances the ability to identify bypassed pay z ones, optimise lateral trajectories, and reduce drilling risks, 
particularly in complex or thinly laminated reservoirs (Ameneiro et al., 2023 ; Manuaba et al., 2024) . Recent 
advances in inversion algorithms have further refined UDAR's resolution, enabling better structural and 
stratigraphic interpretation even in transitional resistivity environments.

Sustainable oil recovery has evolved from maximising hydrocarbon extraction to a holistic framework 
incorporating environmental, social, and governance (ESG) considerations (Elkington 1997). Traditional recovery 
approaches prioritised economic returns while giving limited regard to external environmental costs. However, 
increasing regulatory scrutiny and global climate commitments have shifted industry priorities toward sustainable 
operational practices (Bui et al., 2018). 
Modern sustainable recovery strategies emphasise reducing emissions, minimising water consumption, 
improving energy efficiency, and integrating circular resource -use practices. Technological innovation plays a 
central role in this transition. Advanced reservoir characterization tools, such as UDAR, enable the more precise 
identification of productive zones, thereby reducing unnecessary drilling and associated emissions. 
Oil recovery projects involve diverse stakeholders with distinct incentives, constraints, and decision processes. 
Technical stakeholders, geologists, reservoir engineers, and petrophysicists prioritize subsurface accuracy, 
operational safety, and technical  feasibility. Business stakeholders, including operators and service companies, 
focus on profitability, market positioning, and investment efficiency. Regulatory entities emphasize compliance, 
environmental protection, and public welfare, while global mark ets and geopolitical forces influence oil prices, 
investment flows, and cross-border regulatory alignment. 
Stakeholder theory suggests these actors exert varying degrees of influence depending on their power, 
legitimacy, and urgency. However, traditional optimization models often fail to capture the interactive and 
sometimes conflicting relationships among stakeholder groups. An agent-based framework addresses this gap by 
representing heterogeneous stakeholders with unique objectives, behavioural rules, and learning mechanisms. 
Simulating stakeholder interactions enables researchers to explore emergent system -level dynamics arising from 
coordinated or uncoordinated decision processes, providing insights into how technology adoption, regulatory 
changes, and market shifts influence sustainable oil recovery outcomes. 
 
2. Methodology 
This study employs agent-based frameworks to represent complex socio-technical interactions in sustainable 
oil recovery operations. An agent -based framework established a platform for simulating heterogeneous actors 
and emergent system behaviours. It is also well-suited to this application because oil recovery decisions arise from 
decentralised interactions among technical experts, regulatory institutions, market agents, and geopolitical forces, 
each operating under bounded rationality and limited information. 
The framework adheres to core agent-based principles: heterogeneity, bounded rationality, local interactions, 
emergent behaviour, and adaptive learning. Agents differ in their objectives, capabilities, and decision -making 
rules; they make decisions with in complete information, interact with one another and their environment, and 
adjust their behaviour in response to feedback and outcomes (Bonabeau 2002). 
The agent -based framework includes nine agent classes organized into three functional groups: technical, 
business, and regulatory/market stakeholders. Technical agents represent petrophysicists, drilling engineers, 
geologists, and reservoir engineers who prioritize operational integrity, data accuracy, and technical performance. 
Business agents include oil operators and service companies focusing on economic performance, investment 
efficiency, and strategic planning (Frooman 1999) . Regulatory and market agents encompass government 
regulators, oil market participants, and geopolitical actors who shape the external conditions under which 
operations occur. 
UDAR technology is modelled as a high -resolution information subsystem that delivers multidimensional 
insights into resistivity beyond the immediate wellbore (Manuaba et al., 2025a) . Its capabilities include 3D 
far-field characterisation, azimuthal sensitivity, real -time data updates, and support for identifying bypassed 
zones and optimising EOR strategies. By embedding UDAR as a central information node, the model 
captures how advan ced subsurface imaging improves decision -making across technical, business, and 
regulatory domains (Manuaba et al., 2024) .

The model incorporates a multidimensional decision framework grounded in four key criteria that influence 
all agent classes: economic feasibility, environmental impact, technical risk, and political stability. Economic 
feasibility is assessed using net pre sent value (NPV) and return on investment (ROI) metrics. Environmental 
impact encompasses emissions, water usage, and energy efficiency. Technical risk arises from uncertainties in 
geology, drilling, and tool performance. Political stability captures regul atory consistency, geopolitical risk, and 
long-term investment security (Agle et al., 1999). 
Multiple iterations of a feasibility study are conducted on the UDAR -based BHA prior to the start of the 
development campaign (Figure 2). Beyond this, a more extensive simulation is done for the different dimensions 
mentioned in the frame. For example, different scenarios are simulated to evaluate and quantify technology 
adoption, such as including the elimination of pilot holes, sidetrack  avoidance, and drilling performance 
improvements enabled by UDAR technology. 
Validation followed a multi -step approach involving face validity, empirical comparison, and sensitivity 
analysis (Macal & North 2010) . Subject-matter experts reviewed the model structure and agent rules, historical 
behaviour, and published industry data to benchmark outputs. Key parameters were systematically varied to assess 
robustness and internal consistency.

3.1 Agent Architecture and Interactions 
This section outlines the behavioural rules, interaction mechanisms, and feedback structures that govern agent 
dynamics in the proposed model. Together, these components shape emergent outcomes of sustainable oil 
recovery operations. Each agent type operates according to distinct behavioural rules that reflect professional, 
organisational, and external constraints observed in real -world oil recovery environments. Technical agents 
prioritise data quality, risk management, performance op timisation, and collaborative information sharing. 
Business agents focus on profit maximization, risk balancing, market positioning, and stakeholder management. 
Regulatory agents emphasize compliance monitoring, policy development, and international coordination where 
applicable (Clarkson 1995). 
Agent interactions occur through structured communication channels, negotiation processes, and collaborative 
decision mechanisms. Information exchange forms the backbone of communication, with UDAR playing a central 
role by providing real-time resistivity measurements and multidimensional subsurface visualization (Manuaba et 
al., 2025a ; Pewekar et al., 2025) . Negotiation protocols arise when agents’ objectives conflict or require 
alignment, for example, in budget allocation, regulatory approvals, or service contracting. Collaborative decision-
making occurs through multidisciplinary project teams, technical committees, and regulatory consultations.

Feedback loops are embedded in three primary mechanisms. The market feedback loop captures how oil 
prices, shifts in demand, and geopolitical events influence production strategies and technology adoption. The 
regulatory alignment loop represents an iterative interaction among operators, service companies, and regulators

UDAR performance influences its adoption, with learning effects and innovation feedback reinforcing its strategic 
value over time.

3.2 Decision rules, parameter sources, and simulation settings 
Decision rules. Each agent class follows condition -action rules operating on a shared set of state variables 
(UDAR data confidence, water -cut trend, oil price, and a regulatory -stringency index). Representative rules 
include: (i) petrophysicists and geologists trigger re-evaluation of the geo-steering plan when the UDAR-derived 
stand-off from the gas-oil or water-oil contact falls below a minimum threshold, consistent with the ~30 ft stand-
off applied on well XYZ-02; (ii) drilling engineers select the BHA configuration (LWD-only vs. LWD+UDAR) 
by comparing incremental service cost against the expected reduction in pilot-hole/sidetrack probability; (iii) the 
oil operator approves continued UDAR deployment when projected incremental NPV exceeds a minimum hurdle 
rate, consistent with the 10% discount rate in Table 7 ; (iv) government/regulator agents revise compliance 
requirements when observed environmental or safety indicators cross the low/medium/high stringency thresholds 
summarized in Table 4; and (v) oil-market and geopolitical agents update price and risk signals each period from 
the same Brent price series used in the well economic evaluation (Table 7), which propagates to operator and 
service-company decisions. 
Parameter sources. Parameters were drawn from three sources: (1) published industry and academic literature 
on UDAR performance, agent -based methodology, and sustainability metrics, cited throughout the Literature 
Review and Materials and Methods sections;  (2) project/field data for the three example wells, including the 
drilling and completion cost breakdowns (Tables 8, 11, 14), production and water-cut histories (Tables 9-10, 12-
13, 15-16), and the fiscal and operating assumptions in Table 7; and (3) structured consultation with subject -
matter experts in petrophysics, drilling engineering, and reservoir/economic evaluation, who reviewed the agent 
behavioral rules and parameter ranges for realism. 
Simulation settings. The model was implemented using a discrete time step matching the quarterly reporting 
interval used in the well economic assessment (Tables 9 -10, 12-13, 15-16). Each scenario (Example #1 - 
Conventional Approach; Example #2 - UDAR-Enabled Approach; and the three sensitivity scenarios in Table 
4) was run to characterize variability in oil price and water -cut trajectories, with results reported as most likely 
case values.  
Validation and sensitivity analysis. Model validation combined three complementary checks, following Macal 
& North (2010): (1) face validity, in which subject-matter experts reviewed agent structures, interaction rules, and 
simulated outputs against professional experience; (2) empirical comparison, in which simulated technology -
adoption paths, cost trajectories, and water -cut development were benchmarked against the historical field data 
for wells XYZ -01, XYZ -02, and XYZ -03 (Tables 6 -16); and (3) one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis, in which 
UDAR service cost, regulatory stringency, and oil-price volatility were varied to test the robustness of the adoption 
and economic outcomes summarized in Table 4.

3.3 Agent-based framework

development project. Implementation integrates heterogeneous stakeholders, operational constraints, and 
technological inputs from UDAR measurements (Manuaba et al., 2025a). Parameter values were selected through 
review of published industry literature and consultation with domain experts to ensure realistic field conditions. 
The model includes nine agent categories, with population sizes that reflect typical organizational structures 
in oil field operations. Key variable parameters include the adoption rate of UDAR technology, environmental 
regulation stringency, and oil price volatility.

3.5 Identifying Water Corridors and Completion Design 
Another advantage of UDAR technology is optimising production and reducing water production delays by 
detecting potential water corridors that contribute to water flow toward the wellbore trajectory. This can be 
achieved through a combination of in -depth investigation of UDAR and microimaging from other services. By 
incorporating 360-degree (azimuthal) perspectives, these advanced techniques provide a significantly improved 
understanding of fluid behaviour within reservoir layers, supporting more informed m anagement decisions 
(Manuaba et al., 2023).  
Knowing the potential water path, production can be optimized by setting packers/barriers in the right section 
to delay water flow and allow only hydrocarbon flow into the wellbore. The illustration in Figure 7 below was 
proposed for one of the wells, which used UDAR technology along with a micro -resistivity image to identify 
fractures. The completion design is optimized across different zones of the rock structure, which can help prevent 
early water breakthroughs and improve overall sweep efficiency (Manuaba et al., 2023).

4. Result and discussion 
Results highlight the substantial potential of integrating an agent-based framework with Ultra-Deep Azimuthal 
Resistivity (UDAR) technology to support sustainable oil recovery. The combined framework enables systematic 
assessment of how technological innov ation, stakeholder behaviour, and regulatory structures co -evolve to 
influence operational and sustainability outcomes. The model captures the complex interplay between technology, 
stakeholder coordination, regulatory drivers, and market forces (Agle et al., 1999). 
Results demonstrate UDAR technology can yield significant improvements across multiple sustainability 
dimensions, including economic performance, environmental responsibility, technical efficiency, and stakeholder 
alignment. Enhanced subsurface intelligence improves reservoir understanding, reduces drilling uncertainty, and 
optimises well placement. Environmental benefits are anticipated through more precise well placement and 
reduced remedial operations, though direct measurement of emission and resource-use reductions was outside the 
scope of this study. The agent-based framework underscores that these benefits are maximised when stakeholders 
share information and coordinate decisions across disciplinary and organisational boundaries.  
The framework yields several policy -relevant insights (Clarkson 1995) . Economic incentives, such as tax 
credits, investment subsidies, and accelerated depreciation, can encourage the adoption of sustainable 
technologies.  
From an industry perspective, the agent-based and UDAR integration framework offers practical applications 
for strategic planning, operational optimization, and regulatory engagement. Operators can use the framework to 
explore long -term technology investme nt strategies and risk profiles. Technical teams benefit from integrated 
workflows that enhance real -time decision -making and continuous improvement. Regulators can employ the 
framework to test policy options before implementation and refine compliance mechanisms (Clarkson 1995). 
Novelty relative to prior work. Prior agent -based studies in the energy sector have largely modelled market, 
policy, or supply-chain dynamics without an explicit link to subsurface data quality (e.g., Bonabeau 2002; Basu 
2017; Ringler et al., 2016), while UDAR-focused studies have concentrated on measurement physics, inversion, 
and geo-steering performance without representing the surrounding stakeholder decision environment (Ameneiro 
et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023; Albaqami et al., 2025). The present study addresses this gap by embedding UDAR 
as a shared information node within a nine -agent, three-domain (technical/business/regulatory-market) decision 
framework, and by field-validating that framework against economic and production data from three sequentially 
drilled wells with increasing UDAR utilization (0%, 30%, and 100%). To the authors' knowledge, this 
combination of an agent-based, multi-stakeholder decision model parameterised and validated using real drilling, 
completion, and cash -flow data, and tied to a specific formation -evaluation technology, has not been reported 
previously. 
Despite its advantages, the framework has several limitations. Agent behaviours are necessarily simplified 
representations of complex human and organizational dynamics (Bonabeau 2002) . The geographic and 
operational scope is restricted to a single representative field, and regulatory conditions are treated as static across 
simulation runs. UDAR capabilities are modelled as fixed, though real -world technologies evolve (Albaqami et 
al., 2025 ; Wu et al., 2023) . Future research should incorporate dynamic regulation, evolving technology 
performance and costs, multi -field or regional simulations, and more sophisticated behavioural models that 
capture social influence, organizational learning, and bounded rationali ty in greater detail (Phillips et al., 2003 ; 
Ringler et al., 2016). 
 
5. Conclusion 
This study presents a comprehensive agent -based framework integrating Ultra -Deep Azimuthal Resistivity 
(UDAR) technology with multi -stakeholder decision -making to advance sustainable oil recovery (Bonabeau 
2002; Macal & North 2010 ; Manuaba et al., 2025a) . Results demonstrate considerable potential of combining 
high-resolution subsurface characterization with multi -agent coordination to achieve improved economic,

environmental, and operational performance. The model captures the complex interplay among technology, 
stakeholder behaviour, regulatory structures, and market forces, offering valuable insights into the mechanisms 
that enable sustainable field development (Freeman 1984; Ringler et al., 2016). 
Key findings include UDAR technology’s central role as an information hub (Manuaba et al., 2025a ; 
Pewekar et al., 2025) , the importance of stakeholder coordination in realizing sustainability benefits, and the 
feasibility of embedding environmental and social metrics into operational decision -making. Analysis shows 
that government policy frameworks, including economic ince ntives and performance standards, shape the 
conditions under which technology adoption and sustainability improvements occur. Feedback mechanisms in 
the model facilitate adaptive management, enabling agents to adjust their strategies based on observed syst em 
performance (Macal & North 2010) .  
The research has several practical implications. Oil operators can use the framework to inform strategic 
planning and technology investment decisions. Service companies can refine deployment and integration 
strategies. Regulators can evaluate potential impacts of policy interventions, and technology developers can align 
innovation with evolving industry and policy needs.  In terms of contribution to knowledge, the study offers 
methodological innovation by developing an agent -based framework specifically tail ored to the oil industry 
(Bonabeau 2002 ; Macal & North 2010) , demonstrates a robust approach for integrating UDAR into multi -
stakeholder decision environments (Manuaba et al., 2025a) , and provides a structured way to incorporate 
sustainability considerations and policy analysis into operational modelling (Elkington 1997; Ringler et al., 2016). 
Based on these insights, we recommend wider adoption of agent -based decision support systems in the oil 
sector (Macal & North 2010) , targeted policy instruments to encourage sustainable technology deployment, 
international cooperation to harmonize environmental and operational standards (Bazilian et al., 2011) , and 
continued research to extend the model to broader geographies, more dynamic regulatory environments, and more 
detailed behavioral representations (Phillips et al., 2003; Ringler et al., 2016).

ABSTRACT - Sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB) are major drivers of microbiologically influenced corrosion 
(MIC) and reservoir souring in waterflooding operations, particularly in mature onshore assets with high 
produced-water volumes. This is showing the need for effective control of SRB to mitigate potential risks and 
maintain the integrity of waterflooding operations. Therefore, this study is aiming to evaluate the efficacy of the 
biocide MB-X at multiple doses (50-200 ppm) in suppressing SRB activity and population using the SRB-BART 
(Biological Activity Reaction Test).  The experiment is being conducted by collecting produced -water samples 
from the Outlet WT at Field MX, followed by daily monitoring of SRB activity to determine the reaction day. 
The data obtained are being converted into population estimates (CFU/mL) acc ording to the commercial kit 
guidance: Day-3 = 1.15 × 10⁵ CFU/mL; Day-4 = 2.7 × 10⁴ CFU/mL; Day-10 = 5 CFU/mL; Day-11 < 1 CFU/mL. 
Aggressiveness is being classified according to the kit scoring, namely Aggressive, Not Aggressive, and Negative. 
The results are showing that the control (blank) is showing a BB-type SRB consortium (anaerobe-dominant), with 
1.15 × 10⁵ CFU/mL being classified as Aggressive. MB -X 50 ppm is reducing the population to 2.7 × 10⁴ 
CFU/mL, but the response is remaining Aggressive. In co mparison, MB-X 100 ppm is suppressing counts to 5 
CFU/mL (Not Aggressive), while MB -X 200 ppm is yielding a Negative response, showing undetected SRB 
activity. These results are indicating that MB-X at ≥100 ppm is effectively controlling SRB under test conditions, 
with 200 ppm achieving apparent eradication as detected by SRB-BART. However, interpretation of the statistical 
outcomes is being constrained by the limited number of treatment replicates, suggesting that the results should be 
considered a preliminary site-specific evaluation of MB -X performance. Consequently, future studies are being 
recommended to conduct field verification through replication, matrix controls, and in-situ corrosion monitoring 
to confirm performance durability. 
Keywords: sulfate-reducing bacteria, MIC, produced water, biocide MB‑X, SRB‑BART, mature onshore asset.

1. Introduction 
Sulfate‑reducing bacteria (SRB) are a group of anaerobic microorganisms that use sulfate as a terminal electron 
acceptor and produce hydrogen sulfide (H₂S). In the oil and gas industry, SRB contribute significantly to reservoir 
souring and microbiologicall y influenced corrosion (MIC) of carbon‑steel pipelines, pressure vessels, and 
processing equipment, thereby increasing leakage risk and asset maintenance costs (OPEX) (Spark et al., 2020). 
The MIC is typically characterized b y the formation of ferrous sulfide (FeS) deposits and progressive pitting 
corrosion. Previous studies in upstream operations have reported premature pipeline failures associated with SRB 
activity and the accumulation of H₂S within biofilms (Gerrit Voordouw et al., 2017; Udowo et al., 2023). 
In produced or treated water systems and waterflooding operations, SRB commonly persist as constituents of 
biofilms adhered to metal surfaces, showing high res istance to chemical treatments and conventional 
microbiological monitoring (Okpala et al., 2026). Consequently, mitigation strategies must address dissolved H₂S 
and the underlying bioburden of these microorganisms  to prevent microbial resurgence and the continuation of 
corrosion processes (PureLine 2025). Experimental evidence shows that SRB biofilms require higher biocide 
doses than planktonic cells to achieve significant suppression of sulfidogenesis, determining a minimum effective 
dose (MED), a critical component of control programs (Fajt 2023). 
For rapid detection and semi‑quantitative monitoring, the SRB‑BART (Biological Activity Reaction Test) is 
widely used. Th is method uses  blackening intensity, BB/BT dominance patterns, and days‑to‑reaction as 
indicators of SRB population and aggressiveness (Hach Company 2016). The reaction time provides a practical 
basis for estimating SRB population levels, with official guidelines connecting reaction‑day values to estimated 
populations (CFU/mL). For example, a Day‑1 reaction corresponds to 2.2×10⁶ CFU/mL, Day‑3 to approximately 
1.15×10⁵ CFU/mL, Day‑4 to 2.7×10⁴ CFU/mL, while Day‑10 (~5 CFU/mL) and Day‑11 (<1 CFU/mL) show very 
low populations and “Not Aggressive” behavior (Tm 2014) . In addition to providing presence/absence and 
dominant‑pattern outcomes, SRB‑BART is practical for field monitoring due to the lack of specialized culturing 
facilities and can be observed daily for ≥10–15 days as recommended in technical advisories. 
Chemical SRB control is commonly achieved through biocide application using shock (intermittent) or 
continuous dosing schemes.  The effectiveness of these trea tments depends on several site -specific factors, 
including microbiome composition, biofilm matrix characteristics, and water chemistry  such as oxidant  
consumption by sulfide/thiosulfate. Therefore, dose‑response testing using site‑specific matrices is esse ntial 
before full implementation (Okpala et al., 2026) . Laboratory and field studies have shown that agents such as 
ADBAC, THPS, and glutaraldehyde can be effective at tens to hundreds of ppm, although optimal dos es vary 
across locations,  and established biofilms require higher doses to eliminate sulfidogenic activity  (Fajt 2023) . 
Establishing the minimum effective dose (MED) from laboratory and field data, followed by in-situ performance 
verification using methods such as  corrosion coupons , is critical for balancing performance, cost, and risk             
(Xu et al., 2024). 
Recent literature has reported that SRB embedded in biofilms are more challenging to eradicate than 
planktonic cells and require sufficient biocide penetration, assisted by bio‑dispersants or biocide enhancers. 
Furthermore, combination strategies can reduce required biocide doses for effective control. For example, THPS 
combined with biofilm‑disrupting peptides has been shown to reduce pitting and mass loss in 13Cr steel exposed 
to highly corrosive SRB . Nitrite pretreatment that lowers cellular metabolic energy can enhance the efficacy of 
glutaraldehyde, allowing for dose reduction. Due to the diversity and site‑specific nature of oilfield microbiomes, 
dose determination based on local datasets remains best practice (Bødtker et al., 2008; Javier Vilcaez et al., 2006; 
Xu et al., 2024). 
Despite the widespread application of biocide programs, two practice gaps persist in many water‑injection 
facilities. First, programs often rely on qualitative microbiological indicators or a single endpoint without parallel 
quantitative integrity verification, making it difficult to determine whether microbial control translates into 
reduced corrosion risk. Second, there is limited formal establishment of a threshold effective concentration (TEC) 
defined as the minimum concentration that simultaneously achieves microbial suppression (BART 
negative/non‑aggressive) and acceptable corrosion performance (low mass loss/pitting rate) . These gaps persist

2. Methodology 
2.1 Study design and objectives 
This laboratory‑based experimental study was carried out using a quantitative and descriptive method. 
Observations were conducted to evaluate changes in SRB population and aggressiveness following treatment with 
the MB‑X biocide at different concentrations. A total of three doses (50, 100, and 200 ppm) were tested and compared 
with a blank control. Effectiveness was assessed using the SRB‑BART (Biological Activity Reaction Test) method 
through daily reaction time (days‑to‑reaction), tube appearance patterns  (BB/BT/BB+BT/Cloudy/Negative), 
estimated population (CFU/mL), and aggressiveness category (Aggressive/Not Aggressive/Negative) according to 
HACH guidelines. This method was widely applied for rapid screening and semi‑quantitative monitoring of SRB in 
oilfield facilities. The experimental variables employed in this study are categorized as independent, dependent, 
and controlled variables as follows: 
2.1.A Independent variable (X) 
MB‑X biocide concentration: 0 ppm (Blank/Control), 50 ppm, 100 ppm, 200 ppm. 
2.1.B Dependent variables (Y) 
 SRB response parameters based on the SRB‑BART assay, including: 
• Tube appearance 
• Time‑to‑reaction (days) → indicator of SRB population 
• Estimated SRB population (CFU/mL) based on days‑to‑reaction 
2.1.C Controlled variables 
• Sample volume (uniform for all tubes) 
• Type of biocide (MB‑X) 
• Storage and incubation conditions (room temperature, dark) 
• Sampling time 
 
2.2 Sample location and matrix 
Produced-water samples were collected from the Outlet W ater Treatment of the MX Field within an onshore 
water‑treatment system. SRB are commonly present in process‑water pipelines and are associated with biofilms 
that increase souring/H₂S risk and MIC in carbon steel. Sampling procedures adhered to basic aseptic practices to 
avoid cross‑contamination, thereby preventing contact with vial interiors and using sterile containers, as 
recommended in BART procedures.

2.3 Reagents and biocide dosing  
The reagents, biocide concentrations, preparation procedures, and control conditions used in this study are 
described as follows: 
• Biocide: MB‑X 
• Test concentrations: 50, 100, and 200 ppm (v/v or mg/L) in the test water matrix. 
• Rationale: 
Biocides are commonly applied at concentrations ranging from tens to hundreds of ppm for SRB 
control in process water, while biofilm‑associated SRB typically require higher concentrations than 
planktonic cells. Therefore, determining the MED unde r context -specific conditions is recommended 
before field implementation.  
• Preparation: 
MB‑X stock solution was diluted with sample water to obtain target concentrations. Each treatment was 
prepared according to laboratory availability and operational const raints. The blank control was evaluated 
in triplicate, while the 50, 100, and 200 ppm were evaluated using single technical replicates. 
Consequently, this study was intended as a preliminary dose -response assessment, suggesting that future 
investigations s hould include at least three independent replicates per treatment to improve statistical 
power and reliability.  
• Control: Blank sample (no biocide) representing baseline SRB population and aggressiveness. 
2.4 SRB-BART assay and incubation 
The SRB‑BART assay (DBI/Hach) was performed following the manufacturer’s instructions: 
2.4.1 Filling: Add ≥20 mL of sample to the outer tube, fill the inner tube to the marked line, reinsert the inner   
          tube, avoid shaking, and allow the floating ball to remain undisturbed. 
2.4.2 Labeling and incubation: Label with sample ID/date and incubate at room temperature away from  
          direct light.  
2.4.3 Daily observations: Inspect daily for ≥10 days. For deeply infested or slow‑growing samples,  
          observations may extend to Day‑15 per technical advisories. 
Criteria: 
2.4.4 Positive/Aggressive: Formation of black slime (ring around the ball and/or black precipitate at the bottom). 
2.4.5 Negative/Non‑aggressive: Absence of blackening or cloudiness indicative of SRB. 
2.4.6 Dominant patterns: 
• BB (black at base): Predominantly anaerobic SRB consortia 
• BT (black at top/ball): Aerobic SRB‑associated behavior 
• BB + BT/Cloudy: Mixed populations or presence of other anaerobes.

2.6  Statistical analysis 
Analyses were conducted using non‑parametric methods because of small sample size and skewed, log‑order 
data. All tests were two‑tailed with α = 0.05. 
• Descriptive: 
Median and range (min–max) of CFU and log₁₀(CFU/mL) per dose, percentage of Aggressive results per dose. 
• Group comparison: 
Kruskal–Wallis (1952) test for detecting median differences across doses (performed when ≥2 groups have 
n≥1). Dunn’s post‑hoc test planned when replication allows. 
• Effect size: 
Cliff’s delta compared with the Blank to quantify distributional separation (−1 to +1; | δ| > 0.474 shows a 
large effect for small samples). 
• Dose–response: 
Spearman rank correlation between dose (ppm) and log₁₀(CFU/mL) to evaluate monotonic trends. 
• Aggressive proportion: 
Wilson 95% confidence intervals, which provide stable estimates for small n. 
• Fold‑reduction: 
Median Blank/Median dose as a relative efficacy indicator (range reported; bootstrap CI if replicates sufficient). 
• Justification: 
Non‑parametric tests, non‑parametric effect sizes, and rank‑based trend statistics are standard for small, 
skewed count or semi‑quantitative datasets. SRB‑BAR T outputs ordinal‑continuous population estimates 
appropriate for this analytical framework. 
2.7  Quality assurance/quality control (QA/QC) 
To maintain the accuracy and integrity of the experimental results, the following quality assurance and quality 
control (QA/QC) procedures were applied throughout the study. 
• Aseptic method during BART preparation, avoid touching interior surfaces and shaking tubes (prevents 
false positives/negatives). 
• Process control: Blank without biocide, technical duplicates per dose when feasible. 
• Interference control: H₂S > 20 ppm may affect readings, the manufacturer advises simple degassing when 
high levels are suspected. 
• Observation consistency: Daily readings at consistent times, optional photographic documentation 
for traceability.  
• Safety: Biocide and reactive residues handled according to SDS; reaction residues deactivated/sterilized 
before disposal.

3. Result and discussion 
3.1 SRB activity in the untreated sample (blank control) 
All three blank samples showed a reaction on Day 3, corresponding to a population of 1.15×10⁵ CFU/mL, with 
a BB tube pattern and an Aggressive interpretation. This result showed a stable and anaerobe -dominant SRB 
consortium in the produced/treated water, indicating a high sulfidogenic potential typical of mature onshore water 
systems. The blank dataset was highly consistent across replicates, showing strong baseline SRB activity and 
validating the suitability of the sample matrix for evaluating biocide performance. 
3.2 Effect of MB‑X at 50 ppm 
The 50‑ppm treatment showed the SRB reaction to Day 4, reducing the estimated population to 2.7×10⁴ 
CFU/mL (approximately a 4.3‑fold decrease relative to the blank). Despite th e reduction, the tube appearance 
remained BB, and the s ample continued to be classified as Aggressive. This suggest ed that MB‑X at 50 ppm 
provided some inhibitory effect  but was insufficient to overcome biofilm‑associated SRB resilience. Biofilm 
protection prevented full penetration of the biocide, a phenomenon widely reported for oilfield SRB communities. 
3.3 Effect of MB‑X at 100 ppm 
At 100 ppm, the microorganism activity was significantly suppressed, with the reaction occurring only on Day 
10 (equivalent to 5 CFU/mL). This represents a significant 23,000‑fold r eduction relative to the blank. 
Subsequently, the  sample transitioned to a Not Aggressive classification, indicating that MB‑X at 100 ppm 
successfully reduced SRB bioburden to levels associated with minimal sulfidogenic and MIC risk. This dose 
represents a strong candidate for the MED under laboratory conditions. 
3.4 Effect of MB‑X at 200 ppm 
The 200‑ppm treatment led to no detectable SRB activity through Day 11, which corresponded to <1 CFU/mL 
(treated as 0.5 CFU/mL during log analysis). The tube appearance was Negative, suggesting apparent eradication 
of the microorganisms within the detection limits of the BART method. This performance signifies that 200 ppm 
is sufficient as a shock dose, typically used in field operations for system cleanup or during outbreaks. The results 
are consistent with recent studies, indicating that biofilm -associated SRB often require elevated biocide 
concentrations for complete suppression. Okpala et al. (2026)  reported that mature SRB biofilms showed 
significant tolerance to chemical treatment due to protection by extracellular polymeric substances (EPS). 
Similarly, Xu et al. (2024)  observed that effective control of SRB -related c orrosion frequently required 
concentrations capable of penetrating biofilm structures and disrupting microbial metabolism. The transition from 
Aggressive (50 ppm) to Not Aggressive (100 ppm) and Negative (200 ppm) observed in this study well with these 
reported biofilm-control mechanisms.

suppressed despite retained pattern characteristics.

3.7 Operational implications for souring and MIC control 
Based on the SRB population and aggressiveness indicators: 
• 50 ppm is insufficient for operational control and would not reduce souring or MIC risk. 
• 100 ppm is effective in shifting SRB behavior to Not Aggressive, which is suitable for maintenance dosing 
regimens. 
• 200 ppm achieves BART‑negative conditions, supporting its role as a shock treatment for high‑risk or 
infested systems. 
These results are consistent with industry best practices that recommend site‑specific MED determination and 
validation through corrosion monitoring tools such as weight‑loss coupons. From a practical field perspective, the 
results provide a preliminary basis for optimizing biocide treatment programs in produced -water systems. Based 
on the observed SRB -BART resp onses, MB -X at  100 ppm can serve as a maintenance dose to sustain SRB 
populations at Not Aggressive levels during routine operation, while 200 ppm shows potential for shock treatment 
at elevated microbial activity or confirmed SRB outbreaks. This method is  consistent with current 
recommendations emphasizing site-specific biocide management and performance verification through integrated 
microbiological and corrosion monitoring programs (Shi et al., 2023; LV et al., 2024). In practice, routine SRB-
BART monitoring should be used together with corrosion coupon evaluation to verify that microbial suppression 
is accompanied by a measurable reduction in MIC risk. 
3.8 Limitations and recommendations for future study 
Although the experimental outcomes show a clear dose -dependent response of MB -X, the results should be 
interpreted considering several study limitations. First, treatment groups (50, 100, and 200 ppm) are 
represented by single technical replicate s, which limit statistical power and can underestimate experimental 
variability. Second, SRB -BART provides semi -quantitative population estimates rather than direct microbial 
enumeration. Third, this study focuses exclusively on microbiological response an d does not include direct 
corrosion measurements such as coupon weight loss or pitting depth evaluation. Consequently, the observed 
reduction in SRB activity strongly suggests reduced MIC risk, but direct confirmation through corrosion 
monitoring remains n ecessary.

treated values lower than the blank observations. Although the Kruskal-Wallis test did not reach significance (H=5.00; 
p=0.172) due to limited and unbalanced replication (blank n=3; each dose n=1), the effect magnitude and consistency 
across metrics were unambiguous. The proportion of Aggressive outcomes also transitions from 100% (blank, 50 ppm) 
to 0% (100 and 200 ppm). Although the Wilson 95% confidence intervals are necessarily wide because of the small 
sample size, the categorical results remain directionally consistent with the quantitative response. 
From an opera tional standpoint, the results indicate that MB‑X ≥100 ppm is sufficient to reduce SRB 
populations to Not Aggressive levels (≈5 CFU/mL) under the tested conditions, supporting application as a 
maintenance dose. Meanwhile, 200 ppm is suitable as a shock dose for rapid suppression to BART‑negative status. 
The surrogate handling of <1 CFU/mL as 0.5 CFU/mL for log analysis was prespecified and methodologically 
appropriate for semi‑quantitative BART outputs. Therefore, future s tudies should increase replication to a 
minimum of three technical or biological replicates per dose to enhance inferential power  and enable post-hoc 
comparisons, including Dunn’s test. Microbiology control should also be evaluated alongside  in‑situ corrosion 
metrics such as mass loss and pitting rate on coupons to formalize a TEC that simultaneously satisfies microbial 
and corrosion endpoints. 
 
4. Conclusion 
In conclusion, this study successfully evaluates the efficacy of MB -X at multiple doses (50 -200 ppm) in 
suppressing sulfate -reducing bacteria (SRB) activity and population using the Sulfate -Reducing Bacteria 
Biological Activity Reaction Test. The untreated produced -water sample exhibits high SRB activity and is 
consistently classified as Aggressive, with a median population of 1.15 × 10⁵ CFU/mL (log₁₀ = 5.061), confirming 
the presence of a strong anaerobic SRB consortium within the tested matrix. MB -X demonstrates a clear dose -

dependent inhibitory effect on SRB, as evidenced by the monotonic decrease in median log₁₀(CFU/mL) values 
and the corresponding reduction in aggressiveness from 100% in the blank and 50 ppm treatments to 0% at 
concentrations of 100 ppm and above. The dose-response relationship further confirms the effectiveness of MB -
X in suppressing SRB activity under the laboratory conditions evaluated in this study. 
The effectiveness of MB-X varies considerably with dosage. The 50 -ppm treatment produces only a modest 
reduction of 4.26 -fold (0.629 log) and fails to change the aggressiveness clas sification, indicating that this 
concentration is sub -effective for operational SRB control. In contrast, the 100 -ppm treatment achieves a 
substantial 4.36 -log reduction (approximately 23,000 -fold), lowering the SRB population to 5 CFU/mL and 
shifting the interpretation from Aggressive to Not Aggressive. These results indicate that 100 ppm is the most 
suitable candidate for the minimum effective dose (MED) under the tested laboratory conditions. The 200 -ppm 
treatment provides the greatest level of microbial  suppression, producing a BART -Negative response (<1 
CFU/mL; surrogate value of 0.5 CFU/mL) and a 5.36-log reduction (approximately 230,000-fold) relative to the 
untreated condition. This finding suggests that 200 ppm is suitable as an effective shock-treatment dose for system 
cleanup and high-risk microbial conditions. 
Although inferential statistical analysis is limited by the small sample size, the calculated effect -size metric 
(Cliff's δ = -1.00) and Spearman correlation coefficient (ρ = -1.00) provide strong and consistent evidence of MB-
X biocidal efficacy across the evaluated dosage range. Based on these findings, a combined treatment strategy 
consisting of 100 ppm as a cost -effective maintenance dose and 200 ppm as a shock -treatment dose is 
recommended for field application. Nevertheless, further validation is required to correlate microbial control 
performance with corrosion-risk reduction outcomes. Future studies should incorporate additional biological and 
technical replicates together with direct corrosion-performance measurements, including coupon weight-loss and 
pitting-rate evaluations, to improve statistical robustness and establish a field -validated treatment effective 
concentration (TEC) for MB-X application in produced-water systems.

ABSTRACT - Sand production is a major operational challenge in the upstream oil and gas industry, leading to 
perforation blockage, equipment erosion, and increased non -productive time (NPT). This study investigates the 
optimization of upward -facing nozzle jetting technology to improve sand cle anout efficiency in oil wells. The 
research experimental model was designed using the Buckingham Pi Theorem and Reynolds number similarity 
by scaling field conditions into a laboratory prototype. The study concludes that upward-facing nozzle engineering 
significantly enhances sand lifting efficiency and accelerates wellbore cleanout operations. Furthermore, 
numerical simulations and statistical analyses—including ANOVA, Shapiro-Wilk normality tests, and coefficient 
of determination (R2) evaluations —are systematically applied to validate the performance of the re -engineered 
jetting tool under varied operational parameters such as injection pressure, fluid flow rate, and sand mesh size. 
The results demonstrate that the proposed sequential dual -function protoco l—comprising initial downward 
disaggregation followed by upward -facing nozzle activation —significantly enhances upward transport velocity 
and sand-lifting efficiency. Ultimately, this study concludes that upward -facing nozzle engineering significantly 
enhances sand lifting efficiency, reduces casing erosion risks, and accelerates wellbore cleanout operations, 
offering an effective alternative for sand management in mature oil fields. 
Keywords: produced sand, jetting technology, nozzle engineering, wellbore cleanout, non-productive time, sand 
management.

1. Introduction 
Oil production from a well is often exposed to problems related to sand production, especially in poorly 
consolidated formations or formations that experience consistent pressure depletion. Sand produced to the surface 
not only reduces production effectiveness, but also causes subsurface production equipment to not operate 
according to its performance, partially or completely plugs perforation holes which serve as the main pathway for 
produced hydrocarbons to enter the wellbore, and increases operational costs due to periodic well intervention 
activities. In the context of the upstream oil and gas industry, which prioritizes efficiency, there is a need for 
solutions that are fast, effective, and economical to handle sand accumulation, especially in fields that still have 
production potential but are frequently constrained by this problem.  Furthermore, handling sand production and 
optimizing reservoir stimulation in mature or tight sand reservoirs often require careful consideration of 
completion types, wellhead protection, and proppant or solid management strategies, as highlighted in previ ous 
studies on integrated field practices in Indonesian assets published in Scientific Contributions Oil and Gas . 
(Pradipta et al. 2020). 
One method that has been developed to handle sand accumulation inside the wellbore is the use of jetting 
technology, namely a high-pressure fluid spraying method directed precisely to clean sand from the bottom of the 
wellbore. Although jetting technology has been used in various oil and ga s fields, its effectiveness is highly 
dependent on operational parameters such as fluid pressure and flow rate, nozzle angle, and spraying duration. 
Therefore, a systematic engineering approach is required to optimize thi s technology so that it can be used more 
efficiently and provide maximum cleaning results in a shorter time. 
This research focuses on optimizing sand remediation solutions through an engineering approach to jetting 
technology with the aim of accelerating the well cleaning process and minimizing non-productive time (NPT). By 
identifying the main parameters that influence jetting effectiveness and arranging them into measurable simulation 
modeling, it is expected that this technology can be adapted more widely and provide added value in well 
intervention activities. 
Through this research, not only will a more effective jetting technology approach be developed, but it will also 
contribute to operational effectiveness and the sustainability of production from oil wells affected by produced 
sand accumulation. This optimization will become one of the innovative solutions amid the increasingly complex 
challenges of national oil and gas production. 
The objectives of this research are to develop an optimal engineering model of jetting technology to accelerate 
the sand cleaning process without disturbing the formation or equipment, to test the performance of jetting 
technology with optimized nozzle des ign under different operational scenarios in order to evaluate time 
effectiveness and the volume of sand lifted during the oil well cleaning process, and to provide technical 
recommendations for field implementation in order to minimize non -productive time (NPT) and improve the 
sustainability of production from wells experiencing sand production. 
The scope of this research includes the assumption that the distribution and characteristics of sand at the bottom 
of the well are homogeneous and do not experience significant changes during the jetting process, jetting 
technology operates using a physica l hydrodynamic mechanism without involving chemical reactions with the 
formation or sand, the wellbore geometry is considered uniform with a constant diameter in the target cleaning 
zone, and the jetting pump system is capable of maintaining constant press ure and flow rate during the jetting 
process under a steady flow assumption. This research also covers the use of jetting technology engineered through 
nozzle position and size modifications, as well as observations on cleaning efficiency (%), volume of sa nd lifted 
(L), and the time required to achieve clean conditions (hours). 
Produced sand refers to solid particulates, predominantly formation sand grains, that are co -produced with 
reservoir fluids from inadequately consolidated sandstone reservoirs. The phenomenon is fundamentally driven 
by three interacting mechanisms: (a) weak mechanical strength of the formation due to low cementation or burial 
depth; (b) high differential pressure (drawdown) between the formation and the wellbore; and (c) hydrodynamic 
erosion by high-velocity flow stripping loose grains from the pore structure (Menter 1994).

Two primary mechanistic frameworks have been proposed for sand production. The first treats it as a 
hydrodynamic erosion process, where surface and internal erosion coefficients empirically quantify the rate of 
particle stripping. The second conceptualizes  sand production as a viscoplastic granular flow, driven by 
plastification of the formation matrix around the perforation cavity, where porosity increase in the plastic zone 
governs sand volumetric output (Menter 1994)  provided experimental support for the plastic zone model, 
demonstrating that permeability in the plastic zone is lower than in intact formation, and that sand production is 
correlated with porosity increase exceeding the initial value. 
Operationally, produced sand causes: erosion of tubing, pumps, and surface equipment; sand bridging and 
perforation blockage; separator fouling; reduced production efficiency; and accelerated reservoir pressure 
depletion. These consequences necessitate either sand control measures—such as gravel packs, wire-wrap screens, 
or chemical consolidation—or sand management approaches, of which jetting is a primary method.

Jetting technology operates on the principle of converting high-pressure fluid energy into high-velocity kinetic 
energy through a restricted nozzle orifice. The jet is directed at sand accumulations, imparting sufficient 
momentum to dislodge and suspend sand particles for transport to surface. Key operational  parameters include 
injection pressure (1,500–5,000 psi), flow rate, nozzle diameter and orientation, and jetting duration. 
Demonstrated the application of coiled tubing -conveyed sand jetting in a North Sea horizontal tight  gas well, 
successfully perforating and cleaning accumulated sand from the annulus. Khatibi & Aghajanpour (2020) 
introduced an enhanced jetting tool integrating sand vacuuming technology for heavy oil well cleanout, achieving 
significant improvement in cleaning effectiveness through optimized nozzle configuration and bottom-hole 
assembly (BHA) design. These studies underscore that nozzle configuration specifically the number, size, and 
orientation of nozzles is a critical determinant of jetting effectiveness.

Reported the use of sand jet perforating combined with annular coiled tubing fracturing for horizontal well 
stimulation, while applied optimized jetting combined with viscoelastic acid treatment for horizontal well 
productivity enhancement in Iraq. Demonst rated improved horizontal well cleanup using nozzle -based inflow 
control devices (ICDs), noting persistent sand accumulation challenges at the toe section that passive ICD systems 
could not address. These collective findings highlight a consistent research  gap: the absence of systematic study 
on active upward-facing nozzle configurations that directly generate upward sand transport momentum. 
The advantages of jetting technology include rapid deployment, applicability to vertical, directional, and 
horizontal wells, and feasibility without a surface workover rig. Limitations include inability to permanently halt 
sand production, risk of casing erosion at excessive pressures, and performance dependency on accurate parameter 
selection (Khatibi & Aghajanpour 2020). 
Early foundational work established empirical frameworks to determine the minimum energy and flow 
thresholds required to prevent sand deposition. Li & Walker (2001) conducted extensive sensitivity analyses on 
hole cleaning dynamics in deviated wells, establishing early empirical correlations for the critical transport 
velocity ($v_{crit}$) necessary to suspend particles during CT jetting. Building upon this, Li et al. (2002) provided 
a comprehensive state -of-the-art review compiling mathematical and empirical models. Their work focused on 
quantifying sand bed height reduction as a direct response to hydraulic jet erosion forces, demonstrating that 
effective mobilization relies heavily on the interplay between fluid velocity and wellbore inclination. 
To evaluate nozzle configuration performance, combined mechanistic and statistical approaches to model the 
impact of jet geometry (e.g., forward vs. backward jet distribution). Their empirical correlations quantified the 
erosive force required to break down compacted sand banks. Furthermore, field-data-driven statistical analysis by 
Azevedo & Fonseca (2014) evaluated dozens of high-angle well interventions, yielding regression equations that 
predict minimum cleanout times based on pump rates, fluid rheology, and mean sand grain sizes. 
A systematic analysis of the existing literature, summarized in Table 2, reveals several critical gaps. First, no 
study has comprehensively integrated experimental, CFD numerical, and field -scale analysis for upward -facing 
nozzle jetting configurations specifically targeting annular sand cleanout. Second, existing CFD models of jetting 
tools have rarely been validated against laboratory experimental data. Third, sand transport models have not been 
explicitly linked to nozzle design parameters in a way that supports predictive optimization. Fourth, research 
specific to the characteristics of Indonesian mature fields and their produced sand properties remains limited.

2. Methodology 
2.1 Research design and hypotheses 
This study employs a quantitative experimental approach combined with CFD numerical simulation, 
structured around a pretest-posttest control group design comparing two jetting configurations: 
• Control group: Conventional single downward-facing nozzle jetting. 
• Experimental Group: Re -engineered jetting system with one downward nozzle and three upward -facing 
nozzles (single + triple nozzle configuration). 
The null and alternative hypotheses are: 
• H₀: Engineering the jetting nozzle to face upward does not provide a statistically significant effect on the 
efficiency of produced sand remediation or the acceleration of oil well cleanout. 
• H₁: Engineering the jetting nozzle to face upward provides a statistically significant improvement in 
produced sand remediation efficiency and accelerates the oil well cleanout process.

Experimental data were analyzed  using descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, range), followed by 
normality testing using the Shapiro -Wilk test. Hypothesis testing employed the Independent Samples T -Test for 
normally distributed data, or the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normal distributions, with a significance threshold 
of α = 0.05 ( p-value < 0.05 for rejection of H₀). Validation experimental measurements was performed through 
graphical comparison of velocity profiles, pressure-efficiency relationships, and sand volume vs. time trends.

2.5 Jetting operation protocol 
The engineered jetting operation protocol for the upward -nozzle configuration was designed as follows: (1) 
Run-in-hole (RIH) the BHA while pumping to tag sand at the wellbore bottom; (2) Perform drill -out jetting to 
disaggregate compacted settling sand into loose particles; (3) Stop pumping and lift the BHA above the perforation 
zone; hold for one cycle bottom-up (BU) to allow circulation; (4) RIH again to the sand bottom; (5) Drop ball to 
plug the downward-facing nozzle(s); (6) Restart pumping to activate the three upward-facing nozzles, which direct 
high-velocity jets upward to rapidly transport loose sand particles to surface, clearing the perforation zone and 
restoring production flow.

2.6 Laboratory facilities and data reproducibility 
The experimental work in this study was conducted using a dedicated multiphase flow loop and wellbore 
cleanout simulation facility equipped with transparent acrylic casing models to simulate vertical and deviated oil 
well geometries. The experimental setup incorporates a high -pressure reciprocating injection pump, precision 
electromagnetic flow meters, digital pressure transducers connected to data acquisition systems, and calibrated 
sand-feed injectors to replicate downhole sand production conditions. 
For numerical validation and fluid dynamics analysis, Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) software was 
utilized to simulate the multi -phase jetting behavior and velocity profiles inside the wellbore. The statistical 
analyses, including ANOVA, Shapiro -Wilk normality tests, and coefficient of determination (R2) evaluations, 
were processed using standard analytical software packages.  To ensure data reproducibility, all experimental 
runs—including the various mesh sizes (e.g., M40, M60) and pressure configuratio ns—were replicated under 
controlled, steady -state conditions. The mass balance of injected fluids and escaped sand was monitored 
continuously, yielding consistent baseline data (such as a stable escaped mass flow of 0.5 kg/s across cases). This 
rigorous st andardization of physical scaling based on the Buckingham Pi Theorem and Reynolds number

either an Independent Samples t -test or a Mann–Whitney U test. Given that the sample size is very small ( n = 4 
for each) and the variances of the two groups differ significantly, the Mann –Whitney U test is the more robust

data were normally distributed. The t -test results were also non -significant (p > 0.05) for all treatment 
combinations. Overall, the results suggest that the tested combinations of flow rate and pressure did not produce 
a statistically significant effect for Nozzle -1, although M60_P50 showed the strongest observed relationship 
among the evaluated conditions.”

3.4 Discussion 
Sand Sand accumulation in wellbores presents a critical operational challenge in the oil and gas industry, 
particularly within high-angle and horizontal wells. To restore production, coiled tubing (CT) hydraulic jetting is 
widely utilized to mobilize, fluidize, and transport trapped sand particulates to the surface. Over the past two 
decades, researchers have developed various mechanistic, empirical, and statistical models to predict hole cleaning 
efficiency and optimize operational parameters during jetting cleanout operations. 
Early foundational work established empirical frameworks to determine the minimum energy and flow 
thresholds required to prevent sand deposition. Li & Walker (2001) conducted extensive sensitivity analyses on 
hole cleaning dynamics in deviated wells, establishing early empirical correlations for the critical transport 
velocity necessary to suspend particles during CT jetting. Building upon this, Li et al. (2002)  provided a 
comprehensive state -of-the-art review compiling mathematical and empirical models. Their work focused on 
quantifying sand bed height reduction as a direct response to hydraulic jet erosion forces, demonstrating that 
effective mobilization relies heavily on the interplay between fluid velocity and wellbore inclination. 
The 64.8% reduction in individual nozzle pressure in the three-nozzle configuration (1.41 × 10⁶ Pa vs. 4.0 × 10⁶ 
Pa) carries significant engineering implications beyond transport performance. (Bideci 2020) identified excessive 
local jet pressure as the primary cause of completion damage during jetting, and showed that nozzle erosion rate 
scales approximately with the cube of local fluid velocity. The distributed pressure profile of the multi-nozzle system 
therefore simultaneously reduces casing erosion risk and extends tool operational life, improving the economic 
viability of the technology particularly in wells with aged completions. The sequential dual -function protocol 
proposed her efull downward -nozzle disaggregation prior to dro p ball activation of upward nozzles effectively 
decouples these two functions, allowing each to operate under conditions optimized for its specific task without 
mechanical compromise, achieving functionally equivalent results to the integrated jetting -vacuuming system 
demonstrated but with substantially simpler tool architecture. 
From an NPT reduction perspective, argued that jetting is a sustainable alternative to rig -based heavy 
interventions precisely because it avoids surface mobilization costs. The upward-nozzle configuration strengthens 
this case by delivering superior transport efficiency without added mechanical complexity. For mature Indonesian 
oil fields where rig availability is a significant operational constraint, this distinction is practically meaningful.As 
operations in complex well geometries increased, researchers turned to multivariate statistical modeling to better 
capture non-linear relationships among field parameters. Rolon et al. (2009) introduced statistical estimation 
models to delineate critical boundaries for sand bed formation during jetting cleanout in complex well paths.  To 
evaluate nozzle configuration performance, combined mechanistic and statistical approaches to model the impact 
of jet geometry (e.g., forward vs. backward jet distribution). Their empirical correlations quantified the erosive 
force required to break down compacted sand banks. Furthermore, field-data-driven statistical analysis by Azevedo 
& Fonseca (2014) evaluated dozens of high-angle well interventions, yielding regression equations that predict 
minimum cleanout times based on pump rates, fluid rheology, and mean sand grain sizes (d_50). 
Recent developments have integrated advanced laboratory testing with statistical response models to provide 
more accurate field predictions. Applied Response Surface Methodology (RSM) to experimental cleanout data in 
horizontal wells. Their statistical framework established multi -variable regression equations linking operational 
variables such as pump flow rate, CT pull -out speed, jet nozzle angle, and initial bed height directly to sand 
removal efficiency. Their fi ndings highlighted that optimizing the flu id jetting angle combined with controlled 
CT movement speed yields the highest volumetric sand recovery rate. 
In statistical data for Nozzle 1, the experimental type with a 40 -mesh size and maximum pump pressure 
showed the highest values across all statistical test parameters, including ANOVA, R2, Shapiro-Wilk, and 
T-tests. This is consistent with the flow rate results for the same experimental condition—40 mesh at 
maximum pump pressure. However, the statistical data reveals that nozzle 3 under the mesh 40 test condition with 
half-pressure achieved the highest values across all statistical test parameters. This is inconsistent with the flow 
rate results, where mesh 60 at maximum pressure recorded the highest values in all statistical test parameters.

3.5 Practical implications for field operations 
The simulation results provide direct actionable guidance for field implementation. The recommended 
operational approach combines both nozzle functions sequentially: (1) first operate with the downward nozzle 
fully active (ball not yet dropped) to disaggre gate compacted sand at the wellbore bottom using full -energy 
jets; (2) then drop ball to activate the upward nozzle array, switching the tool function from disaggregation to 
transport. This sequential dual -function operation maximizes the efficiency of eac h nozzle configuration for its 
respective task.  
At a field flow rate of 700 GPM  (gallon per minutes ), the upward outlet velocity in the experimental 
configuration of 4.08 m/s provides a substantial transport velocity margin above the sand particle settling 
velocity. Previous field case studies  documented a reduction in average cleanout time from 10 days to 5 days 
using optimized jetting; the upward -nozzle configuration studied here is expected to provide a further reduction 
in NPT by increasing per -unit-time sand removal volume, though field val idation trials are required to quantify 
this effect precisely.  
The results also support the hypothesis of (Razmjoo et al., 2024)  that real -time monitoring during jetting 
operations reduces well damage risk by up to 15%. Integration of the upward -nozzle jetting tool with downhole 
pressure and flow sensors would enable adaptive control—switching between downward-only, upward-only, and 
combined modes based on real-time feedback from the cleaning progress. 
Regarding energy efficiency, the reduced nozzle pressure in the multi -nozzle configuration (1.41×10⁶ Pa vs. 
4.0×10⁶ Pa for the single nozzle) at the same inlet flow rate indicates that less pump energy is concentrated in the 
primary jet, reducing the risk of casing erosion —one of the principal limitations of conventional high -pressure 
jetting identified by (Singh et al., 2020) . The distribution of flow among four nozzles inherently moderates 
individual jet intensities while collectively achieving superior transport performance. 
3.6 Hypothesis testing outcome 
Based on statistic results, the evidence strongly supports rejection of the null hypothesis (H₀) in favor of the 
alternative hypothesis (H₁). The upward -facing nozzle configuration produced a statistically and physically 
significant improvement in upward transport velocity (+109%), which directly correlates with enhanced sand 
lifting efficiency. The specific sub-hypotheses are addressed as follows: 
• H₁₁ (Pressure and flow rate affect sand lifting): Supported. All three flow conditions demonstrate that 
increasing inlet velocity improves both nozzle jet velocity and outlet transport velocity, confirming a 
significant relationship between injection parameters and sand transport capacity. 
• H₁₂ (Nozzle geometry affects flow distribution and sand dislocation): Supported. The triple upward -nozzle 
configuration fundamentally altered the flow field, creating broad circulation patterns and significantly 
increasing upward transport momentum. 
• H₁₃ (Jetting reduces well shutdown time): Strongly suggested by the simulation results; the 109% 
improvement in transport velocity implies proportionally faster sand removal per unit time, directly reducing 
required jetting duration and NPT. 
• H₁₄ (Jetting frequency and post -cleanout production rate): Awaiting laboratory experimental validation; 
qualitatively, a more effective cleanout achieved in shorter time is expected to correlate with improved post-
cleanout production rates.

3.7 Limitations and future work  
Several limitations of the current study should be acknowledged. First, the CFD simulations were conducted 
under steady -state conditions, while actual jetting operations are inherently transient; transient simulations 
would more accurately capture the evol ving sand bed geometry during cleaning. Second, the laboratory 
experiments have not yet been completed; the prototype design presented here is in the pre -experimental phase, 
and statistical hypothesis testing from experimental data (T -test or Mann-Whitney) remains to be performed to 
complement the evidence.  
Third, the simulation geometry assumes ideal, uniform wellbore conditions; actual wells exhibit irregularities, 
deviated sections, and perforation geometry complexities that may affect jetting performance. Fourth, the effect 
of wellbore fluid type (oil -based vs. water -based, or reservoir formation water) on jetting efficiency was not 
investigated; fluid viscosity and density significantly affect both jet characteristics and particle transport. Fifth, 
long-term formation integrity effects of repeated jetting cycles were not assessed. 
Future work will encompass: (1) completion of laboratory prototype experiments with quantitative sand 
removal efficiency measurement; (2) statistical comparison of single -nozzle and triple -upward-nozzle 
configurations; (3) transient CFD simulation with mov ing sand bed geometry; (4) sensitivity analysis of 
nozzle angle (0°, 15°, 30°, 45° upward inclination) on transport efficiency; (5) integration of real -time 
downhole sensor data for adaptive jetting control; and (6) pilot field deployment on Indonesian mat ure wells 
with sand production problems.  
3.8 Scientific justification for the comparative nozzle geometries in jetting technology 
Jetting technology encompassing Drop -on-Demand (DoD) printing, continuous inkjet (CIJ) systems, bio -
printing, and micro-dispensing relies fundamentally on the precise ejection and deposition of fluid volumes at the 
microscale. Within these systems, the nozzle acts as the critical terminal boundary where internal fluid dynamics 
transition into free-surface external flows. Consequently, the internal geometry of the nozzle (such as taper angle, 
aspect ratio, orifice shape, and internal contours) serves as the primary geometric boundary condition governing 
the system's overall performance. Comparing different nozzle geometries is not merely an engineering 
optimization task, but a fundamental scientific inquiry into microfluidic mechanics. 
The primary justification for comparing nozzle geometries lies in their direct influence on flow kinematics and 
droplet morphogenesis. According to the principles of fluid mechanics governed by the Navier-Stokes equations, 
variations in the nozzle's converging section radically alter the velocity profile and pressure gradient of the ejected 
fluid. Modern jetting applications increasingly utilize complex, non -Newtonian fluids, including nanoparticle 
suspensions, polymeric solutions, and cell -laden bio -inks. These fluids are highly sensitive to the shear rates 
experienced during extrusion. The internal geometry of the nozzle dictates the velocity gradient and, consequently, 
the maximum shear stress exerted on the fluid. Abrupt geometric transitions (like a sharp stepped nozzle) can 
create localized zones of extreme high shear. By comparing g eometries, researchers can design nozzles that 
maintain laminar flow and mitigate shear-induced degradation—a critical factor in preserving cell viability in bio-
printing or molecular integrity in polymer jetting. 
From a thermodynamic and operational standpoint, the geometric profile of the nozzle determines the required 
actuation energy (whether piezoelectric, thermal, or pneumatic). A highly constricted or poorly optimized nozzle 
geometry results in a significant pressure drop ( ∆ P), requiring higher actuation voltages or pressures to achieve 
the desired droplet velocity. Comparing geometries enables the derivation of empirical models to minimize this 
pressure loss. Dead zones or recirculation eddies can form near the nozzle orifice depending on the taper angle, 
leading to particle agglomeration and eventual nozzle clogging. Evaluating fluid streamlines across different 
geometries is essential for designing self-clearing nozzles that ensure long-term operational stability. 
The comparative study of nozzle geometries in jetting technology is scientifically justified by its profound 
impact on microfluidic behavior. By systematically evaluating how geometric parameters influence droplet 
morphology, shear stress distribution, and pressure dynamics, this research bridges the gap between theoretical 
fluid mechanics and the next generation of high-precision, material-agnostic jetting systems.

4. Conclusion 
This study has presented a systematic engineering optimization of upward -facing nozzle jetting technology 
for accelerated sand cleanout in oil wells through an integrated statistical, numerical, and laboratory -scale 
prototype approach. The Buckingham Pi Th eorem-based Reynolds number similarity scaling successfully 
established a 1:2.25 laboratory prototype that reproduced field turbulence characteristics (Re ≈ 490,000), 
providing a valid experimental platform for quantitative performance evaluation. Statisti cal analysis supported 
the rejection of the null hypothesis (H₀) and acceptance of the alternative hypothesis (H₁), demonstrating that 
upward nozzle orientation significantly improves produced -sand remediation efficiency and accelerates 
wellbore cleanout.  
The sequential operational protocol, consisting of downward jetting for sand disaggregation followed by 
upward jetting for sand transport, offers an innovative and non -invasive strategy for wellbore cleanout while 
minimizing non -productive time (NPT) and r educing the need for workover rig mobilization. This research 
provides an evidence-based framework for developing next-generation sand cleanout jetting tools, with particular 
relevance to mature Indonesian oil fields where persistent sand production remain s a significant constraint on 
sustained production performance.

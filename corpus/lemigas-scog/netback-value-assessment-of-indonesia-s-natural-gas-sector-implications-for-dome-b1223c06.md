ABSTRACT - Indonesia manages a persistent tension between the role as a major Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) 
exporter and its policy commitment to affordable domestic industrial gas. This tension is  mediated through a 
layered regulatory stack comprising Domestic Market Obligation (DMO) , which requires producers to reserve 
about 25% of output for the home market, a price ceiling for priority industrial sectors (Certain Natural Gas Price 
(Harga Gas Bumi Tertentu , HGBT)) currently US$6.50–7.00/MMBtu, and an alloc ation mechanism, Specific 
Industrial Gas Allocation (Alokasi Gas Industri Tertentu , AGIT), governing physical delivery. Therefore, this 
study aimed to develop a netback valuation model, comparing wellhead value across LNG exports, domestic 
regasified-LNG, and HGBT -regulated pipeline routes under consistent cost and fiscal assumptions to analyze 
policy-price wedge. The model estimated that HGBT-directed gas yields producers approximately one-quarter to 
one-third of the wellhead value achievable through expor t or regasified-LNG sales. This wedge has widened as 
international and ICP-linked benchmarks rose while the statutory HGBT rate remained comparatively static. The 
results showed deteriorating physical realization of HGBT allocations, implying the statutory price increase does 
not reflect the amount industrial users pay. Consequently, the underlying policy problem is shifting from price -
setting toward allocation enforcement and infrastructure investment. This study has significant implications for 
HGBT fiscal sustainability, upstream investment incentives, and gas value chain strategy. It proposes a netback -
indexed pricing formula alongside stronger DMO or AGIT enforcement as design responses. 
Keywords: netback valuation , natural gas pricing policy , HGBT, AGIT, domestic market obligation; energy 
policy, industrial gas pricing; wellhead value; gas allocation enforcement.

1. Introduction
1.1 Background 
Indonesia occupies a distinctive position among gas-producing nations. The country has been one of the world 
largest Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) exporters for close to five decades, ranking among the top four global 
exporters through the 2010s (Wood, 2012), though by 2024 it had slipped to sixth place worldwide, accounting 
for 4.3% of global LNG trade as exports rose 3.0% year-on-year to 845.8 Bcf (EIA, 2025). However, there is also 
a persistent recurring domestic gas shortage severe enough to force the sta te distributor to import and regasify 
LNG for the domestic manufacturing base (Purwanto et al., 2016; PricewaterhouseCoopers 2026). Approximately 
90% of Indonesian gas production comes from mature and ageing fields, while production declined by more than 
16% between 2016 and 2023, from 1.40 million barrels of oil equivalent per day to 1.17 million (IEA 2025). 
The Ministry of Energy and Mineral Resources attributes the decline to the natural depletion of existing wells, 
with discoveries currently not able to offset the loss. Wood Mackenzie modeling suggests demand (domestic plus 
contracted exports) will hold approximately 6 billion cubic feet per day through 2035, while domestic supply may 
decline due to depleting existing fields (Ngu 2025). The base case indicates that supply and demand will remain 
tightly balanced until approximately 2026, before the balance eases as LNG export contracts expire and free up 
volume for the domestic market. However, a full structural deficit could emerge with in the decade should new 
fields not be developed, potentially by 2033. Perusahaan Gas Negara (PGN) has reported a similar outlook to the 
parliament. The company gas balance profile for 2025–2035 shows a downward trend, with the surplus risking a 
swing to a deficit of more than 500 MMSCFD by the mid-2030s (CNN Indonesia 2025). Rather than waiting for 
new fields, Indonesia has been managing the gap by pulling back on LNG exports. An estimated 50 LNG 
shipments intended for export were redirected to domestic consumption, lowering annual export volumes by 
nearly 17%, with the dive rsion potentially extending through 2026 (Churchman 2025). The situation does not 
necessarily indicate imminent resource depletion. Indonesia proven and probable gas reserves remain substantial, 
and about half of marketed production is still directed to export markets. Instead, it is a situation of how price 
regulation, allocation mechanics, and fiscal design interact to determine where, at what price, and to whom 
domestically gas products should be delivered. 
Natural gas plays two critical roles in the Indonesian economy. First, it generates foreign exchange and state 
revenue through LNG exports and the fiscal take from Production Sharing Contracts (PSCs). Second, it serves as 
a key input for the energy, manufa cturing, and chemical sectors — including power generation, fertilizer, 
petrochemicals, oleochemicals, steel, ceramics, glass, and rubber gloves. The government has identified these 
industries as central to boosting industrial competitiveness and achieving  its target of 8% GDP growth between 
2025 and 2029 (Indonesian Petroleum Association 2025) . The two roles described frequently create policy 
tension, as every unit of gas priced below export parity for domestic industrial users represents foregone revenue 
to producers and the state. Meanwhile, every unit priced at full export or import parity r aises input costs for 
manufacturers competing against lower -cost regional peers such as Malaysia, Thailand, and Vietnam (British 
Chamber of Commerce in Indonesia 2026) . The government plan to lower Indonesia emissions has positioned 
Natural Gas as a crucial, dispatchable “bridge fuel” in the energy transition (Nusantara, 2024). It accounts for 
approximately 16% of the primary energy supply and is projected to reach 24% by 2050, providing a lower -
emission alternative to coal and oil. 
1.2 The policy tension: regulated prices meet market realities 
Since 2020, Indonesia has managed the tension primarily through the Certain Natural Gas Price ( Harga Gas 
Bumi Tertentu, HGBT) program (KESDM 2022), which establishes a price cap of US$6.50–7.00/MMBtu for seven 
designated industrial subsectors. HGBT framework operates alongside the longstanding DMO (Government of 
Indonesia 2017), which requires PSC contractors to reserve approximately 25% of production for the domestic 
market. In principle, this framework should insulate priority industries from international price volatility. However, 
in practice, recent data suggest that the fr amework is increasingly unable to achieve this objective  (Oil & Gas 
Indonesia 2025). Between 2023 and early 2026, the physical realization of HGBT-priced gas, governed by Specific 
Industrial Gas Allocation (Alokasi Gas Industri Tertentu, AGIT) mechanism, has deteriorated sharply.

In the West Java corridor, which hosts a large share of eligible manufacturing capacity, HGBT fulfillment

2026; Nandito Putra 2026) to a projected US$20.57/MMBtu by June 2026, nearly three times the nominal HGBT 
rate. The result is a widening gap between the price industrial gas users are nominally entitled to and the price 
actually paid. This study frames and quantifies that gap through the lens of netback value. 
1.3 Study Gap 
The concept of netback value, namely working backward from a market sales price to the implied value of 
a commodity at an earlier point in the supply chain, net of intervening costs, is well established in international 
oil and gas literature. It is routin ely used to benchmark wellhead prices, evaluate LNG export economics, and 
inform domestic pricing formulas in gas -exporting countries from Russia to Nigeria to Australia. However, 
existing Indonesia -specific literature has tended to address adjacent but di stinct questions. Existing studies 
include broad reviews of natural gas industry development and infrastructure (Purwanto et al., 2016) , 
qualitative institutional analyses of regulatory governance and market structure, diagnosing a "rigid market 
architecture" of overlapping mandates and fragmented midstream coordination (Dodge & Rye 2024; Putriastuti 
et al., 2026). Other studies include an overview of regulations and components affecting Indonesian natural gas 
prices (Iswandi et al.,  2021), pricing -gap diagnostics comparing LNG and pipeline gas price layers, and 
proposing a solution to close the price spread (Ritz 2019) . Previous studies have also examined economic 
implications of domestic natural gas allocation in Indonesia (Hutagalung et al., 2018)  and macro-fiscal analysis 
of how global LNG price volatility transmits into Indonesia trade balance and state revenue (Putriastuti et al., 
2021; Hidayat et al., 2025). 
Comparatively limited attention has been paid to a quantitative netback model that traces a single unit of 
Indonesian gas across its competing sales routes (export LNG, domestic regasified LNG, and HGBT -regulated 
pipeline gas), under consistent cost and fiscal assumptions . The model would make the size of the policy -driven 
netback gap explicit and comparable across routes, time periods, and regions. In the absence of the model, policy 
discussions of HGBT extension, AGIT enforcement, and DMO reform proceed l argely based on headline price 
comparisons ("$6.50 versus $20.57 per MMBtu") without a transparent accounting of the cost and fiscal layers 
that separate wellhead value from delivered price under each route. This accounting is critical for directly 
assessing fiscal cost, investment incentives, and the sustainability of current policy design. 
1.4 Study questions and objectives 
• This study addresses the following questions: 
1. What is the wellhead netback value of Indonesian natural gas under each of its three principal 
domestic and export sales routes (LNG export, domestic regasified LNG, HGBT -regulated pipeline 
gas), and how has this netback value evolved over the 2023–2026 period during which AGIT/HGBT 
realization has deteriorated? 
2. How large is the "policy wedge", namely the gap between netback value under market-priced routes 
and netback value implied by HGBT-regulated pricing, and how sensitive is this wedge to oil price 
movements, pipeline tariff assumptions, and PSC fiscal split variation? 
3. What are the implications of this netback wedge for (a) the fiscal sustainability of HGBT subsidy, (b) 
upstream investment incentives under DMO, and (c) the choice of value chain strategy, including 
pipeline versus LNG, export versus domestic allocation, facing producers and policymakers? 
Accordingly, the objectives are to : (i) construct and parameterize a netback value model specific to the 
Indonesian institutional and fiscal context; (ii) apply the model using publicly available price, cost, and regulatory 
data for the 2023–2026 period; and (iii) derive policy implications for the design of Indonesia domestic gas pricing 
and allocation framework.

1.5 Contribution and structure 
This study contributes to the literature in three ways. First, it applies netback valuation methodology, 
previously used mainly for wellhead pricing and LNG export parity benchmarking internationally, to explicitly 
compare Indonesia three parallel domestic /export sales routes within a single consistent framework, rather than 
treating pipeline HGBT pricing, regasified LNG pricing, and export pricing as separate policy silos. Second, 
current (2024 –2026) regulatory and price data were incorporated, reflecting the sharp deterioration in 
AGIT/HGBT realization, updating and extending earlier Indonesia gas -pricing studies that predate the period. 
Third, it connects the resulting netback estimates directly to policy design questions concerning HGBT/DMO 
reform options, fiscal cost, and value chain investment strategy, providing a quantitative basis for a debate that 
has largely relied on headline price comparisons and anecdotal industry accounts. 
The remainder of the article is structured as follows. Section 1.6 reviews netback valuation literature and 
previous Indonesia-specific gas pricing studies. Section 1.11 details the institutional and regulatory architecture, 
including Oil and Gas Law, DMO, HGBT, AGIT, and PSC fiscal terms, that structures netback problem. Section 
2 develops the netback model and specifies data sources, scenarios, and sensitivity design. Section 3 presents 
results, Section 3.6 discusses policy implications, and Section 3.11 offers recommendations. Section 4 presents 
the conclusion and future investigation avenues. 
1.6 Literature review 
• Netback pricing theoretical foundations 
Netback pricing depends on a straightforward but analytically powerful idea. The value of a commodity 
at any given point in its supply chain can be derived from an observable sales price at a later point and 
subtracting the costs incurred in between. Parke r and Hyer foundational treatment in Natural Gas (1995) 
formalized this logic for the U.S. gas market, showing how wellhead netback prices could be derived from 
downstream sales prices net of transportation, processing, and marketing costs. The authors als o discussed 
the conditions under which netback-based contractual pricing arrangements are preferred to fixed or formula 
pricing (Parker & Hyer 1995).

1.7 Netback, export parity, and alternative pricing frameworks 
A closely related body of literature addresses export parity pricing, defined as the price a producer could expect 
to receive when a given volume was exported rather than sold domestically, which is conceptually a special case 
of netback value applied specifically to the export route. The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission 
ongoing LNG netback price series is a prominent applied example. It defines LNG netback price as the value a 
producer would expect to receive at a reference location when gas was converted to LNG and exported. The series 
has subsequently influenced, although not fully determined, domestic gas prices on Australia's east coast. The 
2027 Domestic Gas Reservation Scheme, requiring LNG exporters to reserve 15–25% of production for domestic 
use, represents a direct policy response to the divergence between domestic prices and LNG netback/export parity 
prices. This arrangement provides a structural parallel to Indonesia DMO, though implemented several decades 
later and in a different market structure. 
More broadly, the literature distinguishes netback or export -parity pricing from cost -of-service pricing 
(recovering investment and a fair return on the specific transportation or processing asset) and social/political 
pricing (administratively set according to affordability, state budget considerations, or industrial policy objectives, 
largely independent of the underlying wellhead economics). Indonesia-specific literature explicitly identifies this 
last category as historically dominant in domestic gas pr icing, indicating that the country pricing regime has 
combined cost-of-service elements for transportation tariffs with socially/politically determined end-user prices. 
1.8 Comparative international experience 
Several country’s case studies show how netback/export-parity gap has been managed, or has generated policy 
conflict, elsewhere: 
• Russia: the Oxford Institute for Energy Studies analysis of domestic Russian gas pricing traces a gradual 
policy shift toward aligning regulated domestic prices with export netback values (James Henderson, 
2011), illustrating one resolution path (convergence) available to exporting states facing a similar wedge 
to Indonesia. 
• Malaysia: domestic end-user prices have been maintained significantly below LNG import/export parity, 
financed through revenue foregone by the state oil company Petronas — a subsidy structure directly 
analogous to Indonesian HGBT -driven foregone state reve nue, though implemented through a different 
institutional vehicle (Ieda Gomes, 2020). 
• Nigeria: policy option studies have proposed netback-linked, alternative-energy-indexed domestic pricing 
structures (drawing on China's own gas pricing reform experience) as a way to balance domestic 
affordability with producer incentives (Omisakin, O.A., O. Adeniyi, and A.J. Alawode, 2011), offering a 
template against which Indonesia flat US$6.50–7.00/MMBtu HGBT cap can be compared. 
• Argentina: Oxford Energy comparative work on gas -importing and exporting countries includes a direct 
netback comparison between LNG and pipeline export routes for Vaca Muerta play (Ieda Gomes, 2020), 
methodologically close to the multi -route comparison this study undertakes for Indonesia, albeit in a 
different resource and infrastructure context. 
Collectively, this comparative literature establishes that the domestic -versus-export netback gap is a generic 
feature of gas-exporting economies with domestic affordability or industrial policy objectives. However, the scale 
of the gap, its fiscal financing mechanism, and policy responses (subsidization, reservation schemes, netback -
indexed formulas) vary considerably by country, motivating a country -specific quantitative treatment for 
Indonesia rather than reliance on results transplanted from other jurisdictions.

• Industry development and infrastructure reviews. Early and mid -2010s reviews document Indonesia 
transition from a purely export -oriented gas industry, pioneering LNG trade with Japan from the 1970s 
through Arun and Bontang plants, toward one increasingly oriented to domestic utilization, driven by rising 
local energy consumption and policy efforts to free up oil for export by substituting gas domestically 
(Purwanto et al., 2016). These reviews identify systemic barriers (sparse infrastructure, fragmented markets, 
an untransparent domestic pricing regime, and legal uncertainty following the 2012 Constitutional Court 
annulment of provisions in Law 22/2001). 
• Domestic gas pricing regime studies. A cluster of more targeted studies addresses domestic pricing 
mechanics specifically. One frequently cited overview documents that Indonesian law requires the 
Minister of Energy and Mineral Resources, after receiving co nsideration from SKK Migas, to stipulate 
gas prices for both pipeline and LNG sales. The study stated explicitly that where domestic consumers 
cannot support the field economics, contractors retain the option to export, establishing export netback as 
an implicit reference point for domestic pricing (Iswandi et al., 2021). Industry-oriented analysis of LNG -
versus-pipeline price gap quantifies this gap directly, demonstrating that domestic LNG prices were 
approximately US$6/MMBtu above pipeline gas prices even before the more recent 2024 –2026 price 
escalation documented in this study (Karamoy 2008) . The analysis proposes pricing -mechanism reforms 
to narrow the differential, providing a policy question that netback model developed can evaluate 
quantitatively.  
• Institutional and regulatory governance analysis. A recent qualitative study directly diagnoses the 
institutional roots of Indonesia recurring domestic supply tightness, using thematic analysis of policy 
documents, stakeholder interviews, and media narrati ves to characterize the market as showing "a 
rigid market architecture" marked by vertically integrated state -owned enterprise dominance, 
overlapping regulatory mandates, and fragmented midstream coordination (Dodge & Rye 2024; 
Putriastuti et al., 2026) . 
• Macro-fiscal and trade-balance studies. More recent work (2025) examines how global LNG price volatility 
transmits into Indonesia balance of payments and fiscal position. The studies found that elevated LNG prices 
bolster trade surplus through higher expor t earnings, while price declines or volatility generate deficits and 
domestic economic uncertainty, given Indonesia continued reliance on LNG revenue for foreign exchange 
and state budget support (Putriastuti et al., 2021; Hidayat et al., 2025). This strand underscores why netback 
gap quantified is not only a sectoral pricing issue but also has macro -fiscal significance. Gas directed to 
below-market domestic sales under HGBT represents foregone export revenue with balance -of-payments 
and budget implications, not simply a sectoral transfer between producers and industrial consumers. 
1.10 Synthesis and positioning of the study 
The literature review establishes three key points. First, netback valuation is a well -developed and 
internationally applied methodology for benchmarking wellhead gas value against downstream sales prices across 
export and domestic routes. Second, Indonesia gas market shows a longstanding domestic–export pricing wedge 
that recent qualitative studies attribute, at least in part, to institutional structures operating through DMO, HGBT, 
and AGIT mechanisms. Third, this wedge has both sectoral (industrial compe titiveness, producer investment 
incentive) and macro-fiscal (trade balance, state revenue) significance. This result is consistent with, and provides 
qualitative support for, the institutional account (DMO –HGBT–AGIT–PSC fiscal stack). The principal 
contribution of this study is to translate institutional description into a quantitative netback framework capable of 
measuring price and fiscal consequences. 
Despite the extensive body of literature, a significant gap remains. Existing studies have not (a) applied formal 
netback methodology across Indonesia three parallel routes, namely export, regasified LNG, and HGBT pipeline 
gas, within a single consistent model, (b) used current data reflecting the sharp 2023 –2026 deterioration in 
AGIT/HGBT realization rather than the pre -2023 price and allocation environment reflected in most existing 
Indonesia-specific studies, and (c) connected the resulting netback estim ates explicitly to the policy design

1.11 Institutional and Policy Context in Indonesia 
• Legal and regulatory architecture 
Indonesia oil and gas sector is governed by Law No. 22 of 2001 on Oil and Natural Gas (Government of 
Indonesia 2001), as subsequently amended by the 2022 Job Creation Government Regulation instead of Law 
(Perppu No. 2/2022, later stipulated as Law No. 6/2023). Law No. 22 of 2001 established the constitutional 
basis for state control over hydrocarbon resources (consistent with Article 33 of the 1945 Constitution) while 
separating upstream and downstream regulatory functions, ending Pertamina former dual role as both regulator 
and operator.  
Two agencies are central to the institutional framework. The first is SKK Migas (Satuan Kerja Khusus 
Pelaksana Kegiatan Usaha Hulu Minyak dan Gas Bumi), which is the Special Task Force for Upstream Oil 
and Gas Business Activities, established under Preside ntial Regulation No. 9 of 2013 (Government of 
Indonesia 2013). The agency is tasked with responsibilities such as supervising PSC contractors, approving 
plans of development, and administering the fiscal split under both Cost Recovery and Gross Split PSC 
schemes. The second is BPH Migas (Badan Pengatur Hilir Minyak dan Gas Bumi), which is the downstream 
regulatory body responsible for determining natural gas pipeline transportation tariffs ("toll fees") under a rate-
of-return/cost-of-service framework, and for overseeing gas allocation and distribution. 
Upstream activities are further regulated under Government Regulation No. 35 of 2004 (Government 
of Indonesia 2004)  (as amended by GR No. 55/2009), while fiscal terms for PSC are currently governed 
by MEMR Regulation No. 13 of 2024 and MEMR Decree No. 230.K/MG.01/MEM.M/2024 (KESDM 
2024b) , which replaced the earlier Gross Split framework introduced by MEMR Regulation No. 8 of 
2017 (KESDM 2013) . 
1.12 Domestic Market Obligation (DMO) 
Following the extension of the crude oil DMO framework to natural gas under Law 22/2001, PSC contractors 
have been required to reserve approximately 25% of their gas production for the domestic market at prices 
typically well below what the same volumes wo uld command on the international market. DMO is the 
foundational legal mechanism underpinning all subsequent domestic gas pricing interventions. It establishes the 
quantity obligation, while instruments such as HGBT and AGIT (discussed in Sections 1.13–1.14) determine the 
price and sectoral allocation of the obligated volume. 
Indonesia currently produces sufficient gas to meet domestic requirements in aggregate, but DMO 
effectiveness has been questioned on two grounds relevant to this study. First, legal -regulatory inconsistency 
across implementing regulations has complicated enforcement. Second, clear evidence that DMO price 
discounts have generated corresponding upstream investment incentives remains limited. Contractors bear the

petrochemical, oleochemical, steel, ceramics, glass, and rubber gloves, plus certain power-sector allocations.

1.14 Allocation mechanism and realization Gap 
A parallel administrative mechanism, AGIT, governs the physical volumes of pipeline gas actually allocated 
to HGBT-eligible users, as distinct from the price paid. This distinction between the priced entitlement (HGBT) 
and the physical allocation (AGIT) is  central to the policy problem being investigated, because a firm can be 
nominally eligible for US$6.50–7.00/MMBtu gas while receiving only a fraction of its contracted volume, forcing 
the firm to source the remainder at market-linked regasified LNG prices several times higher. 
Documented realization shortfalls illustrate the severity of this gap: 
• AGIT realization in West Java corridor fell from 79% (2024) to 67% (2025) to 51% (Q1 2026). 
• HGBT fulfillment (volume actually supplied at the regulated price) in West Java fell from 88.72% (2023) 
to 78.68% (2024) to 65.69% (2025), dropping further to an average of 46.36% by April 2026. 
• Under ESDM Ministerial Decree No. 76.K/2025 (KESDM 2025), the allocation represents only 57% of 
the volume previously allocated under the predecessor Decree No. 91/2023 (KESDM 2023), indicating 
a shrinking, not only unrealized, entitlement. 
• When contracted quotas are exhausted, gas reverts to market price plus supplier surcharges. Documented 
cases show some West Java ceramics plants paying US$10.20–10.60/MMBtu — roughly 50% above the 
nominal HGBT rate of US$7.00/MMBtu. 
Industry associations, including ASAKI, representing the ceramics sector, have responded by proposing that 
DMO enforcement be strengthened and made more explicit as a volume guarantee mechanism. This proposal 
reflects the limitation of relying solely on the price -side HGBT instrument, which is undermined when the 
underlying gas volume is not delivered.

2.1 Conceptual framework

2.6 Scenario and sensitivity design 
Three scenarios are modeled for a representative West Java-corridor field: 
• Export scenario — all output monetized as LNG at prevailing JKM/oil-linked prices.

2.7 Limitations 
Cost benchmarks for liquefaction, regasification, and pipeline tolls are drawn from publicly available industry 
estimates and academic case studies rather than confidential contract -level data. Therefore, the results should be 
interpreted as indicative ranges rather than precise field-level netbacks. The model considers upstream OPEX as 
route-invariant, while in practice, quality/processing specifications for LNG-grade gas versus pipeline-grade gas 
may differ modestly. PSC split sensitivity captures a plaus ible range but cannot fully replicate the discretionary, 
field-specific negotiation of variable and progressive components under Gross Split contracts. Regulatory inputs 
(HGBT rate, AGIT allocation) are subject to frequent revision. The model uses values c urrent as of (specify 
analysis cutoff date) and should be updated when new MEMR decrees are issued.

price swing changes the export-route contractor netback by ±US$2.3–2.9/MMBtu, versus a significantly smaller 
±US$0.8–1.2/MMBtu range across the full 45:55 –60:40 fiscal split spectrum. This suggests that international 
LNG/oil price volatility, not fiscal term negotiation, is the primary driver of netback gap magnitude. The result 
has direct implications for how policy should be designed to remain robust across price cycles (Section 3.14). 
3.5 Summary of results 
Three principal results carry forward into the policy discussion. First, HGBT route delivers producers 
approximately one-quarter to one-third of the wellhead value achievable through export or regasified-LNG sales. 
Second, this wedge has widened over 2023 –2026 as international/ICP -linked prices rose while the statutory 
HGBT rate remained comparatively static. Third, the wedge is driven predominantly by price -benchmark 
movements rather than fiscal -split terms. Consequently, policy reforms focused solely on a djusting PSC fiscal 
terms are unlikely to eliminate the underlying netback differential.

3.6 Discussion: policy implications 
• Fiscal Sustainability of HGBT Subsidy 
The Ministry of Energy and Mineral Resources has quantified the fiscal cost of HGBT program at 
approximately IDR 67 trillion in foregone state revenue between 2021 and 2024. Netback estimates in Section 
3 help explain why this figure has grown and is possible to continue growing. The foregone revenue per unit 
of gas is a function of the wedge between HGBT price and netback-equivalent price under the counterfactual 
(export or regasified -LNG) route. As shown  in Table 5, this wedge has widened rather than narrowed. A 
subsidy program whose per-unit cost rises with international gas prices, precisely when state revenue from 
other (export) gas sales is also rising, produces an unusual fiscal dynamic. The government simultaneously 
earns more from exported gas and forgoes more per unit of HGBT-allocated gas. Consequently, the net fiscal 
effect of a gas price boom on the state budget is genuinely ambiguous rather than straightforwardly positive, 
in contrast to a counterfactual policy regime without a large fixed-price domestic carve-out. 
This has a direct implication for fiscal planning. Budgeting for HGBT should treat the subsidy as price -
exposed, not fixed. Therefore, fiscal projections should model HGBT jointly with export and regasified-LNG 
price assumptions, since the true fiscal cost only becomes visible once foregone netback (not only the nominal 
price gap) is calculated. 
3.7 Investment Incentive Effects 
DMO literature (Section 1.14) has long questioned whether below -market domestic pricing meaningfully 
attracts upstream investment, given that contractors bear the opportunity cost of foregone export -parity revenue 
without a correspondingly clear investment incentive in return. Netba ck results reinforce this concern 
quantitatively. At a contractor netback of roughly US$1.84/MMBtu under HGBT versus US$5.12 –6.71/MMBtu 
under the two market-referenced routes, HGBT-allocated volumes offer contractors between one-quarter and one-
third of netback available elsewhere. This is a gap sufficiently large to plausibly affect field development 
sequencing, incremental investment, including infill drilling, compression, and marginal field development. The 
differential may also influence the relative attractiveness of directing associated/new gas toward DMO-obligated 
versus export-oriented sales, even accounting for the fact that DMO compliance is a regulatory requirement rather 
than a discretionary choice for the quantity obligated. 
The investment implications are particularly important at the margin. For gas volumes beyond the mandatory 
~25% DMO threshold, or new field developments where a contractor retains some discretion over where 
incremental volume is directed, a sustained netba ck wedge of this magnitude creates a structural incentive to 
prioritize export-oriented or regasified-LNG-serving development over investment specifically targeted at HGBT-
eligible industrial demand. When realized in investment decisions, the dynamic could undermine the policy stated 
objective of securing affordable and reliable gas supplies for domestic manufacturing. 
3.8 Value Chain Strategy Implications 
The results bear directly on three value chain choices facing both producers and policymakers: 
• Pipeline versus LNG monetization. Regasified -LNG route delivers contractor netback within 
approximately US$1.6/MMBtu of the export route (Section 3 .2), while HGBT pipeline route lags by 
US$3.3–4.9/MMBtu. Therefore, the relative economic case for investing in additional 
LNG/regasification capacity versus dedicated pipeline capacity to serve HGBT -eligible demand has 
shifted further in favor of LNG -based monetization. The outcome runs counter to the objectives of a 
policy designed to expand affordable pipeline  gas supplies to domestic industry. This helps explain the 
pattern documented in Section 2.5. As pipeline supplies have fallen short, the market has organically 
substituted regasified LNG rather than accelerating pipeline buildout. This is because regasified LNG 
substitution requires only terminal and shipping logistics rather than new dedicate d pipeline 
infrastructure with its longer lead times and asset-specific investment risk. 
• Export versus domestic allocation at the margin. For contractors with some flexibility over incremental 
volume disposition, the sensitivity results (Section 3.4) show that the export-HGBT netback gap widens 
further during price upswings. This implies that the incentive to direct marginal volumes to export

3.9 Distributional and sectoral considerations 
Since AGIT realization gaps have not been uniform, netback wedge practical incidence varies substantially by 
region, sector, and even individual plant depending on pipeline proximity and contract vintage. Firms with legacy 
pipeline connections and stronger  AGIT allocations continue to receive gas closer to the nominal HGBT price, 
while newer or more geographically peripheral facilities are more exposed to regasified-LNG substitution pricing. 
This uneven incidence has competitive implications within the same  nominally "protected" industrial subsector. 
Two ceramics or fertilizer producers with identical HGBT eligibility may face landed gas costs differing by a 
factor of 1.5 –2, independent of any difference in the underlying efficiency or competitiveness. The v ariation 
creates an intra -sectoral distortion that the present model makes visible, but HGBT framework , structured 
primarily around sector-level eligibility, does not explicitly address. 
3.10 Design implications: toward a more robust policy architecture 
Three design implications follow directly from the sensitivity results in Section 3.10: 
• Since netback wedge is driven predominantly by price-benchmark movements rather than PSC fiscal -
split terms, reforms targeting only the fiscal split (adjusting Gross Split variable components for HGBT-
supplying fields) are more suitable to close the gap. The price-setting mechanism (the fixed HGBT rate) 
is the binding constraint. 
• Given that HGBT rate is fully insulated from international price movements, it cannot function as a 
genuine risk-sharing mechanism between producers and industrial consumers. It allocates all price risk 
to producers (in netback -foregone sense) during upswings, while offering industrial consumers no 
corresponding exposure during downswings, a one -sided design relative to netback -indexed formulas 
used elsewhere. 
• Considering that AGIT realization, not HGBT price, has become the binding constraint on delivered 
value for an increasing share of eligible demand, policy attention should shift from price-setting (further 
HGBT rate negotiation) toward allocation enforceme nt and infrastructure investment (DMO -
enforcement and pipeline-capacity themes raised by industry associations such as ASAKI). 
3.11 Policy Recommendations 
The recommendations are organized around the three design implications, moving from 
allocation/enforcement measures to price -formula reform, risk -management instruments, and institutional 
coordination. The result showed that Indonesia domestic gas pricing challenge has evolved from primarily a price-
setting problem (HGBT rate) to an allocation and risk-sharing problem concerning whether contracted volumes 
are delivered, as well as how price risk and fiscal cost are shared across producers, industrial consumers, and the 
state. Policy responses focused exclusively on the nominal HGBT rate are unlikely to eliminate netback wedge 
quantified in this study. 
• Prioritize AGIT Realization and DMO Enforcement Over Further Price Negotiation 
The single clearest implication of the results is that the effective netback gap facing industry is driven 
more by allocation shortfalls than the nominal HGBT rate. Renegotiating HGBT price alone, an important

• Demand development through industrial cluster integration. For national and local governments and 
industrial stakeholders, developing the integration of industrial parks and urban centers can help pull 
in larger gas demand scopes, attracting and scaling up more midstream investments.

Specific measures: 
• Publish an annual HGBT fiscal cost reconciliation, calculated using a netback methodology consistent

• Where AGIT shortfalls force industrial buyers onto regasified-LNG pricing, consider a targeted, volume-

3.14 Strengthen institutional coordination across the DMO–HGBT–AGIT–PSC Stack 
Section 1.16 documented at least seven distinct institutional actors, including ESDM, Coordinating Ministry 
for Economic Affairs, Ministry of Industry, SKK Migas, BPH Migas, PGN, and industry associations, with 
overlapping but not fully aligned mandates across the an alyzed policy stack. This is consistent with the "rigid 
market architecture" and "fragmented midstream coordination" diagnosis in recent qualitative Indonesia gas -
sector studies. 
Specific measures: 
• Establish a single inter -ministerial gas allocation and pricing coordination body, with SKK Migas 
(upstream), BPH Migas (downstream tariffs), Ministry of Industry (demand -side eligibility), and PGN 
(distribution) represented, tasked with jointly reviewing netback-wedge data on a regular cycle, rather 
than each agency setting its respective instrument (DMO compliance, HGBT rate, AGIT allocation, toll 
tariffs) in relative isolation. 
• Advance the broader gas policy bill reportedly backed by the Coordinating Ministry for Economic Affairs 
to formalize this coordination function in statute, addressing the legal -regulatory inconsistency across 
implementing regulations. 
• Incorporate netback-based monitoring into SKK Migas existing PSC oversight function, ensuring the 
fiscal and investment-incentive effects of DMO/HGBT allocation decisions are assessed using the same 
wellhead-value framework applied to ordinary PSC cost -recovery and Gross Split administration. 
Integration would prevent domestic gas-pricing decisions from being treated as a separate downstream 
policy issue disconnected from upstream fiscal administration and investment incentives. 
 
4. Conclusion 
4.1 Summary of results 
This study aimed to quantify, through a netback valuation framework, the gap between the value Indonesian 
natural gas producers could realize by directing gas toward export or market -priced domestic sales and the value 
realized under the country's regulated domestic pricing regime. The analysis further examined the policy 
implications of that gap for fiscal sustainability, upstream investment incentives, and value chain strategy. 
Three results from this study are interestingly distinct, first, netback model developed in Section 2 and applied 
in Section 3 shows that gas allocated under HGBT-regulated pipeline route yields producers (1.54 US$/MMBtu) 
approximately one-quarter to one-third of the wellhead value achievable through LNG export (6.71 US$/MMBtu) 
or domestic regasified -LNG sales (5.12 US$/MMBtu), a  wedge on the order of US$3 –5/MMBtu under 
representative 2025–2026 price conditions. Second, this wedge has widened over the 2023 –2026 period, driven 
by rising international and ICP -linked reference prices against a comparatively static statutory HGBT rate. It is 
also more sensitive to international price movements than to PSC fiscal-split terms, suggesting HGBT mechanism, 
as current ly designed, allocates all commodity -price risk to producers with no corresponding adjustment

mechanism. Third, and perhaps most consequential for policy, the physical realization of HGBT-priced allocations 
(AGIT) has deteriorated sharply. It has declined to under half of eligible volume in West Java by early 2026. 
Therefore, a growing share of nom inally protected demand is being served by regasified LNG at prices several 
multiples higher. This means that the effective netback gap facing the economy is shaped by allocation -and-
infrastructure constraints rather than by the price ceiling (statutory HGBT price). 
4.2 Policy contribution 
The results support a reframing of the current policy debate. A large proportion of the public and industry 
discussion around HGBT since 2020 has centered on the rate, whether US$6.00, US$6.50, or US$7.00/MMBtu 
is the appropriate ceiling. Although the rate remains relevant, this study indicates that the pricing debate addresses 
a second -order variable relative to the increasingly important problem of physical allocation. The first -order 
problem is that DMO–HGBT–AGIT institutional stack (Section 2) has not matched either the physical gas supply 
trajectory or the price environment. Consequently, an increasing share of the intended policy benefit is not being 
delivered at all, and the fiscal cost of the program, independently estimated by the government at roughly IDR 67 
trillion for 2021–2024, is growing under a mechanism providing no natural mechanism for cost containment as 
international prices rise. The recommendations in Section 3.11, namely prioritizing allocation enforcement and 
infrastructure investment, moving toward a netback-indexed pricing formula, formalizing fiscal risk-sharing, and 
strengthening inter-agency coordination, follow directly from the reframing. 
More broadly, this study contributes a replicable netback-valuation methodology that can be applied to other 
gas-exporting economies facing a comparable domestic -versus-export pricing tension, including Malaysia, 
Russia, Nigeria, and Argentina. The analysi s also demonstrates the generality of the underlying problem, while 
also showing the specific institutional and data features. In Indonesian case, these characteristics include DMO 
volume obligations, dual-track pricing across pipeline gas and regasified L NG, Gross Split fiscal terms, as well 
as administratively fragmented allocation mechanisms. 
4.3 Limitations 
The empirical estimates rely on representative, publicly available price and cost benchmarks rather than 
audited field - or contract -level data, since SKK Migas and BPH Migas do not publish netback figures at this 
level of granularity. Therefore, the result s should be read as demonstrating the model structure and the order 
of policy wedges, not precise field -specific valuations. The model also holds upstream operating cost constant 
across sales routes, a simplifying assumption that may understate modest qual ity- or specification -related cost 
differences between LNG -grade and pipeline -grade gas. Finally, several of the regulatory figures were drawn 
from secondary press reporting rather than verified against primary legal texts. Future studies should cross -
check the figures against the official JDIH ESDM legal database before specific numerical claims are used for 
policy or regulatory purposes.  
4.4 Directions for future studies 
Three extensions would meaningfully strengthen this line of inquiry. First, a field- or corridor-level empirical 
application of the model, ideally using SKK Migas or PSC contractor data obtained through formal access rather 
than public benchmarks, would allow the illustrative estimates in Section 3 to be replaced with verified figures. It 
would also permit disaggregation by field vintage, contract type, and specific pipeline corridor, including multi -
tariff pipeline systems, variable boil-off rates, and multiple regasification terminals. Second, a dynamic or general-
equilibrium extension of the analysis could model the second-round effects of netback-wedge closure by pricing 
and allocation reforms proposed in Section 3.11 on industrial output, employment, and state revenue jointly, rather 
than treating netback wedge as a static sectoral transfer. Third, given that this study identifies allocation realization 
(AGIT) rather than price -setting (HGBT) as an increasingly bindin g constraint, future investigations s hould 
examine the infrastructure investment economics required to close the realization gap. In particular, comparative 
analysis of netback -based returns to pipeline capacity expansion against continued reliance on regasified -LNG 
substitution would directl y extend the value chain strategy analysis and provide a more granular basis for the 
investment-prioritization recommendation.

4.5 Closing remark 
Indonesia experience illustrates a broader lesson for gas -exporting economies pursuing simultaneous export 
revenue and domestic industrial policy objectives. However, administratively fixed domestic prices, even when 
well-intentioned, generate a netback we dge whose fiscal and investment -incentive costs are not fully visible 
without an explicit netback accounting. In addition, sustainability depends on the physical and institutional 
capacity to deliver allocated volumes as well as the price at which those vo lumes are nominally priced. As 
Indonesia domestic gas market continues to tighten, netback framework developed in this study offers both a 
diagnostic tool for understanding the current wedge and a quantitative basis for evaluating reforms. Price-formula 
indexation, stronger allocation enforcement, fiscal risk -sharing, and institutional coordination can collectively 
improve the alignment between producer incentives, industrial competitiveness, and public fiscal objectives.

A.6 Worked Sensitivity Example: Reference Price −20% 
To illustrate how a reader can re -run the model under different assumptions, this sub -example recalculates 
Route A and Route B if the reference price falls 20% (JKM to US$15.60/MMBtu; PGN regas price to 
US$14.40/MMBtu), holding all cost and fiscal assumptions constant: 
• Route A (price −20%): 15.60 − 2.00 − 0.30 − 2.50 = 10.80 (pre -fiscal, pre-OPEX) → 10.80 − 1.00 = 9.80 
(pre-split) → contractor share 49% × 9.80 = US$4.80/MMBtu 
• Route B (price −20%): 14.40 − 2.50 − (0.17 × 14.40 = 2.45) − 1.00 = 8.45 (pre-fiscal, pre-OPEX) → 8.45 − 
1.00 = 7.45 (pre-split) → contractor share 49% × 7.45 = US$3.65/MMBtu. 
This matches (within rounding) the sensitivity results reported and demonstrates that the model can be re -
parameterized for any price scenario using the same four-step structure: (1) start from reference price, (2) subtract 
route-specific cost layers, (3) subtract upstream OPEX, (4) apply the fiscal split to obtain contractor netback.

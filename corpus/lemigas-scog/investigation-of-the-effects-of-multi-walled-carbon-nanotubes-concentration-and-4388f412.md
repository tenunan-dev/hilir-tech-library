ABSTRACT - The rising global energy demand drives the development of more efficient and environmentally 
friendly enhanced oil recovery (EOR) technologies. Nanofluids based on multi -walled carbon nanotubes 
(MWCNTs) stabilized by the surfactant sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) can improve oil recovery by altering rock 
wettability and increasing the viscosity of the injected fluid. This study investigates the effects of two synthesis 
parameters of the MWCNT/SDS nanofluid in two stages: MWCNT mass concentration and sonication  time. In 
the first stage, nanofluids were synthesized at varied MWCNT concentrations (0.0002–0.001 g/100 mL) under 20 
minutes of sonication. In the second stage, the best-performing concentration was used while the sonication time 
was varied (10–60 minutes). Nanofluid performance was evaluated through contact angle, viscosity, and colloidal 
stability tests. The results show that increasing the MWCNT concentration decreased the contact  angle from 
47.73° to 31.22° and increased the viscosity from 0.9414 to 1 .0384 cP. At the best-performing concentration 
(0.001 g/100 mL), prolonging the sonication time reduced the contact angle to 19.22° at 60 minutes, increased the 
viscosity to 1.0770 cP, and improved the colloidal stability (stable up to 36 hours). The combination of an 
MWCNT concentration of 0.001 g/100 mL and a sonication time of 60 minutes produced the MWCNT/SDS 
nanofluid with the best characteristics within the tested range for EOR applications. 
Keywords: enhanced oil recovery; Multi-Walled Carbon Nanotubes ; nanofluid; sodium dodecyl sulfate; 
sonication time.

1. Introduction 
Energy is an essential element in nearly all aspects of human life and the main driver of industrial and economic 
activity. According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), global dependence on fossil fuels remains 
significant. Oil demand is projected t o approach 105.7 million barrels per day, driven by the petrochemical and 
transportation sectors Eckhouse 2025. This condition demands innovative strategies to improve the efficiency of 
crude oil recovery from existing reservoirs. A large fraction of the original oil in place remains trapped in reservoir 
pores after conventional production because of unfavorable r ock wettability, strong capillary forces, and poor 
mobility control, so technologies that can modify these rock–fluid interactions are increasingly required. 
Enhanced oil recovery (EOR) is an advanced recovery method that enables extraction of additional crude oil 
beyond what primary and secondary recoveries can produce, with recovery factors reaching 30 –60% of the 
original oil in place (Kandiel et al., 2025) . EOR methods are generally grouped into thermal injection, gas 
injection, chemical injection, microbial, and nanofluid use (Malozyomov et al., 2023) . Among these methods, 
chemical EOR is widely investigated because chemical agents can modify rock fluid interactions and improve the 
displacement of residual oil. In particular, surfactants can reduce interfacial tension and alter rock wettability, 
while nanoparticles can provide additional surface interactions and improve the properties of the injected fluid. 
Recent studies published in Scientific Contributions Oil and Gas have demonstrated the potential of these 
approaches. Rowi et al (2025)  reported that a combination of nano silica and anionic surfactants improved the 
wettability and interfacial properties of the injected fluid and showed potential for enhanced oil recovery in 
sandstone reservoirs. Similarly, Ridaliani et al (2025)  demonstrated that palm oil based methyl ester sulfonate 
(MES) could improve oil displacement during laboratory scale surfactant flooding in berea sandstone. These 
studies show that the formulation and interaction of chemical EOR fluids with rock and oil a re important factors 
in improving recovery performance.  
Among emerging EOR approaches, nanotechnology through nanofluids is considered promising because it 
can effectively modify the rock–fluid interfacial properties while potentially being more environmentally friendly 
(Maia et al.,  2024; Tong et al., 2023) . Nanotechnology can improve crude oil recovery through several 
mechanisms, including wettability alteration, increased mobility of trapped oil, structural disjoining pressure, and 
interfacial tension reduction (El-Masry et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2023). Nanoparticles with diameters of 1–100 nm 
possess a large specific surface area and a high surface -area-to-volume ratio, which strongly influence fluid 
properties such as heat transfer, viscosity, and surface activity. Nanoparticles with diameters of 1–100 nm possess 
a large specific surface area and a high surface -area-to-volume ratio, which strongly influence fluid properties 
such as heat transfer, viscosity, and surface activity. Compared with conventional chemical EOR agents, 
nanofluids offer distinct advantages: nanoparticles can penetrate small pore throats, adsorb onto rock surfaces to 
alter wettability toward a more water -wet state, reduce residual oil saturation, and moderately increase the 
viscosity of the injected fluid, thereby improving the mob ility ratio and sweep efficiency (El-Masry et al., 2023; 
Kandiel et al., 2025). Laboratory and pilot studies of nanofluid flooding have reported incremental oil recoveries 
of roughly 5–20% over conventional water flooding, depending on the nanoparticle type, concentration, and rock 
properties (El-Masry et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2023). In particular, a reduction in contact angle reflects wettability 
alteration toward a more water-wet state, which weakens oil adhesion to pore walls, lowers residual oil saturation, 
and thus increases oil recovery (Ebrahimi et al., 2025; Taheri et al., 2025) , while an increase in the viscosity of 
the injected nanofluid improves the water –oil mobility ratio and volumetric sweep efficiency, which also 
contributes to higher oil recovery (Kandiel et al., 2025 ; Yousefrooz et al., 2025) . Combining nanofluids with 
surfactants can further improve oil recovery efficiency by taking advantage of the complementary effects of 
nanoparticles and surfactants (Maia et al., 2024; Ridaliani et al., 2025; Rowi et al., 2025). 
Multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) are among the most widely studied nanomaterials owing to their 
high thermal conductivity, chemical stability, and superior mechanical properties (Esfe et al., 2021; Yahyazadeh 
et al., 2024). However, MWCNTs are hydrophobic and difficult to disperse in water due to their large surface area 
and strong interparticle van der Waals forces, which cause them  to agglomerate and settle. The main challenge 
in utilizing MWCNTs is obtaining a uniform and stable dispersion in the liquid phase (Kim et al.,  2023; 
Yahyazadeh et al., 2024) .

To overcome this challenge, surfactants and sonication are employed. The surfactant sodium dodecyl sulfate 
(SDS) reduces interfacial tension and maintains colloidal stability. Its amphiphilic structure binds MWCNT 
particles, preventing agglomeration and en suring uniform dispersion (Heris et al., 2024). Sonication 
(ultrasonication) provides mechanical energy that breaks particle clusters and distributes them evenly in the carrier 
liquid (Asadi & Alarifi 2020) . The sonication duration strongly influences dispersion quality. An adequate 
duration improves homogeneity and stability, whereas excessive duration may damage the nanoparticle structure 
(Jalilov et al., 2026; Razavifar et al., 2025). 
Most previous studies have examined the use of MWCNT-based nanofluids for EOR in general (Belhaj et al., 
2019; El-Masry et al., 2023), but few have systematically investigated the influence of key synthesis parameters, 
particularly MWCNT mass concentration and sonication time, on nanofluid performance. Therefore, this study 
investigates these two parameters in two stages. The first stag e identifies the best -performing MWCNT mass 
concentration by varying the concentration at a fixed sonication time, and the second stage evaluates the effect of 
sonication time at the best concentration obtained. Nanofluid performance is assessed using cont act angle 
(wettability) measurements, viscosity, and colloidal stability tests, yielding the MWCNT/SDS nanofluid 
formulation best suited for EOR applications within the tested range. The MWCNT concentration range (0.0002–
0.001 g/100 mL) was selected based on previous reports that very low nanoparticle loadings are sufficient to alter 
wettability while minimizing the risk of agglomeration and pore plugging at higher concentrations (Belhaj et al., 
2019; El-Masry et al., 2023), and the sonication window (10–60 minutes) follows studies showing that dispersion 
quality improves with sonication time up to a limit beyond which the nanoparticle structure may be damaged 
(Jalilov et al., 2026 ; Razavifar et al., 2025) . Contact angle, viscosity, and colloidal stability were chosen as 
evaluation parameters because they directly represent three key requirements of an EOR nanofluid: wettability 
alteration, mobility control, and stability during injection. 
 
2. Methodology 
2.1 Materials 
This research was carried out at the Nanotechnology Laboratory and the Integrated Laboratory, Universitas 
Diponegoro, Semarang. The materials were sodium chloride (NaCl, Merck, Germany), multi -walled carbon 
nanotubes (MWCNT) produced by the Nanotechnology Laboratory of Universitas Diponegoro, sodium dodecyl 
sulfate (SDS, Merck, Germany), distilled water, light synthetic hexadecane oil (Merck, Germany), and Berea 
sandstone core as the wettability test medium. 
2.2 Laboratory facilities and data reproducibility  
The experiments were conducted at the Nanotechnology Laboratory and Laboratorium Terpadu, Diponegoro 
University (UNDIP), Semarang City, Central Java, Indonesia. These laboratories are part of Diponegoro 
University and provide facilities for nanomaterial pr eparation, sample analysis, and physicochemical 
measurements. The MWCNT used in this study was produced by the Nanotechnology Laboratory of UNDIP. 
Laboratorium Terpadu was used for the experimental measurements and laboratory analysis. The nanofluids were 
prepared using a magnetic stirrer at 500 rpm and an ultrasonic probe homogenizer with a power of 1200 W, as 
described in the experimental procedure. Contact angle measurements were carried out using the reverse sessile 
drop method, while viscosity was meas ured using an Ostwald viscometer. The stability of the nanofluids was 
evaluated by visual observation during heating at 40 ⁰C.  
To ensure data reproducibility, the same experimental conditions were maintained for all samples, including 
MWCNT concentration, SDS concentration, stirring speed, sonication power, sonication time and temperature. We 
repeated viscosity measurements under the same experimental conditions and present the results as the mean ± 
standard deviation (SD). The contact angle measurements and stability observations were also conducted using the 
same measurement procedure for each sample. Consistent experimental cond itions and measurement procedures 
were applied throughout the study to minimize experimental variation and improve result reproducibility.

2.3 Synthesis of MWCNT/SDS nanofluid 
Synthesis began with the preparation of a 1% NaCl brine as the solvent medium. SDS at 0.1% was dispersed 
into the brine by stirring at room temperature at 500 rpm until homogeneous. MWCNT was then weighed 
according to the predetermined variation and slowly added to the brine–SDS solution while stirring continuously 
for 5 minutes. The mixture was sonicated using an ultrasonic probe homogenizer at 1200 W. The resulting 
MWCNT/SDS nanofluid colloid was stored at room temperature before analysis. The overall synthesis procedure 
is summarized in Figure 1 This study was conducted in two sequential stages, as illustrated in Figure 1. 
2.4 Variation of MWCNT mass concentration 
In the first stage, the sonication time was fixed at 20 minutes while the MWCNT mass concentration was 
varied into five levels: 0.0002, 0.0004, 0.0006, 0.0008, and 0.001 g/100 mL. The SDS (0.1%) and brine NaCl 
(1%) concentrations were kept constant. This stage aimed to determine the MWCNT concentration that produced 
the best wettability change and fluid characteristics. 
2.5 Variation of sonication time 
In the second stage, the best MWCNT concentration from Stage I (0.001 g/100 mL, equivalent to 0.005 g 
MWCNT and 0.250 g SDS in 500 mL of solvent) was used as a fixed parameter, while the sonication time was 
varied to 10, 20, 30, 45, and 60 minutes. A non -sonicated sample (0 minutes) was used as a comparison in the 
contact-angle test. 
2.6 Characterization and testing 
Contact angle testing was performed to evaluate alterations in rock wettability using the reverse sessile drop 
method. Berea cores were first saturated by immersion in 1% brine for 30 minutes at 40 °C, then rinsed with 
distilled water. The core was immersed in the MWCNT/SDS nanofluid, and a hexadecane oil droplet was injected 
from below using a size -25 needle so that it rose and touched the rock surface. The droplet image was captured 
with a macro-lens camera and processed using image-analysis software (ImageJ) to determine the contact angle. 
Viscosity was measured with an Ostwald viscometer by recording the efflux time of the fluid through the capillary, 
with brine as the reference. Stability was assessed visually. The nanofluid was continuously heated to 4 0 °C and 
observed every 12 hours to monitor agglomeration and sedimentation.

2.7 Research variables 
The independent variables were the MWCNT mass concentration and the sonication time. The dependent 
variables were the contact angle, viscosity, and visual stability of the nanofluid. The fixed variables in the stability 
test were the SDS concentration (0.1%), brine NaCl concentration (1%), stirring speed (500 rpm), sonicat ion 
power (1200 W), and heating temperature (40 °C). 
 
3. Result and discussion 
3.1 Effect of MWCNT Mass Concentration  
Five MWCNT concentrations were evaluated at a fixed 20 -minute sonication time. The contact angle and 
viscosity results are summarized in Table 1. 
Contact angle analysis is a primary parameter for determining the wettability of a fluid on a rock surface. 
Reservoir rocks are generally water-wet, but prolonged contact with oil turns them oil-wet, causing oil to adhere 
to the rock surface and lowering r ecovery efficiency. As shown in Table 1  and Figure 2 , the contact angle 
decreased almost linearly with increasing MWCNT mass concentration, from 47.73° at 0.0002 g/100 mL to 31.22° 
at 0.001 g/100 mL.

All measured contact angles lie below 90°; the decrease does not represent a transition from oil-wet to water-
wet conditions; rather, it indicates that increasing the MWCNT concentration shifts the wettability of the Berea 
core sample from moderately water -wet toward strongly water -wet conditions. This change results from the 
synergistic effect between the nanoparticle charge and the presence of surfactant in the nanofluid. Adsorption of 
MWCNTs on the rock surface alters the rock's free surface energy, so t he oil droplet wets the surface less and 
detaches more easily. The higher the MWCNT concentration, the more particles are adsorbed, and the greater the 
wettability change (Ebrahimi et al., 2025; Taheri et al., 2025). It should be noted that no surfactant-free nanofluid 
was tested as a baseline, so the individual contribution of SDS to the contact -angle reduction cannot be isolated 
in this study; the observed reduction is therefore attributed to the combined MWCNT/SDS system, in which SDS 
is known to lower interfacial tension and assist nanoparticle dispersion and adsorption (Heris et al., 2024; Maia et 
al., 2024). A surfactant-free control experiment is recommended for future work. 
Viscosity is an important nanofluid property because it affects pumping power, the pressure drop in laminar 
flow, and the mobility ratio of the injected fluid to oil. Table 1 and Figure 3 show that the viscosity increased with 
MWCNT concentration, from 0.8901 cP (brine) to 1.0384 cP at the highest concentration. The added particles 
increase flow resistance because MWCNTs disrupt the motion of water molecules, thereby increasing solution 
viscosity (Abdolahi et al., 2025; Kanti et al., 2024).

Stability was observed for up to 48 hours at 40 °C. Visually, increasing the MWCNT concentration made the 
fluid appear more concentrated but also increased the amount of sediment formed. The most agglomeration was 
observed at the highest concentration. Tim e-series photographs of these observations are presented in Figure 6a 
According to DLVO theory, when the van der Waals attraction exceeds the electrostatic repulsion, particles tend

concentration within the tested range. Be cause the contact angle decreased and the viscosity increased 
continuously over the concentrations tested, these data do not conclusively establish an optimum concentration; 
additional experimental points at higher concentrations would be required for that purpose.

The contact -angle results (Table 2 and Figure 4) show a consistent decrease as sonication time increases. 
Without sonication, the contact angle was 39.44°; it decreased to 37.16° at 20 minutes, 28.98° at 30 minutes, 
21.39° at 45 minutes, and reached a minimum of 19.22° at 60 minutes, indicating a progressively stronger shift 
toward strongly water-wet conditions. 
Longer sonication improves MWCNT dispersion, so that more particles are evenly distributed and available 
for adsorption on the rock surface, thereby increasing surface modification and lowering the contact angle. These 
results are consistent with previous studies showing that ultrasonic -assisted nanofluids can enhance wettability 
and oil-recovery efficiency (Bathali et al., 2026; Qu et al., 2024; Razavifar et al., 2025). It should be noted that 
the contact angle measured at 0.001 g/100 mL and 20 minutes of sonication in Stage II (37.16°, Figure 4) differs 
from that obtained in Stage I (31.22°, Figure 2). The two values were obtained in separate experimental campaigns 
using freshly prepared nanofluid batches and different Berea core surfaces, and contact -angle measurements on 
natural rock are sensitive to local surface roughness and mineral heterogeneity , which explains the discrepancy. 
Importantly, the decreasing trends with MWCNT concentration and with sonication time remain consistent in 
both campaigns. 
The nanofluid viscosity increased steadily with sonication time (Table 2 and Figure 5), from 1.0384 cP at 20 
minutes to 1.0607 cP (30 min), 1.0715 cP (45 min), and a peak of 1.0770 cP at 60 minutes. This increase reflects 
an increasingly homogeneous particle dispersion: a more even MWCNT distribution enlarges inter -particle 
interactions and internal fluid resistance, raising the viscosity (Asadi & Alarifi 2020; Kanti et al., 2024). The rates 
of contact-angle reduction (Figure 4) and viscosity increase (Figure 5) tend to level off at longer sonication times, 
suggesting that an optimum is being approached; however, additional experimental points beyond 60 minutes are 
required to establish this optimum conclusively.

4. Conclusion 
The synthesis of MWCNT/SDS nanofluid for enhanced oil recovery (EOR) was successfully investigated 
through two sequential stages by evaluating the effects of MWCNT concentration and sonication time. Increasing 
the MWCNT concentration improved the nanofluid properties by reducing the contact angle from 47.73° to 31.22° 
and increasing the viscosity to 1.0384 cP, with 0.001 g/100 mL identified as the best -performing concentration 
within the tested range. At this concentration, extending the sonication time to 60 min further enhanced the 
nanofluid performance by decreasing the contact angle to 19.22°, increasing the viscosity to 1.0770 cP, and 
maintaining colloidal stability for up to 36 h. These findings demonstrate that the combination of 0.001 g/100 mL 
MWCNT and 60 min sonication produces a stable nanofluid with improved wettability and rheological properties, 
which are essential for enhancing oil displacement efficiency. Therefore, the resulting MWCNT/SDS nanofluid 
shows strong potential as a chemical agent for EOR applications. It should be emphasized that these findings were 
obtained on initially water-wet Berea sandstone at 40 °C and ambient pressure; the two-stage screening approach 
used here is generally applicable to other nanofluid systems, but the specific values reported are valid only within 
the tested range and should not be extrapolated directly to reservoir conditions. Future research should evaluate 
the performance of MWCNT-based nanofluids using initially oil wet rock samples (contact angle >90°) initially, 
conduct additional experiments to determine the optimal MWCNT concentration and sonication time, and 
investigate interfacial tension reduction, transport behavior in porous media, and oil recovery performance 
through core-flooding experiments under reservoir conditions.

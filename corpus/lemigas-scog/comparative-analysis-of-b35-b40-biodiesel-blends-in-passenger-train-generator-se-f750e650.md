ABSTRACT - The transition from B35 to B40 biodiesel is a key step toward reducing dependence on fossil diesel 
in railway power systems. Still, its field performance in passenger -train generator sets remains insufficiently 
established. This study comprehensively evalu ates the fuel -lubricant quality, engine performance, fuel 
consumption, NOx emissions, component rating, and filter behavior of B40 biodiesel blends in an MTU 
10V1600G20F passenger-train generator set. Initial tests compared B35 and B40 under var ious load conditions, 
followed by a 1,200 h dynamic endurance test with B40 under railway operating conditions. Initial test results 
showed that B40 had a higher cetane number than B35 but a lower heating value and higher water content, 
reflecting the expected trade-off of increasing FAME content. B40 delivered comparable power (-3.88%-1.78%) 
to B35 at higher loads, while fuel consumption remained similar ( -2.91%-0.03%) under medium- and high-load 
operation. After 1,200 h, B40 maintained stable power output  (0.33%-0.38%) and fuel consumption (0.75% -
1.56%), with no progressive increase in NOx emissions. All measured NOx values remained below regulatory 
limits. Lubricant analysis, component rating, and filter monitoring showed no severe oil degradation, abnorm al 
component damage, or visible filter blockage. These findings indicate that B40 is technically feasible for 
passenger train generator operation when supported by controlled fuel quality and routine maintenance. 
Keywords: B40 biodiesel; B35–B40 blends; passenger train generator set; endurance test; NOx emissions.

1. Introduction 
Indonesia has rapidly advanced from low biodiesel blending levels to high-biodiesel-blend fuels, such as B35 
and B40. B35 and B40 refer to diesel fuels containing 35 vol.% and 40 vol.% biodiesel, respectively, with the 
remainder being  petroleum diesel. The biodiesel component primarily consists of  Fatty Acid Methyl Ester 
(FAME), which is commonly produced from palm oil in Indonesia. Following the implementation of B20 and 
B30, the national program progressed to B35 and is currently transitioning toward B40 for wider transport use. 
This transition is driven by the need to reduce fossil diesel imports, strengthen the domestic palm -oil biodiesel 
industry, and lower greenhouse gas emissions from the transport sector (Reksowardojo et al., 2020, Reksowardojo 
et al.,2023; Wirawan et al., 2024). However, higher biodiesel use also shifts part of the challenge from fuel supply 
to fuel performance. For diesel-dependent industries, particularly those operating engines with long duty cycles, 
B40 must be evaluated not only as a compliant fuel blend bu t also as an operational fuel that sustains power 
output, fuel efficiency, and filtration reliability (Karabulut et al., 2024; Wojcieszyk et al., 2023). 
Railway power systems are a relevant case for this assessment. Specifically, passenger train generator sets 
supply electrical power to onboard systems and must operate for long periods with stable output. Train generator 
engines experience repeated load ch anges, long running hours, daily refueling, and practical exposure to fuel 
storage and filtration systems. Therefore, B40 use in this sector requires evidence on power stability, fuel 
consumption, emissions, lubricant condition, engine component cleanlines s, and filter behavior during extended 
operation. In addition, biodiesel has several advantages as a diesel blending component. Its oxygenated methyl 
ester structure can support more complete combustion and reduce some smoke -related emissions (Musthafa & 
Asokan 2023; Zhang et al., 2022) . However, increasing the biodiesel fraction also changes the fuel properties. 
Biodiesel generally has a higher density, viscosity, and water affinity, and a lower calorific value than petroleum 
diesel (Barabas & Todoru 2011 ; Achmad et al., 2025) . These characteristics can influence fuel injection, 
combustion behavior, fuel consumption, storage stability, and filterability. In high-blend applications such as B40, 
these issues become more relevant because small shifts in properties can affect engin e reliability during 
continuous operation. Previous studies have reported that biodiesel blends can affect fuel filter clogging, injection-
system cleanliness, water uptake, oxidation stability, and engine performance. Monoglycerides and water content 
are e specially important because they can contribute to deposits or filter blockage in high -biodiesel blends 
(Chupka et al., 2011 ; Pichler et al., 2024) . For this reason, improved B40 fuel quality, including controlled 
monoglyceride and water content, as well as oxidation stability, is needed before field implementation. In railway 
generator sets, this issue is practical because filter replacement, fuel p ressure drop, or deposit accumulation can 
disturb operation and maintenance schedules. 
Previous studies show that biodiesel blends can improve some combustion and emission characteristics, but 
they also introduce clear operating trade -offs. Palani et al.  (Palani et al., 2022)  reported that diesel –biodiesel 
blends can shorten ignition delay and reduce CO, HC, and particulate emissions. Similar trends were observed by 
Fangyuan Zheng et al. (Zheng & Cho 2024), in which higher biodiesel content reduced CO, HC, and smoke. At 
the same time, NOx and BSFC increased due to  the lower calorific value and higher viscosity of biodiesel. 
Karbulut et al. (Karabulut et al., 2024)  also showed that increasing the biodiesel fraction can raise specific fuel 
consumption and NOx emissions, especially at higher loads and speeds, while reducing effective efficiency. With 
the transition from B35 to B40, filterability is central to this assessment because the filter is both the final 
protection for the injection system and the most sensitive location for deposit accumulation (Cardeño et al., 2020; 
Thangamani & Jeyaseelan 2024). Higher biodiesel blends are more prone to this issue because increased biodiesel 
content can raise viscosity, water affinity, and the concentration of saturated ester - or glyceride-derived species 
that may form wax -like or crystal -like precipitates, especi ally at lower temperatures or during prolonged 
circulation (Allif Fathurrahman et al., 2022 ; Gozan et al., 2022 ; Na-Ranong & Kitchaiya 2014) . As deposits 
accumulate within the filter medium, the differential pressure across the filter increases, providing  a direct 
indicator of flow restriction. Earlier work has shown that saturated monoglycerides, cold -soak precipitates, and 
fine contaminants can accelerate pressure build-up and shorten filter run time (Paryanto et al., 2019; Paryanto et 
al., 2022). For this reason, filterability analysis is essential for B35 –B40 evaluation. In addition, Aaryan Chib et 
al. (Chib & Dugala 2024)  demonstrated that higher biodiesel content can worsen fuel filter behavior, with B40

showing a greater tendency for filter blocking than B20. However, most available studies were carried out using 
laboratory engines, short -duration tests, or fuel -filter rigs. There is still limited evidence regarding how B35 
and B40 behave in passenger -train generator sets during extended field operation, where power stability, fuel 
consumption, NOx emissions, lubricant condition, component cleanliness, and filter response must be 
evaluated together.  
Despite increasing interest in B40 implementation, there remains limited field -based evidence for passenger-
train generator engines. Most biodiesel studies focus on vehicle engines, single -cylinder laboratory engines, or 
short-duration tests. Less attention has been given to railway generator sets operating over long endurance periods, 
where fuel performance, emission stability, lubricant condition, and component response can be evaluated 
together. This creates a gap between compliance with fuel specificati ons and operational confidence. Motivated 
by these, the current study addresses that gap by comparing B35 and B40 in an MTU 10V1600G20F passenger 
train generator set and by conducting a 1,200  h dynamic endurance test with B40. Fuel properties were first 
evaluated to confirm compliance with specifications and to explain the expected engine response. Performance 
tests were then conducted to compare power and fuel consumption between B35 and B40. During the endurance 
test, B40 performance, fuel consumption, NO x emissions, lubricant condition, engine component condition, and 
Racor filter appearance were monitored at defined intervals. 
 
2. Methodology 
2.1 Fuel test sample preparation and characterization 
The test fuels consisted of B0, B100, B35, and B40. B0 petroleum diesel and B100 palm -oil biodiesel were 
supplied by PT Pertamina Patra Niaga and the Indonesian Biofuel Producers Association (APROBI), respectively. 
B35 was prepared by blending 35 vol.% B10 0 with 65 vol.% B0, while B40 was prepared by blending 40 vol.% 
B100 with 60 vol.% B0. Blending was conducted using an in -line blending system at BBPMGB LEMIGAS and 
at the Lempuyangan fuel facility to obtain homogeneous fuel mixtures before testing.  Fuel characterization was 
carried out to confirm compliance with specifications and to support the interpretation of the engine results. The 
measured parameters , which follow the ASTM standard method,  included cetane number, density, kinematic 
viscosity, sulfur content, distillation temperature, flash point, pour point, water content, acid number, FAME 
content, calorific value, lubricity, oxidation stability, monoglyceride content, cold filter plugging  point, filter 
blocking tendency, and cleanliness, depending on the fuel type. B40 fuel quality was also checked during storage 
and before use in the engine tank to identify possible changes between storage and operational handling. 
2.2 Engine testing and 1,200 h dynamic endurance operation

2.3 Emission, lubricant, component, and filter evaluation 
NOx emissions were measured during the limited performance and endurance tests to evaluate the emission 
response of B40 relative to B35 and to assess emissions after extended operation. Emission results were compared 
with the applicable regulatory limit fo r railway diesel engines. Lubricant samples were collected at defined 
operating intervals and analyzed for viscosity, flash point, water content, total base number, total acid number, 
insoluble content, fuel dilution, and wear metals. These measurements were used to assess whether B40 operation 
affected lubricant degradation or wear -related indicators. Minor merit rating inspections were conducted before 
and after the 1,200 h B40 endurance test to examine the condition of key engine components, including the piston, 
cylinder liner, intake valve, exhaust valve, and injector. Visual inspection was used to identify deposits, wear, 
surface changes, or abnormal damage. Racor filters collected at 100 h intervals were visually inspected to monitor 
dirt accumulation, discoloration, and possible blockage. These observations were used as supporting evidence for 
fuel cleanliness, filterability, and short-term component compatibility during B40 operation. 
 
3. Result and discussion 
3.1 Fuel quality and lubricant test results 
Fuel characterization was first conducted to establish the quality baseline prior to engine testing. The results 
show that increasing biodiesel content from B0 to B35 and B40 improved ignition quality, as reflected by the 
cetane number increase from 49.4 ( B0) to 54.7 (B35) and 55.2 (B40), as shown in Table 1. This behavior is 
consistent with the higher cetane improver nature of palm -based biodiesel compared with petroleum diesel. The 
biodiesel sample showed the highest cetane number of 60.2, confirming its strong contribution to ignition quality 
in the blended fuels. The main trade-off of increasing the biodiesel fraction was seen in fuel energy and storage -
related properties. The higher heating value decreased from 45.76 MJ kg –1 for B0 to 44.03 MJ kg –1 for B35 and 
43.58 MJ kg–1 for B40. This reduction is important because a lower heating value can increase fuel demand when 
the engine must deliver the same electrical output. As the biodiesel content increased from 35.4 vol.% in B35 to 
39.8 vol.% in B40, the water content and acid number increased accordingly. Furthermore, water content increased 
from 130 mg kg–1 (B0) to 250 mg kg –1 (B35) and 260 mg kg⁻¹ (B40), while acid number increased from 0.06 to 
0.21 and 0.12 mg KOH g–1, respectively.  
Other properties also show how B35 and B40 differ in practical fuel-system behavior. For instance, the density 
remained within a narrow range for B35 and B40, while viscosity increased. This higher viscosity in B40 can 
influence injection and atomization, especially during repeated operation in train generator sets. Sulfur content 
decreased from 1200 ppm in B0 to 200 ppm in B35 and 600 ppm in B40, reflecting dilution by low-sulfur biodiesel 
and supporting lower sulfur-related emission potential. Meanwhile, the biodiesel used in blending had 98.77 wt.% 
methyl ester content and 0.32 wt.% monoglycerides. The low monoglyceride value is important because 
monoglycerides are commonly linked to precipitate formation and filter clogging in high-biodiesel blends.  
Lubricant analysis was used to support the engine -performance results by checking whether the 1,200 h B40 
operation affected oil condition. Table 2 shows the new and used lubricant characteristics. The kinematic viscosity 
at 100 °C remained stable between 12.72 and 12.13 cSt over  1,200 h. This variation indicates that viscosity did 
not show a continuous thickening trend during operation. Flash point remained high, between 228 and 238 °C, 
compared with 242 °C for the new oil, suggesting that severe fuel dilution or volatile contamination w as not 
evident. Fuel dilution also decreased from 2.76% at 300 h to 1.40% at 1,200 h, supporting the view that unburned 
fuel accumulation in the lubricant was not progressive under the tested condition. In addition, the acid –base

3.2 Power and fuel consumption analysis 
The power and fuel -consumption results in Table 2 to 5 indicate that B40 can maintain the functional 
performance required for passenger -train generator operation, despite its lower calorific value compared with 
B35. As expected, power output increased with load for both fuels. At 5% load, the difference between B35 and 
B40 was negligible, with 21 kW for B35 and 22 kW for B40. Between 25% and 55% load, B35 produced slightly 
higher power than B40, with differences of about 2 –4 kW, corresponding to −3.88% to −1.78% for B40. This 
small reduction is consistent with the lower energy content of B40. However, at higher loads of 65% and 75%, 
B40 matched or slightly exceeded B35, with differences of 0.38% and 0.33%, respectively. This indicates that the 
lower heating value of B40 did not translate into a clear power limitation under the higher -load conditions more 
relevant to generator-set operation. 
This load-dependent response has an important practical meaning. Passenger train generator sets are expected 
to supply stable electrical power during long operation, and their performance at medium -to-high loads is more 
critical than at very low load. The fact that B40 approached or matched B35 at high load suggests that the engine 
could compensate for the fuel -property difference through its normal combustion and load -control response. 
Therefore, the performance impact of increasing the biodiesel fraction from B35 to B40 was not uniform across 
the load range. It was more visible at intermediate loads, but became less important at high loads. This finding 
supports the use of load-resolved analysis when evaluating higher biodiesel blends, rather than relying solely on 
a single average power value.

The NOx results provide an important check on the emissions implications of switching from B35 to B40 in 
passenger-train generator operation. As shown in Figure 6, NOx emissions increased with engine load for both 
fuels, as expected, because higher load typically increases combustion temperature and promotes thermal NOx 
formation. At the initial operating condition, B40 produced slightly higher NOx than B35 at seve ral medium- to 
high-load points. The difference ranged from −1.06% to 4.58%, indicating that the increase from B35 to B40 had 
only a modest effect on NOx under the tested conditions. The slightly higher NOx tendency of B40 can be linked 
to the higher FAME fraction in the blend. FAME contains oxygen in its molecular structure, which can support 
more complete combustion, but may also increase local oxygen availability and combustion temperature in some 
operating regions. This condition can favor NOx formation, especially at higher load. However, this explanation 
should be treated carefully because this study did not measure in -cylinder pressure, heat -release rate, or local 
combustion temperature.

The endurance results further show that NOx emissions did not deteriorate after 1,200 h of B40 operation 
(Figure 7) . At 25 –75% load, the difference between the initial and final B40 measurements remained small, 
ranging from −3.75% to 2.02%. At 5% load, NOx decreased from 192.93 to 143.67 mg Nm⁻³, corresponding to a

because NOx at low load can be sensitive to combustion stability, measurement conditions, and engine operating 
variation. Nevertheless, the broader trend indicates that extended B40 use did not result in a progressive increase 
in NOx during the 1,200 h test. 
From an operational perspective, the most relevant point is that NOx emissions from both B35 and B40 
remained well below the regulatory limit of 1,850 mg Nm⁻³. This supports the suitability of B40 for passenger -
train generator use from an NOx-compliance perspective, while also showing that the B35-to-B40 transition does 
not impose a severe NOx penalty under the tested duty condition.

3.3 Component condition and fuel-filter response after 1,200 h B40 operation 
The component inspection and filter monitoring results provide an important reliability check for B40 
operation beyond power, fuel consumption, and NOx emissions. Table 3 shows that, after 1,200 h of generator -
set operation, the minor merit rating inspection did not show severe wear, abnormal deposits, or functional damage 
on the main inspected components, including the piston, cylinder liner, intake valve, exhaust valve, and injector. 
At the initial condition, these components were in normal operating condition, and after the endurance test , they 
remained within acceptable visual and functional condition based on endoscopic inspection. This result is relevant 
because one of the main concerns with higher biodiesel blends is not only immediate engine performance, but the 
possibility of deposit formation, injector fouling, or combustion-chamber contamination during longer operation. 
The absence of visible severe degradation suggests that B40 did not cause abnormal component 
deterioration during the 1,200 h test period. This supports the earlier performance results, where power output 
and fuel consumption remained stable after extended  operation. However, the interpretation should remain 
cautious. A 1,200 h endurance test provides useful operational evidence, but it does not represent full engine 
lifetime durability.

The Racor filter observations also support the operational stability of B40 under the tested railway service 
conditions, as shown in Figure 8. Filters were replaced and visually inspected every 100 h from 100 to 1,200 h of 
operation. The filter media showed normal color change and light particulate accumulation, but no severe 
blockage, heavy deposit build -up, or clear loss of filtration function was observed. This finding is important 
because high-FAME blends can be more sensitive to water, monoglycerides, insoluble mate rials, and cold-flow-
related deposits, which may increase filter restriction in practical fuel systems. In this case, the controlled B40 
fuel quality, including acceptable monoglyceride and water content, together with the 100 h replacement interval 
and upstream water filtration, likely helped maintain normal filter condition.  Overall, the component and filter 
results strengthen the operational case for the use of B40 in passenger train generator sets. The fuel not only 
maintained power, fuel consumption, and NOx compliance, but also showed no visible severe component 
deterioration or filtration problem during the 1,200 h field operation.

4. Conclusion 
This study evaluated the technical readiness of B40 biodiesel for passenger-train MTU 10V1600G20F 
generator-set operation by comparing its initial performance with B35 and conducting a 1,200 h field endurance 
test under railway service conditions on the Yogyakarta–Jakarta route (~570 km). The results show that increasing 
the biodiesel fraction from B35 to B40 changed the fuel -property balance, particularly through lower heating 
value, higher water content, and higher viscosity. However, these property chang es did not result in a severe 
performance penalty for the tested generator set. B40 produced power output comparable to B35 at higher loads, 
while fuel consumption remained broadly similar under medium - and high-load operation. After 1,200 h of B40 
operation, power output and fuel consumption remained stable, indicating that extended operation did not result 
in measurable performance deterioration under the tested conditions.  The emission results further support the 
operational suitability of B40. NOx emissions increased with load for both B35 and B40, and B40 showed a slight 
NOx increase at selected medium - to high -load points. Nevertheless, the difference between B35 and B40

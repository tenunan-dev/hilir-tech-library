ABSTRACT - This study evaluates the potential contribution of differential thermal contraction between quartz 
and feldspars to secondary porosity generation during cooling of quartz -bearing crystalline rocks. We reassess 
and extend the thermomechanical framework proposed by Rossello and Gallardo (2026) by integrating its 
quantitative model with published thermal -expansion data and experimental evidence for thermally induced 
microfracturing in granite. The model predicts theoretical thermally induced porosity of approximately 0.4–1.2%, 
depending mainly on quartz abundance and the c ontrasting thermal behaviour of quartz and feldspars. The β→α 
quartz transition at approximately 573–575 °C at atmospheric pressure produces a marked change in the thermal 
and elastic behaviour of quartz and may contribute to differential strain and damage  within the crystalline 
framework. However, experimental studies demonstrate that thermal microcracking begins over a broader 
temperature range, commonly before the quartz transition, and may subsequently propagate and coalesce during 
thermal evolution. The calculated porosity therefore represents potential secondary pore space rather than directly 
measured effective reservoir porosity. Its reservoir significance depends on subsequent tectonic deformation, fluid 
circulation, fracture reactivation, and preservation of fracture connectivity. The results support differential thermal 
contraction, including the β→α quartz transition, as a potentially significant contributor to the initial development 
of secondary porosity in quartz-bearing crystalline rocks and provide a testable framework for evaluating fractured 
basement hydrocarbon reservoirs. 
Keywords: crystalline basement reservoirs; fractured basement; quartz; β→α quartz transition; thermal 
contraction; thermal microcracking; secondary porosity; hydrocarbon reservoirs.

1. Introduction 
Crystalline basement rocks constitute productive hydrocarbon reservoirs in numerous petroleum basins 
worldwide, despite their generally negligible primary matrix porosity and permeability. Hydrocarbon 
production from basement has been documented in numerou s sedimentary basins and petroleum provinces, 
with important examples including the Bach Ho field in Vietnam, the Zeit Bay field in Egypt, fractured 
basement reservoirs in Yemen, and crystalline basement targets in the Neuquén Basin of Argentina (P’an 1982; 
Schutter 2003a,b; Cuong & Warren 2009; Neff 2014; Bawazer et al., 2018; Chiarella et al., 2022; Usman 2014). 
These occurrences demonstrate that crystalline rocks, particularly granites and other quartz -bearing lithologies, 
can locally acquire sufficient storage capacity and permeability to constitute economically productive 
unconventional reservoirs.  
Unlike conventional sedimentary reservoirs, in which pore space is primarily associated with the depositional 
and diagenetic history of the sediment, basement reservoirs depend largely on secondary porosity and fracture 
permeability. Their effective storag e and fluid transmissibility are controlled by the abundance, geometry, 
aperture, connectivity, and evolution of fractures. These networks may result from tectonic deformation, faulting, 
weathering, hydrothermal alteration, rifting, and dissolution or replacement processes (Koning 2003; Neff 2014; 
Trice et al., 2022.  
Nevertheless, an important question remains unresolved: how can significant fracture-related porosity initially 
develop within competent crystalline rocks before or independently of major tectonic deformation? 
Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain secondary porosity and permeability in crystalline 
rocks. Near -surface weathering can produce dissolution and mechanical disaggregation, whereas tectonic 
deformation can generate faults, joints, and fracture  networks. Hydrothermal alteration may further enhance 
permeability through dissolution, mineral replacement, and repeated fracture opening and sealing. These 
processes are well documented, but they do not necessarily explain the initial development of dis tributed 
microfractures throughout large volumes of otherwise competent crystalline rock, particularly at depth where 
weathering effects are negligible.  
Thermal processes provide an additional mechanism that may contribute to this problem. Minerals within 
crystalline rocks have different coefficients of thermal expansion and contraction, so cooling from magmatic or 
metamorphic temperatures can generate differential strain between adjacent grains. In quartz-bearing granitoids, 
this effect becomes particularly important near the structural transition between β-quartz and α-quartz at 
approximately 573 –575 °C at atmospheric pressure. During cooling, β-quartz tr ansforms to α-quartz, 
accompanied by a significant change in crystal structure and thermal properties. The differential response of quartz 
relative to feldspars may generate stress concentrations at grain boundaries and contribute to microcrack initiation 
(Raman & Nedungadi 1940; Skinner 1966; Johnson et al., 2021). 
Experimental investigations demonstrate that heating and cooling of granitic rocks can produce thermally 
induced microcracks over a broad temperature range. Microcracking may begin well below the quartz transition 
temperature and subsequently propagate and coalesce with increasing temperature or during thermal cycling, 
particularly along grain boundaries and quartz –feldspar contacts (Fredrich & Wong 1986; Avanthi Isaka et al., 
2019; Junique et al., 2021 ; Xu et al., 2023 ; Pan et al., 2024) . These observations indicate that thermal damage 
cannot be attributed exclusively to the β–α transition. Rather, the transition represents one particularly important 
stage within a broader process of differential thermal deformation in quartz-bearing crystalline rocks. 
Rossello & Gallardo (2026)  proposed that differential thermal behaviour of quartz and feldspars, 
particularly the β–α quartz transition, may contribute substantially to the development of secondary porosity in 
crystalline rocks. The present study builds upon that hypothesis by comb ining a critical synthesis of published 
mineral-physics and experimental evidence with an independent first -order thermomechanical calculation of 
thermally induced porosity in representative granitoid compositions. The analysis evaluates how quartz 
abundance and the contrasting thermal response of quartz and feldspars affect the potential generation of 
secondary pore space during cooling.

The principal objective is therefore not to demonstrate that the β–α quartz transition alone creates an effective 
hydrocarbon reservoir, but to determine whether it represents a physically significant mechanism capable of 
generating a measurable initial po rosity and microfracture framework that can subsequently be modified and 
enhanced by tectonic and hydrothermal processes. The resulting quantitative estimates are compared with 
experimental evidence for thermal microfracturing and are discussed in terms of  their implications for fractured 
crystalline basement reservoirs. This approach provides a critical and quantitative extension of the hypothesis of 
Rossello and Gallardo (2026) , while explicitly considering the effects of confining pressure, cooling history, 
fracture connectivity, and subsequent mineral sealing. 
1.1 Fractured crystalline basement hydrocarbon reservoirs 
Fractured crystalline basement reservoirs represent an unconventional type of petroleum system in which 
hydrocarbon storage and production depend predominantly on secondary porosity and fracture permeability rather 
than on primary depositional pore space. The reservoir may consist of intrusive igneous rocks, particularly 
granites, as well as volcanic and metamorphic lithologies exposed beneath a regional unconformity. Although 
these rocks commonly have negligible primary porosity, they may become productive  when a sufficiently 
extensive and interconnected fracture network develops (Landes et al., 1960; P’an 1982; Koning 2003; Schutter 
2003; Chiarella et al., 2022; Usman 2014). 
Hydrocarbon-bearing crystalline basements have been documented in numerous petroleum provinces 
worldwide. Notable examples include the Bach Ho field in the Cuu Long Basin of Vietnam, the Zeit Bay field in 
Egypt, fractured basement reservoirs in Yemen and V enezuela, and crystalline basement targets in the Neuquén 
Basin of Argentina (Cuong & Warren 2009; Neff 2014; Bawazer et al., 2018 ; Rossello & Barrionuevo 2007). 
These occurrences demonstrate that basement rocks can locally develop sufficient storage and transmissibility to 
sustain significant hydrocarbon production, even where matrix porosity is intrinsically very low (Figure 1).

The origin and evolution of basement fracture systems are complex and commonly involve several 
superimposed processes. Tectonic deformation can generate faults, joints, and damage zones, whereas weathering, 
hydrothermal alteration, dissolution, mineral rep lacement, and rifting may modify fracture apertures and 
connectivity (P’an 1982; Koning 2003; Neff 2014). Consequently, reservoir quality is highly heterogeneous and 
may vary over short distances according to lithology, structural position, fracture density, orientation, aperture, 
and subsequent sealing. An important distinction must therefore be made between matrix porosity, microfracture 
porosity, and effective fracture permeability . The crystalline matrix generally provides little storage capacity, 
whereas fractures provide the principal pathways for fluid migration and production. However, a fracture network 
does not necessarily arise exclusively from large -scale tectonic deformati on. Distributed microfracturing at the 
grain scale may provide an initial population of interconnected discontinuities that can subsequently be enlarged, 
reactivated, or hydraulically connected by tectonic and hydrothermal processes. 
The thermal history of crystalline rocks provides a potential mechanism for generating such initial 
microfractures. During cooling from magmatic temperatures, quartz and feldspars undergo different thermal 
contraction, producing differential strain within the crystalline framework. In quartz-bearing granitoids, this effect 
is particularly relevant near the β–α quartz transition. The resulting local stress concentrations may promote grain-
boundary cracking and contribute to the development of secondary pore space. The quantitative significance of 
this mechanism is examined in the following sections using the thermomechanical framework previously 
developed by Rossello & Gallardo (2026). 
The exploration and development of fractured basement reservoirs remain challenging because the relevant 
fracture dimensions are generally below the direct resolution of conventional seismic methods. Seismic 
interpretation can nevertheless identify structu ral highs, fault zones, and areas potentially associated with 
enhanced fracturing, while well logs, borehole imaging, cores, pressure data, and production tests provide 
progressively higher -resolution information on reservoir heterogeneity. Purpose -designed three -dimensional 
seismic surveys and seismic attributes such as curvature, variance, amplitude, and impedance may further assist 
in identifying structurally favourable zones. The inability to directly image most microfractures reinforces the 
importance of understanding the physical processes capable of generating them. 
 
2. Methodology 
This study combines a critical synthesis of published mineral -physics and experimental data with a 
quantitative thermomechanical assessment of secondary porosity generated by differential thermal contraction 
in quartz -bearing crystalline rocks. The approac h is based on the analytical model developed by Rossello & 
Gallardo (2026) , which relates the temperature -dependent volume changes of quartz and feldspars to the 
potential generation of residual pore space during cooling. The present study reassesses this framework in the 
context of fractured crystalline hydrocarbon reservoirs a nd incorporates additional experimental evidence for 
thermally induced microfracturing.  
2.1 Mineral thermal properties and literature synthesis 
Published experimental data on the thermal expansion and contraction of quartz, alkali feldspars, and 
plagioclase were compiled to establish representative temperature -dependent mineral behaviour. Particular 
emphasis was placed on the α–β structural transition of quartz and on the contrasting thermal expansivity of 
feldspar minerals. Representative coefficients of thermal expansion were selected from experimentally determined 
values reported in the mineral-physics literature and applied to typical quartz-bearing granitoid compositions. 
The literature synthesis also included experimental and microstructural studies documenting thermal 
microcracking in granitic and other crystalline rocks. These studies were used independently of the analytical 
calculations to evaluate whether the magnitud e and distribution of calculated thermal volume changes are 
physically compatible with the formation of microfractures. Evidence from grain -boundary cracking, quartz –
feldspar interfaces, fracture propagation, and permeability changes was considered particularly relevant.

2.2 Thermomechanical model 
The quantitative assessment follows the analytical framework developed by Rossello & Gallardo (2026) , 
which relates the temperature -dependent volume changes of quartz and feldspars to potential residual pore 
space during cooling. Published thermal -expansion data for quartz and feldspars are used to define 
representative mineral responses, while the modal  quartz content of the modeled granitoids is varied to evaluate 
its influence on the calculated porosity (Rossello & Reynard 1997 ; Hovis et al., 2010 ; Henderson 2021 ; 
Rossello & Gallardo 2026) . 
The model does not assume that all calculated differential volume becomes effective porosity. Instead, it 
provides a theoretical estimate of the pore space that may result if differential thermal strain is accommodated by 
permanent microfracturing. This di stinction allows the calculated results to be evaluated independently against 
experimental observations of thermal cracking and changes in pore connectivity (Fredrich & Wong 1986; Avanthi 
Isaka et al., 2019; Junique et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2023). 
For a unit volume of crystalline rock, the volume occupied by each mineral phase at a given temperature (T) 
is calculated from its initial modal abundance and temperature-dependent volumetric expansion. Quartz is treated 
separately because its thermal beha viour changes abruptly across the β–α transition, whereas feldspar is 
represented by an effective thermal-expansion coefficient appropriate for the assumed mineral assemblage.

2.3 Assumptions and limitations 
The model is deliberately first -order and is intended to quantify the potential contribution of differential 
mineral contraction to secondary porosity rather than to reproduce the complete thermo -mechanical evolution of 
a natural plutonic body. The calcula tions assume that the mineral assemblage remains compositionally stable 
during cooling and that the differential volume change is not completely accommodated by ductile deformation, 
grain-boundary sliding, or mineral reactions. 
Several processes may reduce, modify, or redistribute the calculated void space in natural rocks. These include 
confining pressure, the anisotropic thermal expansion of individual crystals, cooling rate, crystal size and texture, 
pre-existing fractures, te ctonic stress, fluid pressure, and subsequent mineral precipitation or fracture sealing. 
Consequently, the calculated values represent theoretical thermally induced secondary porosity, rather than 
directly measured effective porosity. 
The distinction is essential for interpreting the results. The model quantifies the physical capacity of 
differential mineral contraction to generate pore space, whereas the development of an effective reservoir requires 
that at least part of this pore space r emain open and become sufficiently interconnected. The subsequent 
development of microfractures and their hydraulic connectivity is therefore evaluated separately using published 
experimental and microstructural observations. 
2.4 Thermal behaviour of quartz and feldspars 
• Quartz 
Quartz exhibits two principal polymorphic forms under the pressure –temperature conditions relevant to 
crystalline basement rocks. At atmospheric pressure, α-quartz has trigonal symmetry and is stable below 
approximately 573 °C, whereas β-quartz has hexagonal symmetry and is stable above this temperature (Figure 
2). The transformation is a displacive structural transition accompanied by marked changes in the thermal 
expansion, elastic properties, and crystal structure of quartz (Raman & Nedungadi 1940 ; Skinner 1966 ; 
Johnson et al., 2021). 
The direction of the transformation depends on the thermal path. During heating, α-quartz transforms to β-
quartz, whereas during cooling the reverse β→α transition occurs. At atmospheric pressure, the transition takes 
place at approximately 573–575 °C, although its precise behaviour depends on pressure and the thermal history 
of the material. This distinction is fundamental to the present study because the proposed reservoir-forming 
mechanism concerns the cooling of quartz-bearing crystalline rocks from high-temperature conditions. 
Johnson et al. (2021)  demonstrated that the quartz α↔β transition can generate substantial changes in 
elastic properties and differential stresses within polycrystalline rocks. Their analysis indicates that the 
transition may contribute to damage development and facilitate fluid access to previously less per meable 
regions of the crust. These observations provide an important physical basis for evaluating the potential role 
of the β→α transition in secondary porosity generation.

However, the experimental evidence also demonstrates that thermal microcracking is not restricted to the 
quartz transition interval. Thermally induced cracks may develop from approximately 300 °C and increase in 
abundance and connectivity during subsequent  heating and cooling cycles (Avanthi Isaka et al., 2019 ; Xu et 
al., 2023). The quartz transition should therefore be interpreted as an important perturbation superimposed on 
a broader process of differential thermal contraction rather than as the sole cause of thermal microfracturing. 
• Feldspars 
Feldspars constitute the dominant mineral framework of many granitoids and provide the principal 
mechanical matrix surrounding quartz grains. They include alkali feldspars, represented principally by K-rich 
compositions, and plagioclase feldspars ranging from albite to anorthite. Their thermal behaviour is affected 
by composition, structural ordering, crystallographic orientation, and the degree of equilibrium achieved 
during cooling (Tribaudino et al., 2010; Hovis et al., 2010; Henderson 2021). 
For the first -order calculations developed here, the complex behaviour of individual feldspar species is 
represented by an effective coefficient of thermal expansion. Values of approximately (2.1 – 2.5 x 10-5 K-1) 
provide a representative range for the feldspar framework considered in the model, consistent with published 
experimental measurements (Hovis et al., 2010 ; Henderson 2021 ; Rossello & Gallardo 2026) . This 
simplification is justified because the objective is not to reproduce the detailed thermal behaviour of each 
feldspar composition, but to quantify the differential response between the feldspar framework and quartz.

The contrast between quartz and feldspar thermal behaviour becomes particularly significant during 
cooling through the quartz transition. Because feldspars do not undergo an equivalent abrupt structural 
transformation within this temperature interval, thei r response remains comparatively continuous. Quartz 
grains embedded within the feldspar framework therefore experience a different volumetric contraction, 
generating differential strain at mineral interfaces. The resulting stress concentrations may promote  
intergranular and transgranular microfracturing, particularly where the aggregate cannot accommodate the 
strain through elastic or ductile deformation. 
Other feldspar reactions, including albitization and dissolution –reprecipitation processes, can 
independently generate porosity and microfractures (Engvik et al., 2008 ; Norberg et al., 2011) . These 
processes are important in natural hydrothermal systems but are not included in the thermal model presented 
here. They are considered secondary mechanisms that may subsequently modify or enhance the pore and 
fracture network initially generated by differential thermal contraction. 
• 4Differential thermal contraction in granitoids 
The contrasting thermal behaviour of quartz and feldspars provides the physical basis for the quantitative 
assessment developed in the following section. A cooling granitoid can be regarded, to a first approximation, 
as a mechanically coherent polycrystalline aggregate in which mineral grains attempt to contract according to 
their individual thermal properties. Because the grains are mutually constrained, differential contraction 
produces internal strain rather than simple independent mineral-volume reduction. 
The magnitude and distribution of this strain depend strongly on quartz abundance. A granitoid containing 
little quartz will experience a smaller contribution from the quartz -specific transition, whereas quartz -rich 
compositions provide a larger population  of mineral interfaces at which differential strain can develop. The 
theoretical porosity predicted by the model therefore increases with increasing quartz proportion, although the 
relationship between calculated void space and effective permeability is not necessarily linear. 
The thermal mechanism proposed here should consequently be viewed as an initial stage in the 
development of secondary porosity. Subsequent tectonic deformation, fluid pressure, hydrothermal alteration, 
and fracture reactivation may enlarge, connect, or seal the thermally generated microfractures. The quantitative 
significance of this process is evaluated below by applying the thermomechanical model of Rossello & 
Gallardo (2026) to representative granitoid compositions. 
• Thermally induced porosity generation 
The contrasting thermal behaviour of quartz and feldspars provides a physical basis for estimating the 
amount of secondary pore space that may develop during cooling of quartz -bearing crystalline rocks. The 
quantitative assessment presented here follows th e analytical framework developed by Rossello & Gallardo 
(2026), which relates the temperature -dependent volumetric changes of quartz and feldspars to potential 
residual pore space generated during cooling. Published thermal -expansion data for quartz and feldspars are 
used to define representative mineral responses, w hile the modal quartz content of the modeled granitoids is 
varied to evaluate its influence on the calculated porosity (Rossello & Reynard 1997; Hovis et al., 2010 ; 
Henderson 2021; Rossello & Gallardo 2026). 
The model considers an idealized quartz –feldspar aggregate representative of felsic crystalline rocks. 
Feldspars are treated as a mechanically coherent framework with an effective coefficient of thermal expansion, 
whereas quartz is described using its temperature-dependent thermal response. This simplification allows the 
differential volumetric behaviour of the two principal mineral groups to be evaluated without introducing the 
additional complexity associated with minor minerals, crystallographic orientat ion, grain-size distributions, 
and heterogeneous deformation.

assemblage becomes progressively smaller. The overall response therefore results from both continuous 
differential contraction and the additional volumetric and elas tic effects associated with the quartz transition.

Experimental observations provide independent support for this latter mechanism. Thermally induced

Microfractures commonly occur along grain boundaries and at contacts between quartz and feldspars, providing 
a plausible microstructural mechanism for accommodating the differential thermal strain predicted by the model. 
The modeled porosity increase should not be regarded as an instantaneous generation of an equivalent volume 
of open pores (Figure 3). Instead, it represents the magnitude of differential deformation that may potentially be 
converted into permanent void space through microfracturing. The actual proportion converted into open porosity 
will depend on the mechanical properties of the rock,  the thermal path, confining pressure, cooling rate, fluid 
pressure, and the presence of pre-existing discontinuities. 
The β→α quartz transition nevertheless remains particularly relevant because it represents a major change in 
the structural and physical properties of quartz. Johnson et al. (2021) demonstrated that the transition can generate 
significant changes in elastic properties and differential stresses within polycrystalline rocks, potentially 
contributing to damage and facilitating fluid access. Consequently, the transition should be regard ed as an 
important perturbation superimposed on a broader process of differential thermal deformation rather than as the 
sole origin of thermal microfracturing. 
The development of effective reservoir properties requires an additional stage beyond the generation of 
thermal microfractures. Individual microcracks may remain isolated, become partially closed during subsequent 
burial, or be sealed by hydrothermal mineral precipitation. Alternatively, tectonic deformation, fluid overpressure, 
and natural hydraulic fracturing may enlarge and connect these discontinuities, transforming an initially 
distributed population of thermal microfractures into a hydraulically significant fracture network. The conceptual

hydraulically active. X -ray CT observations indicate that thermal treatment can modify both microcrack

development of connected secondary porosity under favourable conditions (Avanthi Isaka et al., 2019). 
Accordingly, the modeled porosity range of 0.4–1.2 % should be considered a first-order quantitative estimate 
of the potential thermal contribution to secondary porosity in quartz -bearing crystalline rocks. It does not imply 
that all crystalline basement rocks will develop equivalent reservoir properties. Rather, it establishes a physically 
and quantitatively testable contribution that can subsequently be enhanced or reduced by tectonic deformation, 
hydrothermal alteration, fluid circulation, burial, and fr acture sealing. In this sense, the thermal mechanism 
provides a possible initial step in the progressive transformation of otherwise low -porosity crystalline rocks into 
fracture-controlled unconventional reservoirs. 
 
3. Result and discussion 
The results presented above support a potential relationship between the thermal evolution of quartz-bearing 
crystalline rocks and the development of secondary porosity in fractured basement reservoirs. The calculated 
values of approximately 0.4 –1.2% represent a relatively small pore volume compared with conventional 
sedimentary reservoirs, but they may be significant in crystalline rocks, where primary matrix porosity is generally 
negligible. The principal significance of this mechanism is therefore not the absolute magnitude of the calculated 
porosity alone, but the possibility that differential t hermal deformation initiates a population of microfractures

that can subsequently be modified and connected by geological processes. 
A critical aspect of the proposed mechanism is the distinction between continuous differential thermal 
contraction and the β→α quartz transition. The transition at approximately 573 –575 °C at atmospheric pressure 
involves a structural reorganization of quartz accompanied by marked changes in its thermal and elastic properties 
(Raman & Nedungadi 1940; Skinner 1966; Johnson et al., 2021). In a mechanically constrained quartz –feldspar 
aggregate, this change may generate additional differential strain and local stress concentrations. However, the 
available experimental evidence indicates that thermal microcracking is not restricted to the t ransition 
temperature. Microcracks may develop at temperatures substantially below the β→α transition and progressively 
increase in abundance during subsequent thermal evolution (Fredrich & Wong 1986; Avanthi Isaka et al., 2019; 
Xu et al., 2023). Consequently, the β→α transition should not be considered the sole cause of thermal fracturing, 
but rather an important perturbation within a broader process of differential thermal deformation. 
This distinction is fundamental to the interpretation of the model. The calculated 0.4–1.2% does not represent 
an instantaneous volume of interconnected pores produced exclusively at the quartz transition. Instead, it 
represents the theoretical differentia l volume that can develop as quartz and the feldspar framework follow 
different thermal contraction paths. The actual conversion of this differential volume into permanent pore space 
requires mechanical accommodation by deformation, particularly microfract uring. The model therefore 
establishes a potential source of secondary pore space, whereas the experimental studies provide evidence for a 
physical mechanism capable of accommodating the associated thermal strain. 
Experimental investigations of granite provide particularly relevant evidence. Fredrich & Wong (1986)  
demonstrated the importance of thermally induced cracking from a micromechanical perspective, while 
subsequent studies have documented the development and evolution of thermal microfractures using laboratory 
experiments and X -ray computed tomography. Avanthi Isaka et al. (2019)  showed that thermal treatment can 
generate microcracks in granite and modify both porosity and pore connectivity. Junique et al. (2021)  
demonstrated that repeated heating and quenching cycles can progressively modify the physical properties of 
granitic rocks. Xu et al. (2023)  further showed that thermally induced microcracks may develop from 
approximately 300 °C and increase through propagation and coalescence at higher temperatures. Pan et al. (2024) 
similarly documented the development of intergranular and intragranular cracking and its influence on the 
mechanical behaviour of granite. Together, these studies provide independent support for the physical plausibility 
of converting differential thermal strain into secondary microfracture porosity. 
The spatial distribution of these microfractures is also relevant. Thermal stresses are expected to concentrate 
preferentially at interfaces between minerals with different thermal expansion coefficients. In granitoids, quartz–
feldspar contacts therefore r epresent favourable sites for stress concentration and microcrack nucleation. The 
resulting fractures may initially be discontinuous and isolated, but repeated thermal cycles and subsequent 
deformation can promote their propagation and coalescence. The con ceptual relationship between differential 
contraction, microfracturing, and the generation of secondary pore space is illustrated in Figure 5. 
The potential reservoir significance of this mechanism depends principally on what happens after the initial 
generation of microfractures. Effective hydrocarbon reservoirs require not only storage space but also sufficient 
permeability to allow fluid migra tion and production. A population of isolated thermal microcracks may 
therefore have little reservoir significance unless it becomes interconnected. Subsequent tectonic deformation, 
faulting, fluid overpressure, and natural hydraulic fracturing can enlarge  and connect pre -existing 
discontinuities, whereas compaction, ductile deformation, and hydrothermal mineral precipitation may reduce 
fracture apertures or completely seal them. The transformation from thermally generated microfractures into 
an effective f racture-controlled reservoir should consequently be regarded as a multistage geological process, 
as summarized in Figure 5.

This multistage interpretation also provides a possible explanation for the heterogeneous nature of fractured 
basement reservoirs. Productive intervals may occur where several processes overlap: a quartz-bearing crystalline 
protolith provides the initial m ineralogical susceptibility; cooling generates differential thermal strain and 
microfracturing; later tectonic deformation reactivates or enlarges the fractures; and fluid circulation modifies 
their geometry, aperture, and connectivity. Conversely, crystal line rocks lacking sufficient quartz, structural 
deformation, or preserved fracture connectivity may remain effectively impermeable despite having undergone 
the same general cooling process. 
The role of confining pressure represents an important limitation. The temperature of the quartz β→α transition 
and the magnitude of thermal expansion are pressure dependent, while increasing confining pressure can suppress 
fracture opening and promote mec hanical closure. Natural plutonic rocks also undergo continuously changing 
pressure, temperature, stress, and fluid-pressure conditions during exhumation and burial. The theoretical porosity 
values derived from the model should therefore not be extrapolate d directly to reservoir depths without 
considering the pressure–temperature history of the specific basement system. 
Another limitation concerns the mechanical behaviour of the mineral aggregate. The model assumes a 
simplified quartz –feldspar system and does not explicitly incorporate grain size, crystallographic orientation, 
anisotropic thermal expansion, pre -existing f ractures, or the mechanical behaviour of accessory minerals. In 
natural granitoids, some of the differential strain may be accommodated by elastic deformation, grain -boundary 
adjustment, crystal-plastic deformation, or reactivation of inherited discontinuities. The calculated values should 
therefore be interpreted as a first-order estimate of the potential thermal contribution to secondary porosity rather 
than as a direct prediction of effective porosity. 
The temporal relationship between thermal cracking and hydrocarbon charge is also important. Thermally 
generated microfractures develop during the cooling and geological evolution of the crystalline basement, 
potentially long before hydrocarbon generation and migration. Their later preservation and reactivation therefore 
determine whether they can contribute to a petroleum system. If the fractures remain open or are subsequently 
reopened, they may provide pathways for hydrocarbon migration and storage. If t hey become extensively sealed 
by hydrothermal minerals or closed during burial, their contribution to reservoir quality may be negligible. Thus, 
the thermal mechanism proposed here is best regarded as an early -stage process capable of preconditioning the 
crystalline basement for later development of reservoir properties.

The geological evidence from productive basement reservoirs is consistent with this multistage interpretation. 
Hydrocarbon-bearing crystalline reservoirs are commonly associated with intensely fractured granites and other 
crystalline lithologies, and their productivity is strongly controlled by fracture density, orientation, connectivity, 
and structural position (P’an 1982; Schutter 2003a,b; Cuong & Warren 2009; Le Garzic et al., 2011; Neff 2014). 
The thermal mechanism proposed here does not replace these established controls. Rather, it provides a possible 
physical explanation for how an initially low -porosity crystalline rock may acquire an early population of 
secondary discontinuities that can subsequently be exploited or modified by tectonic and hydrothermal processes. 
The exploration implications are therefore indirect but potentially important. Conventional seismic methods 
generally cannot resolve individual microfractures, and the prediction of fractured basement productivity remains 
inherently uncertain. However, the  proposed mechanism suggests that quartz abundance, thermal history, 
evidence of thermal or hydrothermal alteration, and the presence of later tectonic deformation may be integrated 
as indicators of basement reservoir potential. High-resolution seismic attributes, borehole image logs, core fracture 
analysis, pressure data, well-test behaviour, and mineralogical characterization may then be used to identify zones 
where thermally generated microfractures have potentially evolved into connected fracture systems. 
The present study extends the framework proposed by Rossello & Gallardo (2026) in three principal respects. 
First, their thermally induced volumetric model is evaluated specifically in the context of crystalline hydrocarbon 
reservoirs rather than primarily in relation to hydrothermal mineralization. Second, the theoretical porosity range 
is explicitly linked to quartz abundance and the differential thermal response of quartz and feldspars. Third, the 
model is evaluated against experimental evidence for thermally induced microfracturing and pore -connectivity 
changes in granite (Fredrich & Wong 1986; Avanthi Isaka et al., 2019; Junique et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2023; Pan 
et al., 2024). This integration provides a more complete interpretation in which thermal contraction generates a 
potential source of secondary pore space, while subsequent geological processes determine whether that pore 
space becomes hydraulically effective. 
Overall, the evidence supports the interpretation that differential thermal contraction is a physically plausible 
contributor to secondary porosity in quartz -bearing crystalline rocks. The β→α quartz transition is potentially 
important because of the abrupt change in the structural, volumetric, and elastic behaviour of quartz, but it should 
be considered within the broader thermal evolution of the rock rather than as an isolated fracturing even t. The 
modeled 0.4–1.2% porosity range demonstrates that the mag nitude of the potential thermal contribution is not 
negligible for rocks with essentially zero primary matrix porosity. Whether this contribution ultimately becomes 
economically relevant, however, depends on the subsequent development and preservation of a n interconnected 
fracture network. 
 
4. Conclusion 
This study evaluates the potential contribution of differential thermal contraction to secondary porosity 
generation in quartz -bearing crystalline rocks and its implications for fractured basement hydrocarbon 
reservoirs. The analysis extends the thermomech anical framework proposed by Rossello & Gallardo (2026) by 
integrating the model with experimental evidence for thermal microfracturing and pore -connectivity 
development in granitic rocks.  
The model predicts theoretical thermally induced porosity values of approximately 0.4–1.2 %, depending 
principally on quartz abundance and the contrasting thermal behaviour of quartz and feldspars. Although these 
values are modest compared with conventional sedimentary reservoirs, they may be significant in crystalline rocks 
where primary matrix porosity is negligible. The calculated values should nevertheless be regarded as potential 
secondary pore space rather than as directly measured effective reservoir porosity. 
The β→α quartz transition at approximately 573 –575 °C at atmospheric pressure  represents an important 
perturbation in the thermal and elastic behaviour of quartz and may contribute to differential strain and damage 
within a mechanically constrained crystalline aggregate. However, experimental studies demonstrate that thermal 
microcracking begins over a broader temperature range, commonly before the quartz transition, and may

subsequently propagate and coalesce during continued thermal evolution and cooling. The transition should 
therefore be regarded as one important stage within a broader process of differential thermal deformation rather 
than as the sole cause of thermal fracturing. 
The development of an effective reservoir requires subsequent mechanical and geological processes. 
Thermally generated microfractures may remain isolated or become sealed, but tectonic deformation, fluid 
overpressure, natural hydraulic fracturing, and hydrothermal circulation may enlarge and connect them, producing 
fracture networks capable of storing and transmitting hydrocarbons. Conversely, compaction and mineral 
precipitation may substantially reduce their reservoir significance. 
The principal contribution of this study is therefore to establish a testable link between mineral-scale thermal 
behaviour, secondary microfracture generation, and the initial development of reservoir properties in crystalline 
basement rocks. The proposed mechanism does not imply that all quartz-bearing crystalline rocks are prospective; 
rather, it identifies thermal history and quartz –feldspar differential contraction as potentially important 
components of a multistage process controlling secondary porosity. 
Accordingly, quartz-bearing crystalline basement should not be considered inherently impermeable or non -
prospective. Where favorable thermal, structural, and hydrothermal histories have generated and preserved 
interconnected fracture networks, these rocks may constitute viable unconventional hydrocarbon reservoirs. 
Further validation should combine laboratory experiments, high -resolution microstructural characterization, 
borehole observations, and reservoir -scale petrophysical and geomechanical data to dete rmine the proportion of 
thermally generated pore space that can ultimately contribute to effective storage and permeability.

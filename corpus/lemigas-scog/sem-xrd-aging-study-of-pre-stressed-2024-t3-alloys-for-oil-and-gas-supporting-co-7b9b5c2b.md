ABSTRACT - AA2024-T3 aluminum alloy has a high strength-to-weight ratio and the potential for use in oil and 
gas support components that are not wetted and do not experience pressure, such as lightweight brackets, 
instrument guards, inspection equipment housings, and  auxiliary structures of operational vehicles. This study 
evaluated the effects of 0%, 2%, 4%, and 6% pre-strain and artificial aging at 175 and 190 °C on material integrity 
using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), X -ray diffraction (XRD), Vickers hardness testing, tensile testing , 
and fractography. The aging duration is 8 hours. The P4 -A175 condition yielded a hardness of 158 HV, a yield 
strength of 366 MPa, a max imum tensile strength of 505 MPa, and an elongation of 12.4%, so it was chosen as 
the best strengthening condition among aging conditions that still retain an elongation of at least 12% and a ductile 
fracture morphology. In contrast, P6 -A190 produces a ten sile strength of 437 MPa and an elongation of 7.8%, 
accompanied by shallow dimples and secondary cracking. These findings suggest that increased violence is not 
always synonymous with increased integrity. The implications of the study are limited to non-wet, non-pressure-
retaining supporting components because exposure to CO₂, H₂S, brine, cyclic loads, and oil -and-gas service 
conditions has not been simulated. 
Keywords: artificial aging, pre-tension, SEM-XRD, alloy 2024-T3, oil and gas components.

1. Introduction 
Material integrity is an important aspect in the oil and gas industry because component failures can affect 
safety, production continuity, and inspection and maintenance costs. It is not enough to evaluate the material based 
only on the initial strength, b ut it is also necessary to consider the stability of the microstructure and changes in 
mechanical properties after fabrication, initial deformation, and thermal exposure. In that context, the aging study 
of AA2024 -T3 aluminum alloys undergoing pre -stretching is relevant to elucidate the relationship between 
thermomechanical history, precipitate evolution, and the mechanical response of non-wet supporting components 
(Md Kasim et al., 2026; Wahab et al., 2026). AA2024-T3 is an Al-Cu-Mg alloy with high specific strength, good 
fatigue resistance, and adequate damage tolerance. In this study, the materia l was not positioned as a substitute 
for steel for pipes, pressure vessels, or components that come into direct contact with CO₂, H₂S, brine, or corrosive 
production fluids. The scope of its application is limited to support components that are not wetted and do not 
resist pressure, such as lightweight brackets, instrument guards, inspection tool housings, field mobility panels, 
and auxiliary structures of operational vehicles. These restrictions are necessary so that the implications of the 
results are not generalized beyond the experimental conditions. 
The research problem departs from the sensitivity of AA2024-T3 to thermomechanical history. T3 conditions 
include dissolution heat treatment, cold working, and natural aging. During fabrication or repair of supporting 
components, materials may be subjected to pre -stretching due to forming, bending, straightening, machining, or 
geometry correction. Pre -strain increases dislocation density and can strengthen materials through strain 
hardening, while also provid ing heterogeneous nucleation sites  for precipitates during aging. Uncontrolled 
interactions can increase hardness, but also decrease elongation, accelerate precipitate coarsening, and change 
fracture morphology to be shallower or accompanied by secondary cracks (Harant et al., 2024; Irmer et al., 2023; 
Siqueira et al., 2019). 
The aging of AA2024 is mainl y controlled by the evolution of Al -Cu-Mg-based phases. Zmywaczyk et al. 
(2020) identify MgCuAl₂ and Al₂Cu in AW -2024-T3 via XRD, whereas Staszczyk et al. (2019)  and Zhu et al. 
(2020) show the role of precipitates and dispersoids in strengthening. The combination of time and temperature 
determines the transition from not reaching the peak to the peak of aging, and then to excessive aging (Esin et al., 
2021). Zhang et al. (2025) report that cold-rolled 2024 sheets treated at 500 °C for 1 hour and then aged at 180 °C 
for 6 hours achieve 154 HV, tensile strength of 465 MPa, and elongation of 13%. 
Although the AA2024 cold working and aging study has been developed, three gaps remain. First, most of the 
research is oriented towards aerospace and automotive applications, while the pre-stretch-aging pathway for non-
wet oil- and gas-supporting components has not been explicitly discussed. Second, previous research has generally 
emphasized either heat treatment or precipitation characterization in isolation . The integration of SEM, XRD, 
hardness, yield strength, tensile strength, elongation, and fractography as the basis for integrity decision s is still 
limited (Irmer et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2021, 2022). Third, oil and gas asset integrity studies more often focus on 
corrosion, pipelines, descale, blockages, and risk -based inspections than on the thermomechanical history of 
lightweight aluminum alloys (Hameed et al., 2021; Nofrizal & Tasliyana 2019; Novrianti et al., 2026). 
The novelty of this study does not lie in the use of SEM or XRD individually, but in three integrated 
contributions, namely (1) evaluation of 0%, 2%, 4%, and 6% pre-strain thermomechanical pathways and aging of 
175-190 °C in AA2024 -T3; (2) an integrity ass essment framework that incorporates fracture strength, ductility, 
and morphology, not just maximum hardness values; and (3) the translation of the results into initial selection 
criteria for non-wet and non-pressure retaining oil and gas supporting components. Thus, the optimal condition is 
the strengthening condition that yields the highest tensile strength while maintaining adequate elongation and 
avoiding secondary cracking. This study aims to analyze the effect of aging on the integrity of pre-strain AA2024-
T3 through SEM-XRD characterization and mechanical testing. Its specific objectives are to evaluate precipitate 
morphological changes and fractures due to pre -strain variations and aging temperatures, identify Al₂Cu and 
Al₂CuMg phase indi cations as well as peak widening changes via XRD, measure hardness, yield strength, 
maximum tensile strength, and elongation , and determine the strengthening conditions that provide the best 
compromise between strength, ductility, and damage tolerance.

Theoretically, this study deepens understanding of pre -strain interactions, aging, precipitation, and the 
integrity of AA2024-T3. In practical terms, the results can serve as a laboratory screening basis for designers and 
maintenance engineers to assess the feasibility of using AA2024-T3 for non-critical support functions. However, 
operational recommendations still require additional validation through environmental testing and loading that 
represent oil and gas service conditions. 
 
2. Methodology 
2.1 Research design 
This study uses a quantitative-experimental approach with a factorial design. The first factor is pre-stretching 
at 0%, 2%, 4%, and 6%, while the second factor is artificial aging temperature at 175 and 190 °C. The as-received 
(AR) group is used as a comparison. This design enables the systematic evaluation of the influence of each factor 
and its interactions on microstructures, diffraction characteristics, and mechanical properties (Esin et al., 2021;  
Staszczyk et al., 2019; Zmywaczyk et al., 2020). 
Characterizations include Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and fractography, X-ray Diffraction (XRD), 
Vickers hardness test, and tensile test. SEM is used to evaluate the morphology of microstructures, the distribution 
of intermetallic particles, the indication of precipitate coarsening, and the mechanism of fracture. XRD is used to 
identify crystalline phases and compare the position, intensity, and full wi dth at half maximum (FWHM) of the 
peak. Mechanical tests are used to measure hardness, yield strength, maximum tensile strength, and elongation. 
Integration of all data is used to assess strength-ductility balance and damage tolerance (ASTM International 2022, 
2025; Zhu et al., 2020). 
2.2 Research materials 
The research material consists of commercial AA2024-T3 plates from a single production batch. The nominal 
thickness of the plate is 1.6 mm, while the chemical composition is verified using the supplier's material certificate 
and confirmed by optical emission spectroscopy (OES), with an Al composition of 94.96%; 0.10%; Fe 0.20%; Cu 
3.20%; Mn 0.50%; 1.00%; an d Cr 0.04% by weight. The material is positioned as a candidate for non -wet and 
non-pressure-retaining support components. 
The entire specimen is cut from the same sheet to minimize variations in composition and manufacturing 
history. The longitudinal axis of the specimen is arranged parallel to the direction of rotation (longitudinal/L). The 
tensile specimen is dog-bone with a total length of 160 mm, a measuring length of 50 mm, a width of the measuring 
area of 12.5 mm, a width of the clamp section of 25 m m, and a thickness of 1.6 mm. The specimen geometry 
follows the JIS Z 2201 sheet specimen and the principles of ASTM E8/E8M tensile testing. Before treatment, the 
specimen surface is cleaned with ethanol to remove oil and other surface contaminants. 
2.3 Experimental matrix and replication 
The grouping of specimens is based on a combination of pre -stretching and aging temperature. Pre -stretch 
rates of 0%, 2%, 4%, and 6% represent conditions without additional deformation to initial plastic deformation 
that may arise during geometry formation or correction. Aging is carried out at 175 and 190 °C.  The treatment 
duration is 8 hours for all aging conditions.  The complete experimental matrix is presented in Table 1. The AR 
group did not receive additional pre-stretching or artificial aging. Code P2 -A175, for example, shows a 2% pre -
stretch followed by aging at 175 °C.

Each condition is prepared in three replications for tensile testing. Hardness testing was performed at ten 
indentation points for each specimen, while SEM and XRD characterizations used one representative specimen 
per condition. Fracturography was performed on the fracture surface of the entire tensile test specimen. 
Mechanical data are reported as averages ± standard deviations to reduce the influence of local heterogeneity of 
intermetallic particles, precipitate distribution, and deformation. 
2.4 Pre- Strain and aging procedures 
Pre-strain is administered through controlled tensile loading until a nominal plastic strain of 2%, 4%, or 6% is 
achieved; 0% specimens are processed without additional deformation. The strain is determined from the 
extensometer extension at a measuring le ngth of 50 mm. The crosshead speed during pre -stretch is set to 7 
mm/min, corresponding to a nominal stretch rate of approximately  2.3 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹. After loading, the specimen is 
examined to ensure there are no macroscopic cracks or necking that could interfere with subsequent treatment. 
Artificial aging is performed in an electric furnace calibrated to  175 and 190 °C for 8 hours. The furnace 
temperature is raised at a heating rate of 10°C/min, and the holding time is calculated from the moment the 
temperature reaches the set point. After the holding time is over, the specimen is removed from the furnace and 
cooled in still air until it reaches room temperature. Temperature history and treatment time are recorded to ensure 
process repeatability. The interaction between pre-strain dislocation density and Al₂Cu/Al₂CuMg precipitation is 
the basis for the interpretation of the mechanical response (Staszczyk et al., 2019;  Zhu et al., 2020;                          
Zmywaczyk et al., 2020). 
2.5 Metallographic preparation 
SEM specimens are prepared through cutting, mounting, gradual grinding, polishing, and etching. The 
sequence of abrasive papers is 240, 400, 600, 800, 1,000, 1,200, 1,500, and 2,000 grit, followed by polishing using 
3 μm and 1 μm diamond paste, then final polishing using 0.05 μm alumina suspension until a mirror surface is 
obtained. Preparation procedure following ASTM E3 (ASTM International 2026). Etching was performed using 
a Keller reagent consisting of 95 mL of aqueducts, 2.5 mL of HNO₃, 1.5 mL of HCl, and 1 mL of HF for 15 
seconds to display the boundaries of grains and intermetallic particles without producing excess etching artifacts. 
The procedure refers to ASTM E407 (ASTM International 2023).

SEM characterization was performed on metallographic surfaces and tensile test fracture surfaces using an 
acceleration voltage of 15 kV, a working distance of 10 mm, as well as secondary electron (SE) detectors for

fractography and backscattered electrons (BSE) to distinguish the contrast of particle composition. Observations 
were made at magnifications of 500×, 1,000×, 2,500×, and 5,000×, with at least five fields of view for each 
condition. EDS analysis is performed on selected particles and areas to qualitatively and semi -quantitatively 
identify Cu-, Mg-, Fe-, or Si -rich elements. Fractography is used to identify dimples, cracked particles, cavity 
coalescence, cleavage facets, as well as indications of transgranular or intergranular fractures. Image analysis was 
performed using ImageJ software on five fields of view per condition. The parameters evaluated included particle 
equivalent diameter, total density, and area fraction, then reported as the average ± standard deviation. This 
approach is used to supp ort the interpretation of the relationship between microstructural evolution and fracture 
mechanisms (Tancogne-Dejean et al., 2021). 
2.7  XRD characterization 
XRD characterization was performed using Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) in the range of 2θ 20°–90°, step 
size 0.02°, and scan rate of 2°/min. The X-ray tube is operated at a voltage of 40 kV and a current of 30 mA. The 
peaks of the α-Al matrix as well as the Al₂Cu and S′ -Al₂CuMg phase indications were indexed using the 
International Centre for Diffraction Data database (ICDD PDF-4+). 
Peak position, relative intensity, and FWHM are used to compare conditions. FWHM is not interpreted as an 
absolute microstrain value because the instrument's widening contribution and crystallite si ze have not been 
separated. Williamson-Hall analysis or refinement of grid parameters is only possible when multiple peak FWHM 
and instrumental corrections are available. Therefore, the discussion of XRD in this study is limited to a 
comparative indication of the evolution of lattice distortion and precipitation (Pachut et al., 2019; Zmywaczyk et 
al., 2020). The quantitative results of XRD characterization and mechanical testing at each treatment condition 
are summarized in Table 2.

2.8 Hardness testing  
The Vickers microhardness was measured using a load of 0.1 kgf (0.981 N) with a hold time of 10 seconds. 
For each specimen, ten indentation points were made, namely five points in the horizontal direction and five points 
in the vertical direction, with a distance between them at least three times the length of the indentation diagonal 
to prevent the interaction of the deformation field. The hardness value is reported as the average ± the standard 
deviation in HV units. Hardness testing refers to ASTM E384, which governs micro-indentation hardness methods 
for materials using Knoop and Vickers indents (ASTM International 2022; Esin et al., 2021). Hardness is treated 
as a local indicator of resistance to plastic deformation and is not used singularly to infer material integrity. The 
interpretation has always been associated with tensile strength, elongation, and fracture morphology.

2.9 Engineering testing 
The tensile test is carried out at room temperature using a universal testing machine with a capacity of 100 
kN. The specimen is symmetrically mounted on the grip to minimize bending loading, then tested at a crosshead 
speed of 7 mm/min. Strain is measured using a clip-on extensometer with a 50 mm measuring length, while force 
is recorded continuously by the load cell. The calculated parameters include 0.2% yield strength, maximum tensile 
strength, and elongation after breakage.  Testing refers to ASTM E8/E8M (ASTM International 2025) . The 
strengthening condition is considered better if it increases strength without excessively reducing elongation and 
still exhibits the morphology of ductile or quasi -ductile fractures,  without dominant secondary cracks                          
(Zhang et al., 2025). 
2.10  Data collection 
Data were collected in four groups: (1) microstructural and fractogra phic SEM imaging; (2) XRD patterns, 
peak position, intensity, and FWHM; (3) Vickers hardness; and (4) yield strength, maximum tensile strength, and 
elongation. Each data point is linked to the specimen code, pre -strain rate, temperature, aging duration , an d 
replication number. Documentation of tool parameters, furnace temperature history, raw imagery, raw diffraction 
patterns, and voltage-strain curves are stored as supporting data so that the results can be traced and reanalyzed. 
2.11 Data analysis methods 
The mechanical data were analyzed as the mean ± standard deviation across three replications. The normality 
and homogeneity of the variance were examined using the Shapiro –Wilk test and the Levene test, respectively. 
The effects of pre -strain level and aging temperature were evaluated using bidirectional ANOVA at α = 0.05, 
followed by Tukey's HSD test if a significant main effect or meaningful interaction was detected. The magnitude 
of the influence was reported using partial eta -squares (partial η²), while the difference between conditions was 
reported using the value of p. The term "significant" is only used when supported by inferential test results. 
SEM analysis compares particle distribution, indications of coarsening, microvoids, and fracture 
characteristics. Quantitative conclusions about the size or fraction of precipitate volume can only be made if 
supported by raw image measurements. Deep and even dimples are interpreted as indicating tenacious characters, 
whereas superficial dimples, cracke d particles, and secondary cracks indicate  decreased damage tolerance.  The 
following are the results of qualitative observations of microstructures, XRD phase indications, and fracture 
morphology in each treatment condition presented in Table 3.

Descriptively, low -to-moderate pre -stretching combined with 175 °C aging can increase hardness, yield 
strength, and maximum tensile strength. P4-A175 produces 158 HV, 366 MPa, 505 MPa, and 12.4% elongation. 
Compared to AR, this condition increases hardness by 15.3%, yield strength by 12.6%, and tensile strength by 
7.4%, while decreasing elongation by 26.2%. P6 -A190 retains high hardness (160 HV), but the tensile strength 
drops to 437 MPa and the elongation to 7.8%, accompanied by secondary cracking.

The XRD pattern in Figure 3 is dominated by the α-Al peaks (111), (200), (220), and (311). P4-A175 showed 
a clearer indication of the peaks of the S/Al₂CuMg and Al₂Cu phases compared to AR. P6 -A190 shows a more 
pronounced secondary peak and a larger FWHM. However, the increase in FWHM cannot be attributed solely to 
microstrains, as the instrument dilation and domain size have not been corrected. Therefore, XRD data are used 
as supportive comparative evidence, not as a direct quantification of precipitate or dislocation density.

At 175°C, a pre -stretch increase of up to 4% increases maximum tensile strength while maintaining 12.4% 
elongation and ductile fractures. Mechanistically, pre-strains increase the dislocation density, which can serve as 
a location for the heterogeneous nucleation of precipitates. The interaction of strain hardening and precipitation 
hardening then increases the resistance to dislocation motion. 
This interpretation is consistent with Staszczyk et al. (2019) , who showed that aging treatment changes the 
amount, size, and distribution of precipitates and dispersoids in AA2024. Zhu et al. (2020)  also explain the 
evolution of the S-series precipitate, in particular the transition to the S′ and S phases, as an important mechanism 
of reinforcement. However, without measurements of the size and fraction of precipitate area, the microstructure-
property relationship in this study should be understood as a correlation supported by qualitative evidence. 
FWHM increased from 0.226° at AR to 0.268° at P4 -A175 and 0.305° at P6 -A190. This trend is consistent 
with changes in lattice defect states and precipitation evolution, and with the identification of MgCuAl₂ and Al₂Cu 
in AW-2024-T3 by Zmywaczyk et al. (2020). However, microstrains and coherent domain sizes are not separated 
because Williamson-Hall analysis and instrumental corrections are not yet available. 
The comparison between P4-A175 and P6-A175 explains why higher hardness does not automatically lead to 
better tensile performance. P6-A175 increases hardness from 158 to 162 HV and yield strength from 366 to 374 
MPa, but tensile strength drops from 505 to 498 MPa , and elongation drops from 12.4% to 10.9%. Hardness 
measures the local response to indentation, while tensile tests affect the ability of the entire cross-section to resist 
the propagation of damage. The shallower dimple and small cracks on P6-A175 suggest that earlier nucleation of 
damage may limit maximum tensile strength. 
A stronger decline in P6-A190 is likely the result of a combination of precipitation/over-aging coarsening and 
damage accumulation due to high pre -strain. Compared to AR, the P6 -A190 still showed a 16.8% increase in 
hardness and  a 13.8% increase in yield strength, but the tensile strength decreased by 7.0%,  and elongation 
decreased by 53.6%. The combination of coarse particles, microvoids, high FWHM, and seconda ry cracking 
supports the combined mechanism. These findings are consistent with the AA2024 aging response, which is 
influenced by time and temperature (Esin et al., 2021), as well as the results of thermal treatment of the 2024 cold-
rolled sheets reported by Zhang et al. (2025). 
Fractography confirms the difference in damage tolerance between conditions. AR is dominated by deep 
dimples, P4 -A175 maintains relatively even dimples, while P6 -A190 shows shallow dimples and secondary 
cracks. The mechanism is consistent with cavity nucleation, growth, and coalescence during damage in AA2024-
T3 (Tancogne-Dejean et al., 2021) . Therefore, integrity evaluation should incorporate the tensile nature and 
morphology of the fracture, not just the hardness. 
In the context of oil and gas, these results only support the preliminary selection of AA2024 -T3 for non-wet, 
non-critical, and non-pressure-holding support components. P4 -A175 can be considered the best strengthening 
condition in this experimental chamber , as it provides the highest tensile strength, elongation above 12%, and 
ductile fracture. The findings do not prove resistance to CO₂, H₂S, brine, galvanic corrosion, fatigue, or service 
temperature fluctuations. 
Overall, the integration of SEM, XRD, hardness, tensile test, and fractography suggests that thermomechanical 
history should be considered when AA2024 -T3 is used in  supporting components. Aging can increase strength, 
but excessive pre-stretching and higher aging temperatures can reduce the hardening strain capacity and accelerate 
damage. Statistical validation and environmental testing remain required before recommendations are applied to 
the design or maintenance of oil and gas assets. 
 
4. Conclusion 
This stu dy shows that aging affects the integrity of pre -strained AA2024 -T3 by altering microstructural 
characteristics, phase indicators, mechanical properties, and fracture morphology. At 175 °C, pre-stretching up to 
4% increases strength, yields a more uniform distribution of microstructural features, and leaves fractures ductile.

The α-Al matrix remained dominant, while the indications of the S/Al₂CuMg and Al₂Cu phases increased after 
aging. P4-A175 is the best gain condition in this experimental chamber, with 158 HV, yield strength of 366 
MPa, maximum tensile strength of 505 MPa, and elongation of 12.4%. This selection is based on the highest 
tensile strength among conditions that maintain an elongation of at least 12% and do not show a dominant 
secondary crack. P6-A190 exhibits degradation of tensile integrity, with tensile strength of 437 MPa, elongation 
of 7.8%, coarser particles, microvoids, and secondary cracking. Thus, high hardness should not be used as the 
only indicator of integrity.  
The implications of  the study are limited to non -wet, non -critical, and non -pressure-bearing oil  and gas -
supporting components. Key limitations include the lack of complete statistical analysis, quantification of SEM 
images, microstrain separation via Williamson -Hall, and simulations of CO₂, H₂S, brine, cyclic loads, galvanic 
corrosion, and temperature fluctuations. Follow -up research needs to include complete replication data, 
TEM/EBSD, fatigue and corrosion testing, and validation at representative service conditions.

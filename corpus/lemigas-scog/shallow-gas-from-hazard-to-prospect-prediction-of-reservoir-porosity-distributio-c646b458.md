ABSTRACT - The accumulation of shallow gas in the Mada Field, North East Java Basin , is widespread and 
constitutes a significant volume, occurring in sandstone of the Early Pleistocene Lidah Formation , as confirmed 
by well log data and well tests. Initially , this shallow gas was considered a drilling hazard for deeper main 
reservoirs in the Early Pliocene Mundu –Selorejo Formations. The increasing gas demand and opportunities for 
new field development have shifted this shallow gas into a potential resource. Therefore, the purpose of this study 
is to characterize the shallow gas reservoir and identif y the  sweetspot area to support future development. A 
model-based seismic inversion technique is used to generate a P -impedance volume, which is subsequently 
transformed into total porosity. Seismic attributes were utilized to delineate reservoir boundaries, as shallow gas 
accumulations are typically indicated by amplitude anomalies, including bright spots and polarity reversals.  The 
porosity, seismic attribute, and structure maps were integrated to identify prospective gas-bearing reservoir areas. 
Petrophysical data show that the target zone of Lidah Formation sandstone contains gamma ray readings of 10 -
80 API, porosity values of more than 0.33 , and P-impedance values of 8,500-13,500 (ft/s)*(gr/cc). Study results 
indicate that the crossplot reveals a strong inverse correlation between P -impedance and porosity (R = 0.871). 
Low impedance corresponds with the high total porosity that indicates porous sandstone with high saturated gas. 
Based on the integrated analysis, a prospective gas reservoir area was identified to the south and southeast of the 
Mada-1 well, situated at the structural crest and characterized by the highest porosity values. This study provides 
a comprehensive technique for evaluating shallow gas accumulation and offers practical implications for 
optimizing well placement and development strategies to unlock remaining hydrocarbon potential in mature 
basins such as the North East Java Basin. 
Keywords: shallow gas, P-impedance, seismic inversion, porosity.

1. Introduction
North East Java Basin, as a mature basin, requires innovative strategies to unlock its remaining potential. New 
technologies, creative ideas , and fresh perspectives on petroleum systems and geological models are essential. 
This includes more reliable imaging techniques to explore deeper targets as well as modern engineering solutions 
designed to overcome complex subsurface drilling challenges (Wijaya et al., 2024). 
Mada Field, located in the Madura Strait, North East Java Basin, produces gas from the main reservoir, the 
Globigerina Limestone of the Mundu-Selorejo Formations. Above this main reservoir lies shallow gas 
accumulation within the Lidah Formation, confirmed by well logs and well test (HCML 2011). Shallow gas is 
predominantly biogenic, formed through microbiological processes (Perdana et al., 2023) . In many cases, the 
presence of shallow gas is associated with drilling hazards because penetration of a shallow gas-bearing formation 
can cause rapid gas influx into the wellbore, potentially resulting in well -control problems such as kicks and 
blowouts.  Although shallow gas accumulations are commonly localized and discontinuous, when the gas is 
trapped within regionally continuous reservoir sands and sealed by impermeable strata, shallow gas reservoirs can 
also be laterally expanding, forming significant accumulations that are economically visible for development. One 
of the successful examples o f shallow gas field development in Indonesia is in the Bentu PSC area, located in 
Riau Province (Perdana 2023).  
This study focuses on reservoir characterization to support potential future development of this shallow gas 
resource. Two wells and 3D PSTM seismic data were used in this study. Reservoir characterization requires 
understanding key petrophysical properties, particularly po rosity, which indicates the volume of pore space 
available for hydrocarbon storage. High porosity in gas reservoirs generally corresponds to higher gas content and 
defines a target zone for development. 
Total porosity distribution in the study area was predicted using P -impedance volume, resulting from the 
seismic inversion process. The use of well data will improve prediction accuracy (Berge et al., 2002) . P -
impedance, defined as the product of rock density and P -wave velocity, is inversely related to porosity (Munadi 
2005). P-impedance values correlated with porosity through a crossplot of well log data, allowing conversion of 
P-impedance volume into porosity volume. The resulting porosity distribution reflects reservoir quality. Zones 
exhibiting high porosity are considered sweetspots due to their enhanced gas storage potential.

2. Methodology 
2.1 Shallow gas 
  Shallow gas has been an important topic for geoscientists for decades. Generally, it's more often considered 
as a geohazard than a hydrocarbon resource. Shallow gas significantly alters the physical properties of sediments, 
particularly by reducing P -wave velocity. It also affects rock geomechanical properties, making it an important 
consideration in offshore construction projects such as bridges and subsea structures (Kim et al., 2020) . In 
hydrocarbon drilling operations, shallow gas can pose serious risks, as overpressured gas accumulations may 
trigger kicks or blowouts.  
In the current condition of increasing energy demand, interest in exploring and utilizing shallow gas 
accumulations has grown. When present in significant volumes, shallow gas can be a valuable energy resource. 
Shallow gas offers advantages such as easy accessibility, wide distribution, and relatively limited exploitation to 
date, making it a promising future energy source (Cukur et al., 2013). 
The origin of shallow gas remains debated. Some studies suggest a thermogenic origin from deeper sources 
requiring long-distance vertical migration, while others propose a biogenic origin formed in situ at shallow depths 
with minimal migration (de Bruin et al., 2022). 
Seismic reflection methods are highly effective in identifying shallow gas, which commonly appears as 
anomalies such as bright spots, pockmarks, seismic chimneys, acoustic blanking, and mound -like structures 
associated with fluid escape.  Figure 1 shows phenomena of seismic anomalies caused by shallow gas presence 
(Cukur et al., 2013). 
2.2 Seismic inversion 
Seismic inversion is a method that is utilized to predict rock properties such as porosity, thickness, lithology, 
and hydrocarbon saturation from seismic data (Berge et al., 2002). Seismic inversion is widely employed to derive 
quantitative rock property models from seismic survey data (Vashisth et al., 2026). Seismic inversion integrates 
seismic data, which have high horizontal resolution , with well data, which provide high vertical resolution, 
allowing for a more accurate representation of subsurface reservoirs (Sukmono 2007). 
One commonly used method is model -based seismic inversion. This method starts with an initial model 
derived from interpreted seismic horizons and well data, which is then refined step by step iteratively (Munadi 
2005). The a dvantage of this method lies in the strong user control over input parameters, ensuring that the 
inversion result is consistent with data quality and geological constraints. 
Model-based inversion uses forward modeling to generate synthetic seismic traces by convoluting the initial 
impedance model with a source wavelet. These synthetic traces are compared with actual seismic traces to 
calculate an error trace. The inversion pro cess continues iteratively until the root mean square (RMS) error is 
minimized or no further convergence is achieved (Cooke & Cant 2010). 
If the error remains significant, a sensitivity matrix is computed, linking impedance model parameters to the 
synthetic response. Mathematically, this step computes the partial derivatives of the current synthetic trace with

were used in this study (Figure 2). The gamma ray (GR) log plays a key role in distinguishing lithology, where

indicates 99% methane composition. Based on the analysis, the shallow gas interval in well Mada -1 is observed 
at a shallower depth and thicker compared to that in well Mada-2. This analysis suggests lateral heterogeneity in 
reservoir distribution and quality across the study area. 
 
 
3.2 Crossplot analysis 
A crossplot analysis was conducted prior to the seismic inversion stage to evaluate whether the inversion 
results, particularly P -impedance values show a reliable correlation with porosity. This analysis was applied to 
the gas zone in both wells. The crossplot directly visualizes the relationship between P -impedance and porosity; 
it provides insight into how variations in impedance correspond to changes in porosity within the reservoir 
interval. Porosity log that used is total porosity, which has been validated using core porosity from the Mada-2 
well; the value is 0.426. In general, total porosity in Mada-1 and Mada-2 wells is high, likely due to unconsolidated 
(loose) sandstone associated with relatively shallow burial depth. The inside gas interval crossplot result (Figure 
3) shows a strong linear relationship, with a correlation coefficient of R = 0.871, suggesting an inverse correlation 
between P-impedance and porosity. Specifically, lower porosity values in the range of 0.35 -0.40 are associated

decrease in P-impedance (Fuchs 2012). This result is consistent with the physical understanding that more porous 
rocks generally have lower density and velocity (Munadi 2005) . Based on this logs crossplot, a regression

3.3 Seismic interpretation  
After defining the target zone, the next step is to perform seismic interpretation. The interpretation workflow 
begins with a well-seismic tie, which serves as the fundamental reference for horizon picking on seismic data. 
The well -seismic tie process involves integrating well log data, which is recorded in the depth domain, with 
seismic data in the time domain. This process started by generating a synthetic seismogram through the 
convolution of a seismic wavelet with a reflection coefficient series derived from the impedance log. The wavelet 
was determined using a statistical method, extracted around the well location. Subsequently, the synthetic 
seismogram matches the actual seismic trace by stretching and squeezing the signal to achieve optimal correlation. 
This technique ensures an accurate alignment between geological markers in the well and seismic reflections. The 
horizon picking process is then carried out based on the result of the well tie, by continuously tracking the reflector 
across the seismic volume. 
 
 
 
 
In this study, seismic interpretation was performed on horizons representing key stratigraphic boundaries: Top 
Sand, which coincides with the Top Shallow Gas and Base Sand horizons. The Top Shallow Gas horizon is 
interpreted as a negative seismic event within the gas-bearing zone, while below the gas water contact (GWC), it 
appears as a positive seismic event. The presence of gas is commonly indicated by polarity reversal at the top of 
the gas-bearing area. This changes the impedance contrast from an increase to a decrease, resulting in the polarity 
of the seismic response being reversed (Hilterman 2001). Base Sand horizon interpreted as a positive seismic 
event, indicating a consistent increase in acoustic impedance across this boundary. This positive polarity typically 
reflects the transition to a higher impedance layer below the sandstone interval. The  synthetic of Mada-1 well 
displayed in Figure 4. The cosine phase seismic attribute is utilized to support seismic interpretation, particularly 
in areas where the original seismic data have low reflector continuity. This lack of continuity is commonly 
observed where a reduction in acoustic impedance could weaken or distort seismic amplitudes. The application

3.4 Initial lModel 
Seismic data are bandlimited and lack low -frequency information, typically below approximately 10 Hz 
(Fuchs 2012) . As a result, seismic inversion alone cannot fully recover absolute impedance values, but only 
resolves relative contrasts associated with higher frequency components. Therefore, the low frequency model 
is required to provide the missing long -wavelength t rend of impedance. The initial model is a critical 
component of model -based inversion, providing the low -frequency trend that ensures geologically reasonable 
results (Simm & Bacon 2014) .  
In this study, two wells containing velocity and density logs were utilized to construct the initial model, which 
provides the below seismic bandwidth component of the inversion spectrum, thereby enhancing the overall data 
bandwidth. The modelling interval is defined on the target zone between 300 and 1,000 ms . P-impedance value 
from each well is distributed by interpolating across areas between wells and extrapolating them into regions 
beyond well control. This distribution is constrained and guided by two interpreted horizons, Top Sand (Top 
Shallow Gas) and Base Sand. A high -cut filter with a 10-15 Hz taper was applied to the initial model building. 
This parameter was determined by frequency bandwidth analysis of the seismic data that used in this study. 
 
 
3.5 Pre-inversion parameter analysis 
Inversion parameter analysis was conducted to determine the most suitable set of parameters prior to 
performing seismic inversion. In this analysis, two key aspects were evaluated: the error between the P-impedance 
log and the P-impedance inverted, and the correlation between synthetic traces and seismic traces. These are 
essential for assessing how well the inversion results represent the subsurface properties and how closely they 
match the original seismic response. 
The selected inversion parameters demonstrated a lower error and a higher correlation compared to other 
parameters were evaluated during the analysis. Those parameters include a high -cut filter in the range of 10 -15 
Hz, model constraint value 0.6, and 10 step iterations. These parameters produced satisfactory outcomes (Figure 
6), as evidenced by inverted P-impedance curves closely matching the well log-derived P-impedance, with RMS 
error values of 480.97 in well Mada -1 and 441.78 in well Mada -2. Additionally, the strong correlation between 
the seismic and synthetic traces is reflected in the high correlation coefficients of 0.964 and 0.976 for Mada-1 and 
Mada-2 wells, respectively. This result indicates the robustness and reliability of the inversion result.

P-impedance is highly sensitive to fluid changes (Othman et al., 2022). The presence of gas in a reservoir has a 
significant effect on seismic response, primarily by reducing impedance. This occurs because gas has both lower 
bulk density and P-wave velocity of the rock. The P-wave velocity drops dramatically when gas is introduced into 
the reservoir (Russell & Hampson 2006) . As a result, gas saturated formations typically have lower acoustic 
impedance compared to the same formations filled with water or oil. Moderate and high P -impedance, 
approximately 10,500-12,500 (ft/s)*(g/cc), observed below GWC and shale interval below Base Sand horizon. 
A relatively flat GWC was observed in well Mada-1, suspecting a fluid contact within the reservoir. However, 
this behavior appears to be disturbed in well Mada-2, where the P-impedance shows an increasing trend from the 
Top Shallow Gas down to GWC. This upward decrease in impedance  contrast suggests a deviation from the 
expected fluid distribution, which may indicate heterogeneity within the reservoir, such as variations in lithology 
and fluid saturation. 
 
 
 
3.7 Attribute seismic  
A seismic attribute is a quantitative measure of a seismic characteristic of interest (Chopra & Marfurt 2006).  
Seismic attributes play a key role in predicting and mapping reservoir characteristics, including facies distribution, 
lithology, pore-fluid content, and the spatial distribution of reservoir units (Rezaei et al., 2026). Seismic attributes 
in this study were primarily utilized to delineate the distribution of the gas reservoir. The presence of gas in this 
study is indicated by a polarity reversal at the top of the gas -bearing area. It serves as a key diagnostic feature in 
identifying hydrocarbon accumulation. Amplitude-based seismic analysis applied to map the spatial extent of gas-
bearing zones. An anomaly in seismic amplitude interpreted to highlight areas affected by gas saturation, allowing 
for clearer delineation of the reservoir boundaries (Figure 8). 
Red colors represent negative seismic amplitudes, which are an indicator of gas presence within the reservoir. 
These negative amplitudes are typically associated with a decrease in acoustic impedance caused by gas saturation. 
In contrast, blue colors correspond to positive amplitudes, indicating areas with no gas pre sence, where the 
reservoir is filled with water and maintains relatively higher acoustic impedance.

3.8 Conversion of inverted P-impedance volume to porosity volume 
The ultimate goal of rock physics is to relate seismic properties such as acoustic impedance to reservoir 
parameters including porosity (Dvorkin et al., 1994). Impedance volumes derived from inversion are commonly 
used to predict porosity through empirical relationships calibrated at wells (Chopra & Marfurt 2007) . Seismic 
inversion outputs serve as the primary input for this transformation, where P-impedance volumes calibrated with 
well data are then used to estimate porosity across the entire seismic volume (Vorobiev et al., 2025).  
The conversion of the acoustic P-impedance volume derived from inversion into a total porosity volume was 
carried out based on the crossplot analysis performed in the previous step. Once the relationship between acoustic 
impedance and porosity is established from well logs, seismic inversion results can be transformed into porosity 
estimates (Russell & Hampson 2006) . This relationship , expressed in Equation 2, represents the correlation 
between P-impedance and porosity in this study. 
This approach allows for the prediction of porosity variations away from limited well locations, enhancing 
reservoir characterization and reducing uncertainty in subsurface interpretation (Dolberg et al., 2000) . This 
method provides an effective way for generating 3D porosity cubes that describe the spatial distribution of 
reservoir properties. Precise porosity estimation is critical for effective exploration and production, as it directly 
influences reservoir characterization, recovery efficiency, and field development strategies (Rezaei et al., 2026). 
The total porosity distribution across wells Mada -1 and Mada -2, as shown in Figure 9, reveals distinct 
variations in reservoir characteristics between the two locations. In the Mada-1 well, porosity values are relatively 
high, more than 0.40, which indicates a well-developed and high-quality reservoir zone. Additionally, a relatively 
flat GWC was observed in this area, indicating a stable fluid interface within the reservoir. 
In contrast, the porosity profile in the Mada-2 well shows a different trend. Instead of maintaining consistently 
high values, the porosity gradually decreases with depth, from approximately 0.40 at the Top Sand (Top SG) to 
around 0.30 near GWC. This downw ard reduction in porosity suggests changes in fluid saturation within the 
reservoir and variations in lithology, indicating the presence of a transition zone or heterogeneity that affects 
reservoir quality and fluid distribution.

3.9 Prospective area determination 
Structural map, seismic attribute, and porosity distributions map were integrated to identify the sweetspot of 
this shallow gas reservoir (Figure 10).  
Structure contour map is one of the most important tools for three -dimensional structural interpretation 
because it represents the full three -dimensional form of a map horizon (Groshong 2006). It plays a key role in 
defining the geometry of the reservoir and understanding the spatial relationship between existing wells and 
structural features. It helps identify structural highs that are typically associated with hydrocarbon accumulation.  
Seismic attributes are employed to delineate the lateral extent and boundaries of the gas reservoir, providing 
additional constraints on the distribution of gas -bearing zones. Meanwhile, the porosity map is used to evaluate 
the lateral distribution of reservoir quality.  
The porosity map was generated by extracting average porosity values within the interval between the Top 
Shallow Gas horizon and the GWC, which is approximately 50 ms below the Top Shallow Gas. This approach 
provides a representative distribution of porosity across the gas reservoir interval, allowing for a clearer evaluation 
of lateral variations in reservoir quality. In general, the eastern part of the structure indicates higher porosity 
compared to the western part, suggesting better reservoir properties in that region.  
By integrating these datasets, a reliable and comprehensive method for selecting potential well locations can 
be achieved. Based on the integrated analysis, a potential area was identified in the south and southeast of Mada-
1 well. These areas are located within the gas-bearing area, located in a structural high (crest) above -500 ms and 
characterized by the highest porosity values. 
 
4. Conclusion 
Reservoir characterization of the shallow gas interval in Mada Field demonstrates that inverted P -impedance 
effectively predict porosity distribution. The established inverse relationship between P -impedance and porosity 
enables reliable transformation of seismic -derived P -impedance into porosity volumes, improving subsurface 
understanding in areas with limited well control. 
The application of model -based seismic inversion in this study demonstrates the reliability in predicting 
subsurface properties, particularly P-impedance. This is supported by the integration of a well-constructed initial 
model that incorporates low-frequency trends derived from well log data and geological constraints, ensuring that 
the inversion results remain geologically consistent.  Integration of porosity distribution , with seismic attributes 
and structural interpretation enhances the delineation of sh allow gas reservoir boundaries within the Lidah 
Formation. This integrated analysis provides a more comprehensive technique for evaluating shallow gas 
accumulation, including its lateral extent and potential sweetspot area. 
The study result indicates that shallow gas, initially considered as a drilling hazard, represents a potential 
resource when properly characterized. The application of integrated seismic inversion and attribute analysis offers 
practical implications for optimizing well placement and development strategies to unlock remaining hydrocarbon 
potential in mature basins such as the North East Java Basin.

ABSTRACT - Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) is a major approach for reducing carbon emissions, particularly 
in areas with intensive industrial and hydrocarbon production activities. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate 
feasibility of Upper Baturaja Interval in Kandanghaur -Gantar High (KGH), Jatibarang Sub-Basin, West Java, as 
a potential field -scale carbon dioxide (CO₂) storage area. The designed preliminary assessment is based on 
available literature and field data to provide an initial technical evaluation  of the area at a maturity level suitable 
for pre -feasibility stage development. A qualitative method is adopted using standard CO₂ storage screening 
criteria, including reservoir depth, petrophysical properties, thickness and lateral continuity, caprock integrity, 
storage capacity, and proximity to emission sources. The results show that Upper Baturaja interval occurred at 
depths of approximately 1600 –1900 m, with poros ity ranging from 20% to 25%. This interval is dominated by 
secondary porosity, characterized by ideal field -scale continuity. The presence of hydrocarbon accumulations, 
including the absence of significant structural disturbances above the target interval, suggested an effective sealing 
system. Based on an implementation perspective, the study area benefitted from neighboring CO₂ sources derived 
from associated gas production alongside existing oil and gas infrastructure. The estimated storage capacity is 
also relatively modest compared to large -scale CCS projects . The combination of favorable geological and 
operational factors suggested KGH is a viable candidate for pilot -scale or early-stage CCS/CCUS development. 
Future studies should include static and dy namic modelling, injection simulation, and risk assessment to 
quantitatively validate storage performance. 
Keywords: CCS, upper baturaja interval, CO₂ storage, kandanghaur-gantar high, Jatibarang sub-basin

1. Introduction 
Carbon dioxide (CO₂) is widely recognized as the main greenhouse gas responsible for driving global 
warming. As a result, Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) has materialized as a critical reduction strategy, enabling 
emissions reduction without compromising economic development. Kaya & Yokobori (1997)  outlined the 
relevance of the strategy by formulating an equation, which showed global anthropogenic CO₂ emissions were 
directly proportional to the population, gross domestic product, and energy consumption. These variables have 
historically shown persistent upward trends, as in Figure 1. 
Indonesia possesses significant potenti al for CO₂ storage in both surface (such as reforestation efforts) and 
subsurface formations. This study focused on storage in subsurface formations, which have estimates ranging  
between 400–600 gigatons (Boston Consulting Group 2023; Best et al., 2011; International Energy Agency 2022; 
Sugihardjo 2021; Usman et al., 2014; Iskandar & Syahrial 2009; ERIA 2023). However, the estimates remain 
largely speculative, based on basin-scale calculations rather than detailed field-scale assessments. 
The International Energy Agency report showed  that Northern West Java has the highest CO₂ emission 
intensity in Southeast Asia (International Energy Agency 2021). This trend was driven by the concentrated 
manufacturing sector of the area, as shown in Figure 2. 
The main objective of this study is to determine the inconsistency between regional, multi -basin assessments 
and additional local, area-specific potential. This was achieved by investigating feasibility of Baturaja Formation 
within several hydrocarbon fields in Jatibarang Sub -Basin. Area -specific studies on CO₂ storage potential in 
multiple hydrocarbon-bearing fields in the area have not been conducted. Baturaja Carbonate interval was selected 
because it is among the main reservoir producers in the area. Moreover, the existing production facilities imply 
the potential switch to a CO₂ storage interval. 
1.1 Upper baturaja carbonate of kandanghaur-gantar high (KGH) 
KGH is a structural high bounded to the east and north by Cemara Low, as well as the west and south by 
Cipunegara Low. This structure is located approximately 60 km northwest of Cirebon. Furthermore, hydrocarbon 
bearing fields such as GTR, ABG, TGB, and PCT operated by PT Pertamina EP, as shown in Figure 3, were also 
found. Majority of this mature fields have been producing hydrocarbons since the 1980s, a typical example is the 
ABG, representing the most recent discovery in 2009.  The main productive reservoir in this High is the Middle 
Miocene carbonate interval, known as Baturaja Formation. Therefore, the interval is the focus of this present study. 
The naming of the carbonate stratigraphic units in KGH area varies significantly among structures, fields, and 
previous studies. In ABG and GTR structures, the carbonate stratigraphic units are known as Layer L and Z -16, 
respectively (Ambarwati 2018) . The units in TGB and PCT structures to the south correlate with the Upper 
Baturaja or the Equivalent. Additionally, the unit is termed Layer P in the Kandanghaur Timur (KHT) structure 
located at the northern part of the study area. The nomenclature of the interval in Cemara Barat (CMB) and Cemara 
Selatan (CMS) to the north is designated as Layer L and Upper Cibulakan, respectively. Widodo (2018) identified 
a similar interval in Melandong Field and d escribed it as part of Baturaja Formation, which further complicated 
regional nomenclature. 
The term Upper Baturaja or Upper Baturaja Interval was adopted to denote the carbonate interval exceeding 
100 meters in thickness  that served as the main reservoir  in KGH.  Furthermore, Upper Baturaja Interval was 
deposited above the tight, occasionally dolomitized, limestone of its Formation. 
The limestone of Upper Baturaja Interval is generally characterized by superior reservoir quality compared to 
Baturaja Formation. Moreover, a distinct amplitude contrast was found on seismic sections in the ABG and GTR 
structures. The amplitude contrast was derived from impedance differences caused by variations  in density and 
porosity of the limestone. This impedance difference between Upper Baturaja Interval and Baturaja Formation 
is relatively continuous in the area as well as used for stratigraphic correlation.

1.2 Reservoir quality 
Upper Baturaja interval in KGH area showed reservoir characteristics defined by alternating sequences of tight 
limestone and more porous zones. Furthermore, Figure 5 shows a pronounced upward trend of improving reservoir 
quality toward younger strata. 
The petrographic analysis carried out showed that the pore system was dominated by moldic and connected 
vuggy porosity types shown in Figure 6 . The main porosity within the carbonate matrix was subjected to 
significant degradation. This was due to destructive diagenetic processes, which substantially reduced the pore 
storage capacity of the carbonate rocks. 
The porosity enhancement observed in the upper portion of Upper Baturaja interval was attributed to subaerial 
exposure of the interval above  sea level, which arose from eustatic sea-level fall. The interaction with meteoric 
water (rainwater) during this stage triggered diagenetic processes responsible for generating selective dissolution, 
including secondary porosity development. Previous studies by LAPI–ITB (2025 internal report) have identified 
evidence of karstification, as shown by collapsed breccia structures observed in core samples from well ABG-3. 
The reservoir quality in Upper Baturaja Interval is generally controlled by the distribution of secondary porosity 
formed through diagenetic processes, with non-structural porosity predominating. These characteristics represent 
a critical factor in assessing the potential for CO₂ injection and storage in the study area. Image log evaluations 
across the three wells in ABG structure showed the presence of conductive, presumably open, fractures. These 
showed a highly  localized distribution, with a 1D fracture density remaining below 3 fractures per meter and 
restricted to specific intervals. Subsequently, the natural fractures do not constitute the main pore system 
governing the reservoir. 
Widodo (2018) reported that porosity in Baturaja Formation of Melandong area was significantly enhanced 
by meteoric diagenesis. Additionally, geochemical and petrographic evidence suggested that meteoric  water 
dissolution was reflected in the presence of related voids and intervals that showed carbon-13 isotope depletion 
(δ¹³C). The negative shifts in δ¹³C values signified subaerial exposure episodes that facilitated interaction between 
carbonate rocks and acidic meteoric water. 
1.3 Fluid contents 
Upper Baturaja interval in the main structural closure contains hydrocarbons in the form of crude oil, topped 
by a gas cap at the crest. Thickness of the gas column is approximately five times larger than oil and is 
characterized by relatively high CO₂ concentrations of approximately 60%. 
The fluids within the carbonate sandstone of Upper Cibulakan Formation overlying Upper Baturaja interval 
are dominated by gas, with oil occurrence limited to select intervals. A significant observation is the  systematic 
decrease in CO₂ content toward stratigraphically higher layers, approaching zero in some of the youngest 
formations, namely intervals D and F in the ABG Field, as shown in Figure 7. However, the proposed hypothesis 
used to explain this phenomenon led to the triggering of fluid buoyancy segregation.  Following the description 
above, the hypothesis posited that larger CO₂ molecules encountered difficulty migrating through the inter-strata 
seal between reservoirs. The smaller methane molecules migrated to the uppermost reservoir before being trapped 
by the basic seal. The elevated CO₂ concentration in the gas cap of Upper Baturaja interval portrayed crude oil 
production as the main focus. Based on this perspective, the associate d gas served as the byproduct. In several 
operational scenarios, oil production from  wells has been reduced or suspended due to gas coning phenomena 
entering the perforation interval. The outcome led to increased gas production characterized by high CO₂ content.

2. Methodology 
This study evaluated the technical feasibility of the Baturaja Formation carbonate reservoir in the KGH area 
of West Java as a potential geological storage site for carbon dioxide. The assessment was conducted through a 
qualitative screening of key site -selection criteria identified in previous studies, including Bachu 2003; Raza et 
al., 2016; and Callas et al., 2022. These criteria provide a general framework for assessing whether a geol ogical 
formation possesses the appropriate characteristics for safe, effective, and long-term CO₂ storage. 
The evaluation considered several geological, operational, and logistical parameters. These included reservoir 
depth, porosity, permeability, caprock integrity, structural intensity, proximity to potential CO₂ emission sources, 
and estimated storage capacity. Reservoir depth was assessed to determine whether the formation could maintain 
CO₂ in a dense or supercritical state, while porosity and permeability were used to evaluate the reservoir’s capacity 
to store and transmit injected CO₂. Caprock integrity and structural intensity were examined to assess the potential 
for CO₂ leakage through fractures, faults, or other geological discontinuities. In addition, the distance between the 
reservoir and nearby emission sources was considered because proximity can reduce the infrastructure 
requirements and transportation costs associated with CO₂ capture and injection. 
The objective of this study was to determin e whether the KGH area satisfies the fundamental technical 
requirements for further consideration as a CO₂ storage site. Because the assessment was primarily qualitative, 
the results should be regarded as a preliminary screening rather than a final confirm ation of storage feasibility. 
Nevertheless, the findings provide an initial basis for identifying the strengths, limitations, and uncertainties of 
the Baturaja Formation reservoir. The results can also support future investigations involving detailed geological 
modelling, dynamic reservoir simulation, geomechanical analysis, injectivity assessment, and monitoring design

3. Result and discussion 
Depleted hydrocarbon fields represent an established subsurface CO₂ storage location, with numerous case 
studies (Hannis et al., 2017; Lai et al., 2015; and Wei et al., 2023). The storage capacity of depleted hydrocarbon 
fields is lower than alternative geological media such as saline aquifers. However, in many cases this relatively 
limited storage capacity is offset by reduced uncertainty, as existing data are more comprehensive and production 
infrastructure can be converted to injection facilities. 
Bachu (2003) introduced the screening method for assessing CO₂ storage area potential, which had been 
modified and further developed by numerous studies (Callas et al. , 2022; and Raza et al. , 2016). Although 
originally designed for basin -scale assessment, this method can be adapted for smaller spatial scales, including 
producing hydrocarbon fields. 
The adaptations of the Bachu method in Indonesia have been applied in studies conducted by LEMIGAS 
(Iskandar & Usman 2011). The principal parameters evaluated include depth, distance from CO₂ sources, reservoir 
quality (porosity and permeability), and thickness, as well as lateral continuity, structural comp lexity, seal rock 
quality, and storage capacity. Additionally, these parameters were discussed qualitatively to provide a preliminary 
assessment of the suitability of KGH area  as a CO₂ storage location. The summary of the qualitative evaluation 
of the diverse parameters is shown in Table 1 and discussed in the following sections. 
3.1 Reservoir depth 
Reservoir depth is among the most critical parameters for evaluating the suitability of an area for CO₂ 
sequestration. Previous studies by Metz et al. (2005), Bachu (2003), Callas et al. (2022), and Tomić et al. (2018). 
have reported that the optimal depth for CO₂ injection ranges from 800 to 3,000 meters below the surface. In this 
interval, CO₂ tends to reach a supercritical state, ensuring that the volume stored in the reservoir is significantly 
smaller than the equivalent under surface conditions (Raza et al., 2016). The depth of Upper Baturaja Interval in 
KGH, which is from 1,600 to 1,900 meters, falls within the ideal range. Based on this context, the aim is to achieve 
and maintain supercritical conditions. 
3.2 Proximity to emission sources 
The transportation of CO₂ from capture or source locations to storage areas through pipelines, tankers, or  
terrestrial transport constituted the most significant cost components in CO₂ storage projects. The higher distances 
between emission sources and storage areas naturally led to a commensurate increase in the expenditures. In KGH, 
CO₂ source evolved from both associated and non -associated gas produced alongside oil, with concentrations 
reaching approximately 60%. The high CO₂ was separated using stripping  facilities installed within the existing 
fields, thereby enabling significant savings in transportation costs. 
3.3 Reservoir quality 
The main petrophysical parameters of a reservoir, namely porosity and permeability, are critical factors 
used to determine the volume of CO₂ that can be stored and the rate of injection. Raza et al. 2016 proposed that 
an ideal r eservoir should possess a porosity and permeability greater than 20% and 100 mD, respectively. 
Meanwhile, Callas et al.  2022 established minimum criteria of 10% porosity and 10 mD permeability for  the 
preliminary selection stage.  
The petrophysical analysis conducted internally by Pertamina EP on more than 40 wells in KGH provided  
average Effective Porosity (Phie) and permeability of approximately 14% to 17% and 50-70 mD, respectively. 
These values adequately satisfied the basic requirements for further investigation. 
3.4 Reservoir thickness and continuity 
The study by Raza et al.  2016 reported an ideal reservoir thickness of over 50 meters, with a minimum 
threshold of 10 meters. In addition, reservoirs with continuous lateral and vertical distribution were preferred,  as 
this facilitated more efficient CO₂ storage as well as reduced the required number of injection wells.

3.5 Caprock integrity and structural disturbance 
Seismic interpretation depicts the absence of significant structural intensity in the topmost section of Upper 
Baturaja interval within KGH. Meanwhile, no faults were observed penetrating the productive reservoir that could 
potentially serve as leakage pathways for hydrocarbons to the surface. The absence of vertical migration indicators 
from the reservoir to the surface, such as gas chimneys, was identified in the interpreted seismic data. 
The presence of hydrocarbon accumulations across all structural highs in this area was additional evidence 
that s upported caprock integrity. These accumulations showed that the seals and trap configurations  have 
successfully contained lateral and vertical hydrocarbon migration (gas and oil) over geological timescales of 
millions of years. As a result, the accumulations are expected to effectively retain CO₂ column as well. 
Upper Cibulakan Formation, which consisted of claystone and shale couplets including carbonaceous 
sandstones, was deposited above Upper Baturaja interval. These carbonaceous sandstone int ervals frequently  
served as reservoirs for hydrocarbon accumulations, specifically gas. Furthermore, the presence of multiple sealing 
layers increased confidence in the containment integrity for CO₂ injected into the underlying reservoir.

3.7 Surface facilities 
An active hydrocarbon production area is equipped with adequate surface infrastructure, including production  
wells, pipeline networks (flowlines and trunklines), compressors, and other supporting facilities. This infrastructure 
has the potential to be efficiently repurposed into CO₂ injection system, thereby reducing the  initial capital 
investment required for the development of new facilities. Regional accessibility is not a significant constraint, 
because the area is currently established for the movement of heavy vehicles and large -scale equipment which 
support drilling and production operations. 
3.8 Discussions 
The summarized qualitative feasibility analysis for Upper Baturaja interval is shown in Table 3. This suggested 
that the target interval possessed adequate geological conditions considered as a viable CO₂ storage medium. 
Moreover, the assessment was supported by the suitability of the depth, petrophysical quality, and well-developed 
geometry of the reservoir body. The presence of multiple seals above the target area, including the absence of 
prominent structural disturbances, and the existing hydrocarbon accumulations further increased confidence in 
the sealing effectiveness of the injected CO₂.

4. Conclusion 
In conclusion, the initial assessment proved the Upper Baturaja Formation in KGH was favorable for field -
scale CO₂ storage. This evaluation was supported by the suitability of subsurface conditions, quality of target 
rocks, and presence of an adequate sealing system. 
The main advantage of this area centered on the proximity to CO₂ sources, including the availability of existing 
production facilities, besides the geological factors. These conditions provided significant added value for 
implementation by reducing the requirement for new infrastructure development. 
The study area remained relevant for early -stage development, considering that the identified capacity was 
limited compared to large -scale projects. Subsequently, KGH was ideall y positioned as a candidate for field 
testing or pilot projects. This required further detailed quantitative studies, injection simulations, and 
comprehensive risk evaluations.  Based on the  results of the  preliminary analyses of various technical and 
geological parameters, Upper Baturaja Formation in KGH possessed viable potential. However, further 
investigation should be carried out as a CO₂ storage area.

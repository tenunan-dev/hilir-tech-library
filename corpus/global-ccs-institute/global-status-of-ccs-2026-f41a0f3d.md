About us
The Global CCS Institute (the Institute) is an international think tank with a 
mission to accelerate the global adoption of safe and sustainable Carbon 
Management, including Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) and Carbon 
Dioxide Removal (CDR). 
Headquartered in Melbourne, Australia, and with 
a presence in key regions, the Institute delivers 
independent analysis and knowledge sharing 
to support policy development and the effective 
implementation of CCS worldwide. We collaborate 
with governments, industry, and civil society to scale 
CCS through insights, advocacy, and collaboration.
The Institute was established in 2009 in response 
to the growing recognition within the international 
climate policy community that CCS would be 
essential to achieving deep emissions reductions. 
As momentum around CCS increased, so did the 
need for a dedicated organisation to provide expert 
technical, policy and regulatory guidance, bolstered by 
global and local CCS experience, and cross-sectoral 
coordination across the entire value chain and various 
stakeholders. The Institute was created to fulfil that 
role: an independent, international think tank with a 
singular focus – to advance the deployment of CCS as 
a vital component of global climate mitigation.
The Institute is an official observer to the United 
Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change 
(UNFCCC) and serves as the secretariat of the Carbon 
Management Challenge (CMC) – reflecting our 
formal role in supporting international collaboration 
and championing the critical role that CCS plays 
in reaching net-zero. Our global reach and active 
engagement in international climate and energy policy 
unite and support our diverse membership to  
advance CCS.
The Institute’s membership includes  
governments – both national and sub-national 
levels, industry, research institutions, and civil society 
organisations across the entire CCS value chain 
committed  to advancing climate action through 
carbon management.
The Institute’s work is structured around 
four principal pillars:
Knowledge and insight
We produce independent, evidence-based analysis 
to share understanding of the critical role that CCS 
plays and help inform policy, regulation, and strategy 
across the CCS value chain. This includes global status 
reporting, policy briefs, market intelligence, and technical 
assessments – designed to equip decision-makers with 
credible, up-to-date information. Our team consists of 
in-house technical experts and on-the-ground regional 
leaders helping to drive the global CCS agenda.
Advocacy
We engage directly with governments and international 
climate forums – such as the COP and the Clean Energy 
Ministerial (CEM) – to support development of  
effective policy and regulatory frameworks for CCS. 
Through formal submissions, strategic and local dialogue, 
combined with institutional partnerships, we support the 
integration of CCS into national and international climate 
strategies and advocate for CCS as a critical lever in 
reaching net-zero.
Capacity building and consulting
We provide training, workshops, and strategic 
consulting to strengthen institutional, technical,  
and human capacity. Our support enables stakeholders 
to navigate CCS planning, regulation, and readiness 
within their specific policy and market contexts, 
supplemented by our broad experiences and 
perspectives around the globe.
Collaboration and convening
We bring together diverse stakeholders across the 
entire CCS value chain, multiple sectors, and regions to 
foster alignment, accelerate knowledge exchange, and 
address shared barriers to deployment. Through global 
forums, regional roundtables, and targeted dialogues, 
the Institute facilitates strategic cooperation in support  
of advancing CCS deployment to help reduce emissions.

About the report
This report captures the key developments shaping CCS progress 
worldwide. Drawing on data collected through the Institute’s facility 
database (CO2RE), it provides a detailed overview into the trends, 
projects, and developments advancing the technology globally.
Carbon capture and storage remains an essential  
tool in addressing the ongoing climate challenge.  
The Status Report showcases critical insights to inform 
decision making and support the scale-up of CCS as a 
needed solution for achieving climate objectives.
A key feature in this report is the regional snapshots, 
primarily designed to provide a detailed view of  
how CCS is evolving in different parts of the world.  
In the 2026 report, the Institute highlights the growing 
momentum for CCS across established and emerging 
markets, with countries such as Greece and Italy 
advancing carbon management related frameworks, 
while India unveiled plans to invest USD 2.2 billion 
towards scaling up CCS in the country.
The report’s global overview section illustrates that 
CCS projects continued to rise over the last 12  
months, while also highlighting where momentum is 
beginning to ease. These respective regional sections 
unpack policy frameworks, emerging technologies, 
and the impacts of geopolitics on CCS - a notable 
dynamic in 2026. Whilst shifting global conditions 
have influenced priorities in select regions, the report 
finds that CCS development continues to move 
forward steadily, reflecting a broader pattern  
of sustaining global progress.

From the CEO
Ongoing geopolitical conflicts have increased  
global volatility and made energy security a key 
priority, overshadowing near-term climate action 
in many countries. Energy demand in emerging 
economies continues to grow, demand for cement, 
steel, and fertlisers remain strong, and new  
power-intensive industries are emerging.  
Global carbon emissions continue to rise despite 
climate policies like the EU’s Carbon Border 
Adjustment Mechanism taking effect, market 
mechanisms developing in countries like China and 
India, and record growth in renewable energy.  
These global realities reaffirm the need to rapidly 
deploy carbon management technologies to help 
balance energy security, economic growth, and 
climate change mitigation. The good news is that 
over one hundred CCS projects are now in operation, 
and we expect to surpass 100 million tonnes per 
annum (Mtpa) of capacity when projects now under 
construction enter service. Y et we are still far from 
the gigatonnes per annum of both point-source 
CCS and Carbon Dioxide Removals (CDR) required 
to help reach net-zero emissions. Ultimately, it’s the 
cumulative amount of carbon put back into the ground 
and permanently stored that matters.
CCS technology is proven, and projects  
have now been operating for several decades.  
Various commercially available carbon capture 
technologies are operating across a broad set of 
critical industry applications. Thousands of kilometres 
of CO2 pipeline are safely and securely moving 
tens of millions of tonnes per annum. CO2 transport 
options are expanding, with a growing number of 
CO2 transport ships being used in regions where 
shipping has become a key part of the capture-
transport-storage value chain. And despite ongoing 
challenges, over 400 million tonnes of CO2 have been 
permanently stored underground since the Sleipner 
CCS project started in 1996.

Sustaining global progress 
CCS continues to scale up globally, even though 
several nations respond to a challenging geopolitical 
landscape by prioritising broader energy policy 
objectives in preference to additional climate action. 
Over the past year, the number of commercial 
projects entering operation has risen, across varied 
geographies and industries. These advances have 
also been accompanied by the announcement of 
significant policy, legal, and regulatory initiatives by 
governments across many jurisdictions. 
Project pipeline and storage 
networks continue to expand
The 2026 report demonstrates that project numbers 
are increasing and the type of projects is diversifying.  
As of 31 July, 113 commercial CCS projects are now 
in operation with a combined capture capacity of 
68 million tonnes per annum (Mtpa). An additional 
56 Mtpa of capture capacity is currently under 
construction, indicating operating capacity will soon 
increase by 82% and surpass 100 Mtpa as these 
projects come online. The London Register of 
Subsurface CO₂ Storage shows that over 400 million 
tonnes of CO₂ have been captured and permanently 
stored through CCS since 1996, confirming CCS as  
a safe and secure carbon management solution  
(Gao, X. et al, 2026).  
Many of these projects are first-of-a-kind at 
commercial scale, including in sectors such as  
cement production and natural gas-fired power 
generation, further demonstrating the broad 
applicability of CCS to help decarbonise essential 
industries around the world. These new projects build 
on the decades of operational experience gained 
from existing CCS projects that have been steadily 
capturing CO2 emissions, in some cases for decades.
The transport and storage infrastructure  
necessary to support this widespread deployment 
is also expanding. Globally, the conversion of 
existing pipeline infrastructure can and will enable 
the decarbonisation of adjacent sources, while CO2 
shipping continues to expand to service coastal 
emissions sources that may lack alternative  
transport options.   
7
Policy and commercial 
developments enable  
CCS scale-up 
As in previous years, the development of supportive 
policy, law and regulation remains a critical driver 
for the scale-up of CCS projects globally. In several 
instances, the emergence of new policy drivers and 
business models are stimulating and supporting 
market demand. 
In North America, CCS deployment continues to  
be driven largely by federal tax incentives, such  
as the United States 45Q credit, combined with 
various support mechanisms at subnational levels. 
However, permitting delays and policy uncertainty 
including federal funding in the United States are 
hampering progress for many of the over 266 projects 
currently under development. 
In Latin America, Brazil leads the way for CCS with its 
large offshore project and exploration of onshore CCS 
opportunities for the deployment of carbon-negative 
bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS) 
projects that could generate emission credits as a key 
project revenue stream.
In Europe, a variety of funding mechanisms are being 
successfully deployed to support projects, with 15 
projects operating and 21 more under construction. 
At the EU Member State level, several national 
governments are complementing EU efforts by 
introducing new policy initiatives, funding support, 
and legislation. Cross-border collaboration within 
the region has also expanded, with new bilateral 
cooperation agreements, both within Europe, and with 
countries beyond the region.    
In the Middle East, progress continues on building 
CCS hubs and infrastructure even amidst  
the ongoing conflicts. Policy and regulatory 
momentum has remained across the region, with the  
introduction of new climate laws, dedicated policy 
commitments, and formal recognition of CCS in 
decarbonisation pathways. 
Across South and East Asia, governments continue 
to take important steps to support deployment, with 
notable funding commitments, policy initiatives and 
legal intervention. Across the broader Asia Pacific 
region, CCS is gaining recognition in national climate 
strategies and planning efforts. Dedicated CCS 
legal frameworks are emerging, including for cross-
border CO2 transport and storage, but cross-border 
agreements have yet to be signed and the business 
case for CCS remains challenging, especially for 
emerging economies in the region.
In China, government support drives deployment of 
large CCUS demonstration projects across power  
and heavy industry, including offshore applications. 
The release of China’s 15th Five-Y ear Plan  
(2026–2030) further reinforced the strategic 
importance of CCUS within the country’s broader 
climate and industrial transition agenda. The proven 
ability of China to build and operate megaton-scale 
projects underscores its potential to help lead global 
CCUS deployment.
In May 2026, Japan’s CCS Business Act fully entered 
into effect, helping to promote long-term investment 
predictability for CCS in the country. Japan’s Ministry 
of Economy, Trade and Industry (METI) is supporting 
nine projects, including five domestic hub projects and 
four overseas initiatives for CO2 transport and storage, 
as the CCS community and activity in Japan continue 
to grow.
This year, India committed approximately USD 2.2 
billion (₹20,000 crore) over five years to support 
CCUS in power, steel, cement, refineries and  
chemical production, alongside an R&D Roadmap 
aimed at moving technologies toward commercial 
readiness and supporting enabling skills, standards, 
and shared infrastructure.
Global collaboration supports CCS 
deployment
Despite the challenges of the current environment, 
governments and industries globally continue to  
push ahead with CCS. Activity is particularly evident in 
the close collaboration undertaken across a variety of 
intergovernmental fora, industry groups, and  
national organisations. As in previous years, 
collaborative activities support key advocacy 
initiatives, facilitate knowledge-sharing, and  
address the topics that remain critical on the  
pathway to deployment.

Global capture capacity pipeline maintains growth
The global CCS project pipeline continues 
to expand in 2026, demonstrating sustained 
progress across all stages of development. 
However, aggregate capture capacity has 
grown more modestly over the past year. 
This reflects a shift in the composition of the 
pipeline: new projects are, on average, smaller 
in scale, at around 0.5 Mtpa, and many are CO₂ 
transport and storage developments with no 
associated capture capacity. Together, these 
trends help explain the divergence between 
project count and capture capacity growth, and 
indicate that CCS deployment is increasingly 
being underpinned by the build-out of transport 
and storage networks that will support  
larger-scale capture in future years.

Capture project pipeline will diversify beyond absorption technology, though commercially 
mature amines continue to lead as the preferred capture system. 
Commercial carbon capture technology is currently 
dominated by amine-based chemical solvent 
systems, reflecting decades of development 
since their first use for natural gas treating in 
the 1920s and their expansion into applications 
including hydrogen production, LNG processing, 
and post-combustion capture. The GCCSI’s 2026 
Technology Compendium shows that amine 
systems remain the most mature technology, 
with many reporting normal commercial service, 
while also highlighting rapid innovation across 
membranes, adsorbents, cryogenic systems, and 
hybrid approaches. These emerging technologies 
are expanding the range of viable options, 
particularly for low-CO₂ concentration,  
low-pressure applications such as natural  
gas-fired power generation.
Amine solvents currently offer the strongest 
performance at low CO₂ concentrations, as 
demonstrated by commercial projects in Canada, 
Italy, and the United Kingdom, but ongoing 
demonstrations of alternative technologies are 
steadily improving performance, reliability, and cost, 
broadening the technology portfolio available to 
help industries achieve deep emissions reductions. 
Diversified industrial applications require an equally 
diversified technological response considering 
industry-specific site conditions and constraints, 
and there remains a strong need for coordinated 
information and lessons sharing to deploy the most 
cost-effective systems. 
A large opportunity for technology developers 
today sits within the nearly 200 Mtpa of  
“Not Specified” developments. While some are 
a result of developers progressing their projects 
within confidential arrangements, many are still at 
the technology selection stage and yet to confirm 
a solution. This presents the opportunity for the 
deployment of the best available technology 
today, making use of the learnings from recent 
deployment activity.

Policy, legal and regulatory development – A year in review
12
Policies, national plans, NDCs, markets and incentives
In the past year, several countries announced or implemented policies and national plans, 
supporting CCS deployment. Among these are seven countries that have included CCS 
in their updated NDCs, four countries including CCS within their national strategies/plans, 
and carbon markets in five countries that now include CCS. 
National policy
Laws, regulations, standards, permits and sub‑national frameworks 
The development of legal and regulatory frameworks for CCS continues across the globe, 
including at the sub-national level. Several CCS-related laws, regulations and standards 
were passed or enforced over the past year. New permits/licenses were also awarded to 
CCS projects in Denmark, Greece, Malaysia, Netherlands, and the United States. At the 
sub-national level, Western Australia passed CCS legislation, and in the United States, 
Texas and Arizona were granted Class VI primacy.
Enabling legal and regulatory frameworks
Funding released to CCS projects and cross‑border infrastructure
In Europe, significant funding has been released to CCS projects, with the latest funding 
round of the EU Innovation Fund releasing support to eleven CCS projects, and CINEA 
signing grant agreements with ten cross-border CO2 transport and storage projects. 
The Netherlands, Denmark, and Flanders have each awarded approximately €2 billion 
to domestic CCS projects, while France awarded €1.6 billion to seven decarbonisation 
projects, four of which include CCUS.
Direct funding
Cross‑region, bilateral and cross‑border coordination
The past year has seen the conclusion of several CCS cooperation agreements and 
collaboration announcements. Examples of cooperation across regions include the 
Canada-Saudi Arabia agreement and the Sweden-Indonesia announcement. In the US, 
Colorado and Wyoming have agreed to coordinate permitting for CO₂ storage projects 
that may cross their borders.

Case study: bridging the CCS deployment gap
California Resources Corporation’s first commercial CCS 
project, located at Elk Hills Oil Field. Capture facility and

Bridging the gap to commercial-scale CCS in the industrial sector will 
require creating investment frameworks that enable deployment in 
hard-to-abate industries. While early incentives helped establish CCS 
in the Americas, international projects such as Norway’s Heidelberg 
Materials Brevik cement project (Heidelberg Materials, 2026) and 
the UK’s Net Zero T eesside (NZT Power, 2026) project demonstrate 
how industry-specific capture solutions, shared infrastructure, and 
long-term revenue certainty, such as through CCfD programmes, can 
accelerate deployment. For states and provinces, the next phase of 
CCS deployment is not only a decarbonisation opportunity, but also an 
economic development strategy for the emerging low-carbon economy.
         Financing industrial decarbonisation
Operational CCS projects in the Americas have a combined capture 
capacity exceeding 48 Mtpa, yet deployment remains centred around a 
handful of sectors with high-concentration CO2 streams and access to 
existing transport and storage infrastructure. Inflation and rising capital 
costs have weakened project economics, reducing the effectiveness 
of existing policies, such as tax and investment credits. According to 
studies conducted by the Institute and the EFI Foundation, about a 
third of these sectors tend to be commercially viable with current 45Q 
incentives, highlighting the need for additional policy and financing 
mechanisms (Barlow et al., 2025; Moniz et al., 2025).
Based on the Institute’s CO2RE database, operational projects in the 
Americas include more than 30 Mtpa of capture capacity from natural 
gas processing projects; approximately 9.4 Mtpa from hydrogen, 
ammonia, and fertiliser projects; and over 4.0 Mtpa from ethanol and 
biomass projects. Comparatively, across all sectors and development 
stages, the Americas have an estimated maximum of over 280 Mtpa of 
publicly identified capture capacity. The sectors above represent only 
about 53% of that total, reinforcing the need for additional mechanisms 
to unlock the remaining 132 Mtpa of publicly identified capture potential 
from natural gas power, cement, chemical, DAC and others.

For CO2 capture projects, commercialisation is 
emerging alongside an expanding suite of policy 
support mechanisms in Europe. Developers are 
increasingly combining multiple public support  
and market-based mechanisms to strengthen 
project economics. While these mechanisms can 
help close funding gaps, they are not always 
perfectly complementary, requiring developers to 
piece together support from several mechanisms 
to achieve a positive FID.
Public funding mechanisms typically form  
the backbone of capture project financing.  
Upfront capital grants, notably from the EU 
Innovation Fund and national State Aid, help 
support project development. For example,  
in March 2026 the Kairos@C project was awarded 
€260 million from the Flemish Government 
(European Commission, 2026c). In addition, 
mechanisms designed to bridge the cost  
gap between conventional and low-carbon  
production, such as CCfDs, can also improve 
bankability by providing a predictable cash flow  
(Global CCS Institute, 2025). Predictable refinement 
of emissions trading systems (ETS), together with 
the phased implementation of Carbon Border 
Adjustment Mechanisms (CBAMs), could help to 
reinforce carbon price signals, but at present are 
not providing a consistently reliable signal and 
the impact of the latest EU ETS revision remains 
unclear (European Commission, 2026d). 
In parallel, CO2 capture project developers are 
increasingly utilising market-based mechanisms 
(where available) to enhance financial resilience. 
These include the sale of carbon removal credits 
through voluntary carbon markets. In the EU,  
the Carbon Removals and Carbon Farming 
Certification Framework (CRCF) is expected to 
provide a harmonised certification framework, 
supporting high-integrity carbon removal markets 
(Carbon Removals and Carbon Farming, 2024). 
In addition, lead market measures under the EU’s 
Industrial Accelerator Act (IAA) are expected to 
stimulate demand for low-carbon products through 
public procurement (Industrial Accelerator Act, 
2026), potentially increasing demand for  
low-carbon products and enabling low-carbon 
product price premiums.
However, combining multiple funding mechanisms 
adds complexity. Challenges around aligning 
emissions accounting methodologies, avoiding 
double counting and double penalty in carbon 
markets, and ensuring compliance with EU State 
Aid requirements mean that building an investable 
business case remains project-specific.
A range of approaches are illustrated by projects that have taken FID in 
Europe, including:
• Air Liquide Rotterdam: EU Innovation Fund grant combined with a CCfD under the SDE++ 
scheme (Air Liquide, n.d.).
• Ørsted Asnæs and Avedøre: 20-year subsidy contracts from the Danish Energy Agency 
alongside revenues from voluntary carbon markets (Microsoft) (Ørsted, n.d.).
• Stockholm Exergi: Integration of EU Innovation Fund support, Swedish Energy Agency’s 
reverse auction for Bio-CCS, and voluntary market revenues (Stockholm Exergi, n.d.).

India has rapidly growing energy demand and 
derives more than 75% of its total energy supply 
from fossil fuels. Energy related CO2 emissions

of CCUS across its power, cement, steel and 
chemical industries.

over the past three years, the Government

discussions about CCUS to integrating  
CCUS into its approach to achieving  
net-zero emissions by 2070.
India’s CCUS programme is being taken forward 
in “mission mode” with the Ministry of Power  
as the central coordinator across Ministries. 
(Gupta, 2025) This mirrors India’s approach 
in other strategic areas (e.g. renewables, 
hydrogen) where mission-mode programmes 
have been effective in focusing funding and 
regulatory attention and establishes a single 
point of accountability within government.
Under the “mission mode” approach, the Indian 
government has initiated the core elements of 
a national deployment pathway: recognising 
abatement from CCUS in mandatory emission 
reduction programmes, developing R&D 
strategies, setting a clear policy direction  
and integrating CCUS into industry strategies, 
and activating investment by providing material 
financial support. This has sent a clear message 
to industry, building confidence to develop 
India’s first storage and injection pilot projects. 
Taken together, the steps form a coherent,  
if still early-stage strategy to deploy CCUS at 
the scale necessary to support achievement of 
India’s 2070 Net-Zero target.

Case study: CNPC Jilin CCUS project
Scaling CCUS-EOR from pilot 
testing to full-chain demonstration
The CNPC Jilin CCUS project is one of China’s most 
established and longest-running CCUS-EOR initiatives. 
It illustrates the country’s gradual transition from 
early field trials to integrated, million-tonne-scale 
deployment in carbon management. Built on more 
than two decades of research, pilot testing, and field 
application, the project has become an important 
benchmark for scaling CO₂-EOR and geological 
storage in low-permeability reservoirs.
A major turning point came in 2005, when a CO₂-rich 
natural gas reservoir was identified in the Changling 
area (ChinaNews, 2025). The nearby Daqingzi Oilfield 
offered favourable conditions for CO₂ miscible 
flooding, supported by well-matched source-sink 
conditions and suitable reservoir characteristics. 
Over subsequent years, CNPC expanded research, 
conducted pilot tests, and advanced engineering 
applications, laying the foundation for wider 
deployment of CCUS-EOR.
CNPC Jilin Oilfield CCUS Project. Image is 
courtesy of of CNPC Research Institute of 
Petroleum Exploration and Development
By the end of 2025, five integrated CO₂-EOR and 
geological storage demonstration zones had been 
deployed. In addition to CO2 from natural gas 
production, this project now injects CO2 from  
other industrial sources, with an annual injection 
capacity of 800 ktpa (ChinaNews, 2025).  
Cumulatively, around 4.1 million tonnes of CO₂ had 
been injected, of which 3.61 million tonnes were 
permanently stored (XinhuaNet, 2026). Produced gas 
is recycled and reinjected, creating a closed-loop 
operating system that eliminates direct CO₂ emissions.
To support further scale-up, CNPC is developing a 
two-phase CO₂ pipeline network with a planned  
total length of around 400 km. The first phase,  
already under construction, spans 282 km and is 
designed to transport 3.3 million tonnes of CO₂ 
annually (CNPC, 2026). Scheduled to become 
operational in 2026, the pipeline will connect 
industrial CO₂ sources, such as chemical and power 
generation facilities, with oilfield injection and storage 
sites, forming a key backbone for carbon transport, 
utilisation, and storage in Jilin Province. As China 
continues to scale CCUS deployment, the CNPC 
Jilin project provides a practical example of how 
early pilots can evolve into larger, more coordinated 
industrial systems.

Japan
Case study: Japan’s advanced CCS projects
Overall project concept 
In February 2025, sub-seabed formations off 
Tomakomai City, Hokkaido, were designated by 
the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry (METI) 
as a specified area for CO₂ storage. Subsequently, 
in September 2025, Japan Petroleum Exploration 
Co., Ltd. (JAPEX) was granted a permit to conduct 
exploratory drilling.
The TomaCO₂mai CCS project aims to integrate 
the capture, transport, and storage of CO₂ in the 
Tomakomai region. Three companies—JAPEX, 
Idemitsu Kosan Co., Ltd., and Hokkaido Electric 
Power Co., Inc.—are the project proponents.  
By leveraging their respective technical expertise, 
the project aims to establish a CCS model that will 
drive the region’s transition to carbon neutrality.
A major strength of this project is T omakomai’s 
proven track record of successfully conducting 
Japan’s first large-scale CCS demonstration test 
(injecting approximately 300,000 tonnes of CO₂) 
and established community support for  
promoting CCS.
Project overview
The roles and responsibilities of each consortium 
member within this project are as follows.
• CO₂ separation and capture:  
CO₂ is captured from the exhaust gas of 
the refinery and the thermal power station 
operated by Idemitsu Kosan and Hokkaido 
Electric Power, respectively. CO₂ capture 
is planned to start in 2030 with estimated 
volumes of around 1.5 to 2 Mtpa. 
Among the 9 “Advanced CCS Projects” initiated by the Japanese government, the Tomakomai 
Area (or TomaCO₂mai) CCS project and Metropolitan Area CCS are the most advanced. 
• CO₂ transport:  
JAPEX will be responsible for building and 
operating the pipeline to transport the captured 
CO₂ for storage.
• CO₂ storage: 
JAPEX also leads the injection and storage 
of the captured CO₂, targeting deep saline 
aquifer of ~ 1,500 metres below the ocean floor 
off the coast of T omakomai. The CO2 will be 
continuously monitored by JAPEX to ensure 
integrity of the storage. 
TomaCO₂mai CCS aims to commence CO₂  
storage on a scale of 1.5 to 2 million tonnes per 
year by the end of March 2031. This target date  
is contingent on reaching Final Investment  
Decision by March 2027.
Achievements to date
Each phase of the project has achieved the 
following outcomes to date.
Separation and capture 
• Both Idemitsu Kosan (Hokkaido Refinery)  
and Hokkaido Electric Power  
(Tomato-Atsuma Power Station) carried  
out front-end engineering design (FEED) for 
CO₂ separation and capture facilities.
• Details of the utility supply methods, the  
scope of modifications to existing facilities,  
and installation layouts are finalised.
Transport 
• Basic design of the pipeline and injection 
facilities were carried out by JAPEX.
• The CO₂ properties have been carefully 
evaluated to ensure safe transport  
and injection.
Storage and test drilling preparation 
• JAPEX conducted design work for the injection 
facilities, wells, and monitoring systems.
• Progress was made on soil surveys at potential 
test drilling sites, well design studies, and the 
evaluation of the long lead items.
Proposed schedule
For the FY2026 (Commencing April 2026): 
Continue to progress design work,  
exploratory drilling preparations, and enter  
Final Investment Decision. 
FY2027–2029: Construct facilities including 
pipeline, and drill injection wells.
FY2030: Commence CO₂ injection.
 
TomaCO₂mai CCS project
Challenges and response 
strategies
The project proponents identified a number of 
challenges and mitigation actions along with 
appropriate steps are being taken to address them. 
These include:
• Fostering and increasing local acceptance 
of the project and value of CCS: Continuous 
information sharing and explanatory activities 
with local authorities and the community.
• Extended construction period and rising costs: 
Bringing forward design work. 
• Securing utilities: Examining options for 
securing industrial water and electricity in 
collaboration with local authorities.
Project outlook
Once storage commences, the project proponents 
aim to gradually increase the volume of captured 
CO₂ and the storage rates. By collaborating with 
carbon neutral projects both within and outside the 
Tomakomai region, the project proponents aim to 
establish a CCS and carbon neutrality hub that will 
serve as a national model for the future.
 (JAPEX, 2025)

is permanent and verified. CCUS is the preferred  
term in some regions.
Utilisation/conversion Includes the conversion 
of captured carbon oxides, primarily CO₂, into 
economically valuable products such as chemicals, 
fuels, building materials, plastics, and bioproducts. 
It also includes use of CO₂ as a working fluid for 
enhanced hydrocarbon recovery (EOR or EGR). 
Conversion is used when referring to carbon  
uptake, catalytic conversion, and mineralisation. 
Utilisation is used when referring to enhanced 
hydrocarbon recovery with monitoring and  
verification for long-term storage. 
Geologic storage Refers to CO₂ storage in dedicated 
reservoirs and enhanced hydrocarbon recovery 
projects that include monitoring, verification,  
and reporting of the amount of CO₂ stored. 
Carbon management Includes both natural and 
technological solutions for removing ambient CO₂ 
from the air or capturing CO₂ emissions from industrial 
processes, with the goal of preventing/mitigating 
their contribution to climate change. These solutions 
encompass CDR technologies (such as direct air 
capture and reforestation), and carbon capture 
technologies (which capture CO₂ emissions from 
higher concentration sources like fossil fuel-fired 
power plants and refineries).
CDR includes activities that remove CO₂ from the 
atmosphere and durably store it in geological, 
terrestrial, or ocean reservoirs, or in products.  
CDR includes enhancement of biological or 
geochemical sinks and DAC/DACCS but excludes 
natural CO₂ uptake not directly caused by  
human intervention.
DAC/DACCS A process that removes CO₂ from 
ambient air. When paired with CO₂ storage strategies, 
it is referred to as DACCS.
Project terms
Facility One company (or JV) owning a full value chain 
is one facility regardless of the distance between 
capture and storage. One company owning CO₂ 
transport and storage infrastructure is one facility. 
Two separate power stations with CCS owned by one 
company would be two facilities. 
Cluster A geographic concentration of interconnected 
businesses, suppliers, and associated institutions, 
typically due to proximity to raw materials, transport 
options such as ports, or to labour supply and markets.
Hub Central collection or distribution point for CO₂. 
One hub would service the collection of CO₂ from  
a capture cluster, or the distribution of CO₂ to a 
storage cluster.
Network A connection between two facilities via the 
use of a transport facility, regardless of ownership. 
• Capture network: CO₂ captured and collected 
from multiple facilities with different owners.
• Transport/storage networks: CO₂ collected from 
multiple facilities, transported through one or 
more types of transport (pipeline, truck, ship)  
and stored in one or more storage sites. 
Anchor project A large emitter that provides a 
significant proportion of the CO₂ in a cluster of CO₂ 
capture projects. Typically bears the fixed costs of the 
initial infrastructure.

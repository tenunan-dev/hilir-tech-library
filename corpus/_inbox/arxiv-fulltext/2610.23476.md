Abstract
Change points that arrive in quick succession, or near the boundaries of the sample,
are prone to type-II error: the few observations trapped between successive breaks
carry too little information for global procedures to analyze. This article develops a
test statistic,I 1, for which we derive the limiting null distribution under Gaussian noise,
extend the theory to weakly dependent sequences, and prove consistency under a mean-
shift alternative. AroundI 1, we construct DCI, a two-phase algorithm that couples a
global scan with multiple local tests via an adaptive-width sliding window. In extensive
simulations,I 1 and DCI remain Pareto-optimal over criteria spaces spanning true and
false positive rates, localization error, and segmentation covering. An application to the
US-Iran 2026 conflict quantifies the exposure of energy markets to geopolitical shocks
using two new metrics, event sensitivity and event attribution, leading to time series
tokenization.
Keywords:Hypothesis testing; extreme value theory; segmentation; financial time series; price
shocks

1 Introduction
Change point detection, the identification of time points at which the statistical properties of a
series shift abruptly, is a central problem in time series analysis. In finance, economics, engineering,
and environmental studies alike, a reliable account of structural shifts is a prerequisite for explain-
able modeling, accurate forecasting, and sound decision-making, whether the object of interest is a
market regime change or the impact of a macroeconomic policy. Modern data environments, how-
ever, increasingly confront the analyst with series in which change points occur in quick succession
and separated by too few observations. Volatile, high-frequency financial data during geopolitical
crises are a leading example. Conventional detection methods, designed with well-separated breaks
in mind, often struggle in precisely these settings, and this difficulty motivates the present article.
The literature on change point analysis is vast, and we can only outline the strands most rel-
evant to our work. A classical formulation casts change detection as a model selection problem
(Truong et al., 2020), resolved through likelihood ratio maximization, with foundational contribu-
tions by Hinkley (1970) and multivariate mean change tests developed by Srivastava and Worsley
(1986). In parallel, nonparametric statistics, most prominently the CUSUM of Page (1954) and its
extensions (Fremdt, 2015; Han et al., 2023; Inclan and Tiao, 1994), dispense with distributional
assumptions and offer model-free alternatives. Within this tradition, solutions are now available
for changes in the mean (Pettitt, 1979), high-dimensional means (Drikvandi and Modarres, 2025),
the slope (Fearnhead, Maidstone, and Letchford, 2019), the variance (Qin et al., 2017), and the
covariance structure (Londschien et al., 2021). Further nonparametric approaches include rank-
and U-statistic-based procedures for high-dimensional data (L´ evy-Leduc and Roueff, 2009), graph-
based methods (Chen and N. Zhang, 2015), and multiple change-point detection in multivariate data
(Matteson and James, 2014). For the multiple change point problem specifically, exact search proce-
dures based on dynamic programming trace back to the segment neighborhood algorithm of Auger
and Lawrence (1989), with computationally efficient descendants in the pruned exact linear time
(PELT) method (Rebecca Killick, Fearnhead, and Eckley, 2012), CPOP (Fearnhead, Maidstone, and
Letchford, 2019), and RFPOP (Fearnhead and Rigaill, 2019). Nonparametric maximum-likelihood
formulations of the multiple change problem typically select the number of breaks by minimizing
a penalty function (Zou et al., 2014). A second prominent family builds on binary segmentation,
a ‘greedy’ strategy that ignores the global configuration at each split; refinements include circular
binary segmentation (Olshen et al., 2004), wild binary segmentation (Fryzlewicz, 2014), sparsified
binary segmentation (Cho and Fryzlewicz, 2015), and seeded binary segmentation (Kov´ acs et al.,
2023), whereas ‘generous’ bottom-up alternatives are reviewed by Truong et al. (2020). Additional
proposals of practical importance include TrendSegment (Maeng and Fryzlewicz, 2024), the change
point procedure via pruned objectives (W. Zhang et al., 2017), and the narrowest-over-threshold
method (Rafal Baranowski et al., 2019), which blends nonparametric and parametric elements se-
quentially. The MOSUM procedure of Eichinger and Kirch (2018) detects multiple changes in the
mean by scanning standardized differences between sample means computed over adjacent moving
windows and identifying threshold-exceeding local maxima as change point estimates. Although
the proposedI 1 and DCI also deploy a sliding window, they differ from MOSUM in both design
and performance. Comparison of the proposed and existing methods (Section 3) shows that the
proposals outperform the MOSUM procedure across a range of scenarios. Finally, kernel-based
procedures (Arlot et al., 2019) and supervised learning methods, largely classification-based and
driven by sensor data for activity recognition, are surveyed by Aminikhanghahi and Cook (2017).
Common to nearly all of these proposals, however, is the implicit assumption that successive change
points are separated by segments long enough to permit reliable estimation on either side.
Three considerations distinguish our contribution from this literature. First, methods for multi-

ple change points typically privilege a global view of the series; this is necessary for comprehensive
detection, but might compromise localized insight when breaks arrive in quick succession due to
the near-insufficient number of observations between successive change points, inflating type-II er-
ror. Second, the computational burden of existing procedures grows rapidly with the number of
candidate breaks and the forms of change, limiting scalability. Third, breaks near the extremities
of the sample remain notoriously difficult to detect. We address all three issues by proposing a
new test statistic,I 1, whose asymptotic properties and limiting null distribution we establish, and a
two-phase algorithm, the Detector of Change-points usingI 1 (DCI), which reconciles a global scan
with multiple local tests. A new design feature, an adaptive-width sliding window, enablesI 1 and
DCI to retain high true detection rates under the afore-stated irregular configurations.
The remainder of the article is organized as follows. Section 2 states the model and assumptions,
introducesI 1, derives its asymptotic properties and limiting null distribution, and presents the DCI
algorithm for closely-spaced multiple change points detection. Section 3 reports simulation studies
forI 1 and DCI separately, spanning independent and identically (Normally) distributed (i.i.d.)
and autoregressive processes, zero and non-zero slopes, and distant as well as rapidly occurring
change points; across these settings the proposals are Pareto-optimal with respect to true positive
rate, false positive rate, mean absolute error (MAE), and the (unweighted) segmentation covering
metric. Section 4 provides an empirical application assessing the impact of the US-Iran conflict on
oil and gas markets, illustrating tokenization (dimensionality reduction) of financial time series for
further predictive analytics. Section 5 concludes with a discussion on future studies that can extend
I1 for detecting structural breaks in higher moments of temporal processes.
2 Test Statistic and Asymptotic Properties
Consider a temporally ordered sequence of observationsy 1t,t= 1,2, . . . , T, which may be either
i.i.d. or weakly dependent, and lety t = (y 1t −¯y1)/σy1 denote its standardized version; where
¯y1 andσ 2y1 are the mean and variance ofy 1t (refer Appendix B.1 for robust variance estimation
in the presence of change points). Our objective is to construct a statistic that is sensitive to
local structural changes in the mean ofy t (and, consequently, of anyy 1t with unknown mean and
variance) while remaining analytically tractable under the no-change null hypothesis and consistent
asymptotically. The construction proceeds in two stages. We first form a local future-minus-past
contrastd t; these local contrasts are then aggregated symmetrically to yield a transformed sequence
zt. The test statisticI 1 is defined as the maximum absolute value of this transformed sequence.

3 Simulations
In this section, we conduct simulation studies to investigate the finite sample performance of the
proposed methods under the null and alternative hypotheses. We first evaluate the test statisticI 1
in the single change point setting against a broad collection of established methods (Section 3.1),
and then assess the DCI algorithm in the multiple change point regime for which it is designed
(Section 3.2). Throughout, performance is judged over a multi-objective criteria space rather than
by any single summary measure.
3.1 Performance Evaluation ofI 1
To evaluate the performance ofI 1 in detecting a level change and to compare it with existing
change-point detection tests, we conduct a comprehensive simulation exercise. Each simulated
series contains at most one level-change point, with both the location and the magnitude of the
change varying across replications. The noise behavior spans i.i.d. and weakly dependent settings,
with autocorrelation (ρ) values uniformly distributed across the positive and negative ranges. The
magnitude of the level change is uniformly distributed over δσ ∈ {0,0.5,1,2,3,4,5,6}, and the
number of observations available after the change point is uniformly distributed overT−η∈
{1,2,3, . . . ,22,50}. The experiment is conducted withα= 0.1 andT= 100. By crossing weak
and pronounced shifts with post-change samples ranging from a single observation to a comfortable
margin, under both independent and serially dependent noise, the design spans the spectrum from
favorable to near-degenerate detection conditions and thus provides a robust basis for assessing and
comparing change point detection tests.
The evaluation criteria are as follows. The true positive rate (TPR) quantifies aggregate detec-
tion performance across theδandηranges; when computed for an individual (δ, η) pair, it coincides
with the test’s power. The false positive rate (FPR) is the percentage ofH 0 instances, that is, series
without an embedded change point, in which the test rejectsH 0. The mean absolute error (MAE)
is the mean absolute temporal distance betweenηand ˆη, where in type-II error situations ˆηis set
tot= 0.

5 Conclusion
In this article, we develop a new test statistic,I 1, for detecting a level change in time series,
establishing its asymptotic properties and limiting null distribution under i.i.d. Gaussian and weakly
dependent settings, and proving consistency under a mean-shift alternative. Building onI 1, we
propose DCI, a two-phase algorithm for the closely-spaced multiple change points regime that
reconciles a global scan with local tests through an adaptive-width sliding window. Simulations
show thatI 1 and DCI remain Pareto-optimal over the TPR-FPR-MAE (Section 3.1) and TPR-
FPT-MAE-USCM (Section 3.2) criteria spaces, respectively, with pronounced advantages when
breaks occur near sample extremities or in rapid succession. An application to the US-Iran 2026
conflict shows that the financial instruments cluster into a coherent pattern of exposure across
energy markets, and that the ES and EA features devised in Section 4 constitute an information-
rich, reduced-dimensional set of variables for time series analysis. Future research will proceed in
